# The Registry

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The registry was in Mitaka, in a government archive annex that smells of paper and floor
wax and the particular cold of a building that is empty at four in the afternoon.

Hoshino had come to read a family register — a koseki — and she had come because a dead
woman's tags named a town and a year, and because Hoshino was a genealogist, and because
the estate had, in the trade, become the kind of case you take when you have stopped being
able to sleep.

The Kanzaki koseki was a single sheet of certified paper, and it held the whole family, in
the way these records do, in a set of columns that describe births and deaths and adoptions
and, sometimes, in the space where a cause of death is written, a silence.

Kanzaki Sada, mother of Mitsuko, died in 1971, cause listed as illness. Kanzaki Mitsuko,
born 1947, died 2023. And between them, in the household, a third name Hoshino had not
expected: a man, Kuroda Genji, listed not as family but as a temporary resident of the
household, for the year 1958, with a date of removal that matched the date he was found in
the pond at Inokashira.

names, dates, and places; one line is magnified in the composition, showing a temporary
resident entry for 1958 and a removal date.]

---

Hoshino sat with that line for a long time.

A koseki is not a diary. It records status, not events. A man does not appear in a household
register as a temporary resident unless someone formally took him in, which means that at
some point in 1958, Kuroda Genji was registered at the Kanzaki house. He was not a guest.
He was a resident. He lived there, or was recorded as living there, for long enough to be
entered and then removed.

And the date of removal matched the date of his death.

Hoshino had done genealogies for twenty-two years and she had seen this pattern exactly
twice: a resident entered, a resident removed, the removal dated to a death, and the file
otherwise silent. Both other times, the silence had been a cover-up, and both other times,
the family had later quietly sold the house and moved.

The Kanzakis had not moved. Mitsuko had stayed in that house for sixty-five more years, in
the room where the man had died, and had bought back the room's contents one by one, and had
died there in March.

That is not grief. Grief leaves. That is a woman keeping watch over a document she could not
change.

[IMAGE 13.2 — a hand-drawn genealogy of the Kanzaki and Kuroda households in pencil: two
family trees joined by a single dotted line marked 1958; beside the Kuroda side, a small
tombstone symbol and the word INOKASHIRA.]

---

Hoshino requested the Kuroda registry next, and that was where the account turned.

The Kuroda family koseki was a single sheet, and it listed, in the space for the head of
household, Kuroda Genji, and then, in the column for his death, a cause and a place:
drowning, Inokashira, 1958. And then, in the space reserved for a note, a single line that
Hoshino had never seen in any register in twenty-two years.

The line said, in the formal language of the register: *the family declines to state.*

Four characters. The family had been asked, at the time of the death, for a statement, and
had declined, and the archivist had written the refusal into the record, where it had sat
for sixty-five years.

Hoshino photographed it. It was the first documentary proof she had that the Kurodas had
known, at the time, that the death was not an accident, and that the family had chosen — in
writing, in a government register — to say nothing.

She sat back and looked at the two sheets, side by side, the Kanzaki register and the Kuroda
register, and she understood the shape of the whole thing, which was not a murder and not a
cover-up alone. It was a negotiation. The Kurodas had lost a man, and had known he had done
something, and had declined to state. The Kanzakis had lost nothing official, and had
absorbed the man's death into their household record and into their house, and had stayed,
and had begun to collect.

Two families, each holding one half of the night, each unable to speak it, and between them
a room, and in the room, gradually, over sixty years, every object the night had touched.

Hoshino called Aoi.

---

"There was a resident," she said. "Kuroda Genji was registered at the Kanzaki house in 1958.
It's the only reason the Kanzakis were never charged. The death happened at home, in his
registered residence, and the family that would have complained declined to state."

Aoi wrote it down. "So both families signed the lie."

"Not a lie," Hoshino said. "A silence. There's a difference. The Kurodas declined to state
because stating it would name what he had done. The Kanzakis absorbed the death into their
house because stating it would name what their daughter had done. Two silences that fit
together. That's why the room survived."

"And Mitsuko?"

Hoshino looked at the third document on the table, which she had not yet described to Aoi:
a small handwritten note, filed in the archive with the Kanzaki koseki, dated 1961, and
signed by an official.

"Your grandmother," Hoshino said. "Hana. She filed a household change in 1961. She requested
a transfer out of the Kanzaki household. The reason given is one word."

"What word."

"Employment."

Aoi was quiet.

"The same year the first tag is dated," Hoshino said. "1961. Your grandmother left the house
the year Mitsuko started collecting. She was paid — we know that from the mirror — and she
left, and she took the mirror, which was the weapon, and she never spoke, and her daughter
never spoke, and you grew up in Tateishi not knowing any of it. That's the last silence, and
it's the one that kept you alive until April."

[IMAGE 13.3 — two certified documents side by side on the archive table: the Kanzaki
register and the Kuroda register, each with a single line circled; between them, a small
handwritten filing note dated 1961 with the word EMPLOYMENT underlined.]

---

The archivist came at five to say the building was closing, and Hoshino gathered up her
photographs and her notebook and walked out into the cold of Mitaka station, and stood on
the platform, and understood that she had just read the whole crime and could prove none of
it, because both families had decline-to-state stamped into the paper, and a silence is not
a confession, and a registry is not a witness.

She went home. She put the photographs in a folder and the folder in a drawer, and she wrote
one line in her notebook, which is the line that was found there afterward:

*the register says everything except the one thing, and the one thing is the only thing that
matters, and that is why she collected objects instead of documents. you cannot decline to
state an object. you can only hold it.*

And then she went to bed, and in the morning the folder was on the kitchen table instead of
in the drawer, and the photographs were face down, and the first one had a small tag taped
to it, in a hand she had spent three days learning to read:

*HOSHINO — 2023 — MITAKA.*

She had been filed. She was a record now, like Mikami, like Kubo, like the blades — a person
who knew a piece of the truth and could be relied upon to keep it. She put the tag in her
pocket and did not call the police, because she did not yet have a crime, only a silence,
and because she had begun to understand that the room preferred its witnesses silent, and
that being preferred was very close to being chosen.

[IMAGE 13.4 — a kitchen table in the morning: a folder open, photographs face down in a
row; on the first photograph, a small handwritten tag taped in place, reading HOSHINO, 2023,
MITAKA; a cup of tea gone cold.]


---

Hoshino did not go to the police, and she has never fully explained why, and the notebook she
left explains it better than she could.

She wrote: *a register is a promise the state makes to itself. If the state declines to state,
the promise is void, and a void promise is not nothing. It is a permission. Whoever held the
pen at the moment of the refusal holds the room. That is what I found. That is why I could not
file it.*

She also wrote a list, and the list is the thing that makes her a witness rather than a
reader. It was a list of the *other* rooms — the two other genealogies she had done in
twenty-two years where a resident was entered and removed and the file went silent. She had
not thought about them in years. Now she went back to both.

Both rooms had been in shitamachi districts. Both deaths had been ruled accidents. Both
families had, within a decade, transferred a large household object — a chest, in one case; a
cabinet, in the other — to a domestic servant, and the servant had left the household, and the
household had stayed.

Hoshino had found the pattern and had been terrified by it, because a pattern that repeats
across three families is not a room. It is a *practice*. Somewhere in the shitamachi, at some
point, an arrangement had been made: a household object takes the blame, a servant carries it
out, a silence is paid, and the family stays in the house and keeps the room.

And Mitsuko, at eleven, had grown up inside the practice, and had done the only thing a child
could do with a practice no one would name: she had made it *into objects*, and had spent her
life getting the objects back, so that the practice would at last have a shape someone could
read.

Hoshino wrote: *she did not invent the room. The room is old. She was the first person in sixty
years willing to keep the receipt.*

[IMAGE 16.5 — a notebook page in a careful hand: two columns listing three "silent" cases,
with the household object and the servant's departure noted; below, a single underlined
sentence; the notebook lies open on an archive table.]


---

Hoshino's last act was to file a correction, and the correction is the only official document in
the whole account that tells the truth.

She went back to Mitaka in November with the folder and with Aoi and with a request, on a form,
to amend two family registers, and the request was refused, as she knew it would be, because
you cannot amend the dead, and because a decline-to-state is permanent, and because the state
does not reopen what it has closed.

So she did the only other thing a registrar can do. She wrote a *note* and filed it with the
Kuroda register, as a note from a researcher, which is permitted, and the note is a page long,
and it says, in the formal language of the archive, everything the register declined to state.

Aoi read the note on the train home. It is the document she has kept, and I have kept it too,
and it is the last thing I will quote before the end.

*Note, filed by researcher H., Mitaka annex, November. The family register of the Kuroda
household, 1958, records the death of Kuroda Genji at Inokashira, and records that the family
declines to state. This note is filed to place on record, for any future reader, that the
decline was made after the family had been informed, by the Kanzaki household, of the
circumstances of the death, and that the circumstances were: an assault upon a minor within
the Kanzaki residence; the intervention of the minor's mother; and the death of Kuroda Genji by
misadventure in the course of that intervention. The Kanzaki register records a resident removed
on the same date, and a child of eleven in the household, and the child's mother later
confessed to the death and was never charged. This researcher makes no finding of law. This
researcher records only that both registers declined, and that a register that declines is still
a document, and that a document is still a promise, and that the promise was to the public, and
that the public has not been told.*

That is the whole of it, in the language of the archive: the two families declined, and the
public was never told, and a child grew up inside the refusal and spent sixty years buying it
back so that someone would read it.

Aoi folded the note into the folder and looked out the window at the lights of Mitaka and
understood that she had, at last, the one thing the account had been missing since April: a
document, in an official file, in a government archive, that a stranger could read, that did
not rely on a ghost or a curse or a room, and that said, in plain bureaucratic Japanese, that a
man had attacked a child and the child's mother had stopped him and the world had agreed not to
say so.

It was not justice. It was a note in a file.

But it was *readable*, and Kanzaki Mitsuko had spent her life collecting objects because objects
are readable, and Aoi understood, on the train, that the note was the last piece of the
collection, and that it belonged in the room.
