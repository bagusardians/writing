# The Doll

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The doll had a tag and the tag had a child's name and the child was alive.

That was the thing the police could never have explained and the thing that made Kubo open
the envelope eleven times without opening it. He ran a small repair shop for dolls in
Asakusa, which is a trade you enter by being unable to throw anything away, and he had
restored the doll in April without reading its tag, because the tag was not why he was
there.

He read it in June.

[IMAGE 11.1 — a bisque-headed doll on a bench, traditional Japanese costume, one hand
repaired; a small tag tied at the wrist in the same small upright hand as the other lots.
The doll's face is turned away from the camera.]

*HANA — 1958 — YANAKA.*

---

Kubo was sixty-eight and he had repaired dolls for forty-one years, and he could tell you
that a doll is not a toy. A doll is a stand-in. A child gives a doll a name and a history
and a set of injuries, and the child keeps the doll, and the doll keeps the child's
childhood, and when the child grows up and cannot bear it, the doll comes to Kubo, and he
mends it, and it goes back into a box, and the box goes into a cupboard, and that is the
whole trade: mending the stand-in so the original can rest.

He had four hundred dolls in his shop, in boxes, named on the boxes, waiting.

The Hana doll was a girl's doll, Taishō, bisque head, silk costume faded from crimson to
the colour of dried blood. Its right hand had been repaired once, badly, long ago. And in
its sash, sewn into the lining where a child would hide something, Kubo found a folded
strip of paper.

He almost did not open it. He had a rule about hidden things: a hidden thing belongs to
whoever hid it, and a mender is not a finder. But the doll had come from the Kanzaki estate,
and by June the estate had a reputation in the trade, and Kubo had begun to have a bad
feeling about the whole month of April.

He opened it.

[IMAGE 11.2 — the unfolded strip of paper: a child's handwriting, a numbered list of names,
the last one set below a ruled line and written in a steadier hand, reading SHIRAI; the
paper is water-stained and sewn-edge frayed.]

On it, in a child's hand, a list of names. The names on the tags. All twelve of them, in the
order they appear in the room, with a thirteenth written apart, below a line: *the one who
came after.*

The thirteenth was a child's name. Kubo looked it up because he could not stop himself,
which is the whole disease of his trade and hers.

The child was nine years old and lived in Katsushika, in Tateishi, and her family name was
Shirai.

---

Kubo called Aoi because she had left her card at the estate, and because he had met her
once, in April, when they both stood in the Kiyosumi-Shirakawa hall and read the same
table of tags and did not speak.

"Shirai," he said. "That's your name."

"Yes."

"There's a child. Nine. Tateishi."

Aoi was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "That's where I lived when I was nine. It's
where my mother died."

Kubo did not understand. He has never understood. He only knows that he put the doll in a
box and the box on a shelf and that in the morning the box was open, and the doll was
facing the door, and the tag had been rewound to show the blank side.

He called Aoi again, that morning, and told her, and she asked him to read the list once
more, slowly, and he did, and she stopped him at the fourth name.

"Say that one again."

"Tachibana," he said.

"Tachibana is my mother's maiden name," Aoi said. "My mother was a Tachibana. The fourth
name on the room list is my mother's family."

The room in Yanaka, 1958, had twelve people in it. Five of them, by the end of sixty years,
had descendants who lived within a few kilometres of each other and did not know it: a
buyer in Tateishi, a mender in Sendagi, a doll repairer in Asakusa, a locksmith in Yanaka, a
bank clerk in Asakusa. The room had not just been rebuilt. It had been seeded.

"Somebody put us here," Aoi said.

"Somebody kept us here," Kubo said. "There's a difference. You can't put a person
somewhere and make them stay thirty years. But you can choose who to buy from, and who to
mend for, and whose child to give your card to. She was a customer of everyone in this
city. She had sixty years to pick."

[IMAGE 11.3 — a shelf in a repair shop at dawn: an open box, a doll inside it now facing
outward toward the door; the tag on its wrist has been turned blank-side-up; a strip of
paper lies on the shelf below.]

---

Kubo went to lock the shop and found the key already turned.

He stood in his own doorway with the key in his hand and understood that he had locked it
the night before, and that the lock was a good lock, and that the doll had moved, and that
he was standing in a room full of four hundred stand-ins each of which held a childhood,
and that one of the childhoods had stood up and walked.

That is the phrase he used, afterward, when he tried to explain it, and he never got
further than that phrase: *one of the childhoods stood up and walked.*

He did not sleep. He sat with the light on and the door in view and the doll in the open box
facing him, and at some point in the small hours he understood that the exercise of a
lifetime — mending the stand-in so the original can rest — had been performed on him. He
was the stand-in. He had been mended, and filed, and left in a shop in Asakusa to keep a
piece of the room safe, and the room had come to collect.

In the morning there was a stamp on his counter, and an envelope, addressed in a hand he
recognised from the tag.

[IMAGE 11.4 — a photograph of a Tateishi drinking-alley (yokochō) at night, narrow, lantern
lit; in the far frame, a child-sized figure seen only from behind, walking away; the
photographer's presence is implied by a shadow entering the bottom of the frame.]

The envelope held a single photograph, of the Tateishi alley, and on the back, in pencil,
small and upright: *thank you for keeping her. she is the last of the set. when you are
ready, the room is in Kuramae, and it has a place for everyone who held a piece.*

Kubo put the photograph down and looked at the doll, and the doll looked at the door, and he
understood that he had been invited, and that the invitation was also the count, and that
somewhere a woman who had been dead since March had known, to the week, when he would open
the envelope.


---

Aoi asked Kubo the question she had been afraid to ask, and it was this: whether the doll had
ever belonged to a child who was alive.

Kubo said no. He said a doll that passes to a repairer is a doll that has been *retired*, and
a doll is retired when the child grows up, and a child who owned this doll would be in her
eighties now. But the doll's costume, and the doll's hand, and the way the silk had been
mended, told him something else: that the doll had not been retired. It had been *kept*.

"Kept how," Aoi asked.

"Kept the way you keep a thing you're going to give to someone," Kubo said. "The hand was
mended badly, long ago, by someone who didn't know how, and the costume was taken in at the
waist, and the tag was tied with cotton thread, not the shop's cord. Whoever kept this doll
kept it *ready*. They were holding it for a child."

Aoi sat with that. "For the thirteenth child. The one below the line."

"I think so," Kubo said. "I think the doll was the room's gift. A doll is what you give a
child to hold so she doesn't have to hold what she saw. And I think the old woman kept it for
sixty years because she never got to give it."

He turned the doll over. There was one more thing, and he had saved it, because he said he
had wanted Aoi to hear the rest first, so that she would understand what it meant.

Inside the doll's other sleeve, sewn into the lining, there was a second slip of paper, in
the small upright hand, older than the first, and on it: *if a child ever comes for you,
give her this. Tell her she can keep it. Tell her I'm sorry about the room.*

[IMAGE 11.5 — the interior of a doll's sleeve, held open: a slip of paper sewn into the
lining in an old hand; the doll lies on a bench beside a needle and thread; the tag at the
wrist is visible.]


---

Aoi found Kubo's box the next month, because Kubo had called her and because he wanted her to
have it, and the box held the doll and the doll's slips and Kubo's own account of the four
hundred stand-ins.

She went to Asakusa on a wet Sunday and sat in the repair shop among the boxes and asked Kubo
the question that had been forming since the temple.

She asked him whether any of the four hundred dolls had ever come back.

Kubo did not understand, at first. She asked again: whether a doll he had mended had ever come
back to the shop a second time, months or years later, changed. He said no, dolls do not walk,
and then he looked at the Hana doll in its open box and said, slowly, that there was one case.

In 1993, a doll had come back. A woman had brought in a doll he had mended in 1988, and she had
not wanted it mended; she had wanted it *inspected*, and she had asked him to look inside the
sash, and he had looked, and there had been nothing. And she had seemed, he said, disappointed
and relieved at once, and had taken the doll away, and had asked him to write nothing on the
ticket.

Aoi asked what the woman looked like.

Kubo said: fifties, well-dressed, small upright handwriting. She had paid in cash. And she had
asked him, before she left, one question he had never forgotten.

She had asked: "Is there any way to make a thing that has been read *unread*?"

Kubo had told her no. You cannot unread a thing. You can only read it differently, or keep it
from someone else.

And the woman had nodded, and had said, "Then I will keep it from someone else," and had left,
and Kubo had thought about it for years, and had never understood — until the Hana doll came
into his shop this spring, with the child's list sewn into its sash, and he had understood that
the woman in 1993 had been looking for the other slip of paper, the second one, the one he had
found in the *other* sleeve, and had not found it, and had gone away believing the doll was
clean.

Which meant the doll had been carrying the truth since 1958, and someone had been searching for
it for thirty-five years, and it had come to Kubo, and he had found it, and he had read it, and
the reading had put him in the room.

Aoi asked Kubo one last thing: whether the woman had left a name.

Kubo said no. But he said she had left a *district*, because he had asked where to send a
receipt, and she had said Sendagi, and had given an address three streets from Mikami's
workshop, and had asked him not to write it down.
