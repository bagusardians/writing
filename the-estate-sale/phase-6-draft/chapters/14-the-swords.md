# The Swords

*Close third, past tense.*

---

Ito had appraised sword fittings for thirty years and he had never once been afraid of a
blade. That is what he told the police, and it was true, and it did not help him.

He dealt in tsuba and menuki and kozuka, the small metal fittings of the samurai
sword, out of a shop in Nezu behind the shrine, and he dealt mostly with the fittings and
rarely with the blades, because blades carry a different kind of history and a different
kind of buyer. The Kanzaki estate sent him a lot in April: a daishō stand with two blades,
both in shirasaya, both unsigned, both with a small tag tied to the stand.

Tag: *KURODA — 1958 — YANAKA*.

[IMAGE 12.1 — a daishō stand on a workshop table, two swords in plain wooden shirasaya
resting on the hooks; a small handwritten tag tied to the stand reading KURODA, 1958,
YANAKA; a loupe lies beside it.]

---

The blades were not Kanzaki blades. That was the first thing. The Kanzaki family had no
sword tradition; the stand was the only martial object in the estate. And the blades were
fine — a matched pair, Shintō period, made for a man of some standing, and cut down at some
point to a shorter length, which is what you do when a blade is no longer for war and
becomes, instead, a thing a man keeps in a room to remind himself what he is.

Ito examined the nakago, the tang, for the file marks, and found them, and found also a
second set of marks that did not belong: the marks of a polisher's stone, recent, within the
lifetime of the blade's ownership. Someone had had the blades polished, and not long ago,
and the polish had been done well.

A man does not polish the blades of a matched pair and then sell them in an estate sale. A
man polishes them to keep them. And then a woman buys back the stand they sit on, which
means the stand was let go by the man and reclaimed by the woman, and the blades went with
the stand, and the blades had been polished by the last hand before hers.

Ito called the estate, and got Kei, and asked who had polished the blades.

"Polished?" Kei said. "They're just old swords. My mother never touched them."

"Someone did," Ito said. "Within fifteen years, and well."

Kei was quiet. Then he said, "There was a man. He came to the house once, maybe fifteen
years ago. I wasn't allowed in the room. I heard him crying."

"Crying."

"I was sixteen. I thought it was strange. I asked my mother afterward who he was, and she
said a customer, and I said customers don't cry, and she said, 'This one does.'"

[IMAGE 12.2 — a close photograph of a sword tang (nakago) with file marks, lit by a
loupe lamp; a faint second set of stone marks crosses the first at an angle; a small tag is
tied to the tang.]

---

Ito did the thing that a dealer does, which is to look at the fittings, because the fittings
tell you what the blade was for.

He took the tsuba off the stand. It was iron, dark, a good Edo piece, and on its reverse,
where a hand would rest, there was a small incised mark, worn but legible: a plum blossom
and a character. He knew the character. It was the crest of a family he had appraised for,
years ago, and the family name was Kuroda.

So the stand was Kuroda's, and the blades were Kuroda's, and the estate's tag named a
Kuroda, and the woman who had spent sixty years assembling a room had put a family's
weapons in it, which is not the act of a collector. It is the act of a witness, setting the
table for a confession.

Ito called Kei back and asked, directly: "Did your mother ever tell you what happened in that
room in 1958?"

Kei said no.

"Did anyone else."

Kei said nothing for a long moment. Then he said, in a voice that had gone somewhere else:
"An old man came once, when I was a boy. He stood at the door of the room and wouldn't go
in. He said, 'Tell her it was the swords that did the talking.' I never knew what he meant.
She cried when I told her. It's the only time I saw her cry."

The swords that did the talking.

Ito looked at the two blades in their plain scabbards and understood, the way you understand
a valuation before you can name it, that these were not weapons of the night. They were the
Kuroda family's formal pair, the pair a patron wears when he goes to a house to be
important and to be seen. Kuroda Genji had gone to the Kanzaki house in 1958 carrying the
family's pride, and had done something unspeakable while wearing it, and someone — not the
Kanzakis, who had no swords — had polished the blades afterward, and had put them back on
the stand, and had kept them, and had cried, and had brought them back to the woman's room
fifteen years ago because the room had a place for the blades and he could not finish
keeping them alone.

[IMAGE 12.3 — the tsuba's reverse: an iron guard photographed close, showing a worn plum
blossom and a family character incised at the edge; the light catches the worn metal.]

---

Ito made a mistake. He will say this himself, and he said it to the police, and it is the
only part of the account he blames himself for.

He called the Kuroda family to offer the blades. It was professional. When a blade with a
family crest comes into the trade, you notify the family; it is a courtesy and a small
reward and no one thinks twice. He left a message with the family's office in Nezu.

The next day, a man came to the shop.

He was perhaps sixty, well-dressed, polite, with his hands folded, and he introduced himself
as Kuroda Jun, the grandson, and he asked to see the blades. Ito, who was a dealer and
pleased to have found the rightful owner, brought them out.

Kuroda Jun did not look at the blades. He looked at the tag on the stand, and at the tsuba,
and at the nakago where the stone marks crossed the file marks, and he set the sword down
and said, "You looked at the tang."

"I appraise," Ito said.

"Yes," said Kuroda Jun. "That's the problem."

Ito did not understand, at first, and then he did, and he understood too late, the way you
understand a jewel is paste the moment after you have said the price out loud. The blades
were the only physical proof that a Kuroda had gone to the house wearing his family's formal
pair. The blades could not say what had happened. But they could say he was there, and the
stone marks could say someone had tried, afterward, to make the pair fit to be kept, which
is what a family does after a member has done something that the family cannot bear and
cannot bury.

"You've been very helpful," said Kuroda Jun, and reached across the table, and put his hand
flat on the sword, and did not let Ito lift it.

[IMAGE 12.4 — a table from above: two hands, one old and one gloved, both resting on the
same sword in shirasaya; the tag on the stand between them; the frame is otherwise empty and
the room behind is dark.]

Ito's account ends there. The account that follows is not his; it is assembled from the
shop's ledger, which was found open on the table, and from the single line Ito had written
in it that morning, before the man arrived:

*KURODA blades — family notified — do not sell to family. K. knows what they did.*

He wrote that line and did not act on it, because he did not yet believe it, and by the
afternoon it was true, and he was not there to say so.


---

Aoi went to the Nezu shop two days after Ito was found, because she had by then trained
herself to arrive at a death within forty-eight hours, and because she wanted to read the
ledger that Ito had left open.

The ledger was gone. The police had taken it. But the shop's shutters were up and there was a
note on the door in a hand Aoi did not recognise, saying the shop was closed pending
inventory, and under the door there was a small tag, and the tag had no name on it, and it had
a date, and the date was today.

She put it in her pocket and did not read it until she was home.

Then she went to the storage room behind the shop, because the door was unlocked and because
she had become the kind of person who tries doors, and inside there were the blades, in
shirasaya, on the stand, and the stand was the one from the estate, and the two blades were
on it, and on the stand there was a tag, and the tag said KURODA — 1958 — YANAKA, and it was
the original tag, which the estate had sold with the stand, which meant the blades had been
brought back.

Which meant Kuroda Jun had already been here.

Which meant Kuroda Jun had stood in this room and put the blades back on the stand that the
dead woman had tagged, and had left them, and had left the shop open, and had left a tag under
the door with no name and today's date.

Aoi understood, standing in the storage room with the blades, that Kuroda Jun was not tidying.
He was *returning*. He had been returning the pieces to the room for years — the coat, the
stand, the blades — and the deaths were not the point of the returns. The returns were the
point of the returns. He was putting the room back together the only way a man who believes he
is the victim's heir can: by owning it.

And then Aoi understood the thing she would not understand fully until Kuramae: that Kuroda
Jun and the collector had the same project, from opposite ends, and that the tags were the
hinge, and that a third party — the one who had been clearing the witnesses — was walking the
same road with neither of them, and that whoever stood in the gap would settle it.

[IMAGE 14.5 — a storage room behind a Nezu shop: a daishō stand with two blades in shirasaya,
the original tag tied on; a dealer's ledger gone from the table, the dust showing its
rectangle; the shop's back door open to a narrow lane.]


---

The blades came back the week before the room closed, and Aoi was the one who received them,
because there was no one else left, and because a courier does not ask why a shirasaya is being
sent to an appraiser.

The parcel was addressed to "the last reader" in the steady hand, c/o Aoi's office, and it had
no return address, and inside, wrapped in cloth, were the two blades on the daishō stand, with
the original tag, and a letter.

The letter is the document Aoi read most often in December, and it is the document that made
her certain the ones who did the tidying were not monsters in the way a reader wants a monster
to be.

It read:

*You have the folder. You will have worked out that she kept more than one of us. Hers is the
testimony. Mine is the record-keeping: the tickets, the registers, the files that let a death be
closed as an accident. I am not sorry for it, but I am going to tell you why, because you are
the only person who has read the whole of it and a reader is owed a reason.*

*In 1958 my mother carried a mirror out of that house and was paid to be silent for thirty
years. She never told me; I found it in a register, the way I find everything, in the year she
died, when I was fourteen, and I understood two things. First, that a silence can be paid for
and passed down. Second, that the only cure for a paid silence is an unpaid truth.*

*Kanzaki Mitsuko spent her life buying the truth back, object by object, and she could not
speak it, because she was eleven when it happened and because her mother had signed it away in
a register and because a woman in a house in Yanaka cannot accuse a Kuroda. She could only
build. So she built, for sixty years, and then in 1989 she wrote to two families whose
elder had carried her night and been paid to forget it — the driver's line and mine — and she
asked us to carry it, and we did, because I was my mother's shame and she was my family's, and
between us we could make one true thing.*

*The deaths were not her design. They were ours, and I want that exact — mine among them. She
wrote the list and we walked it; the living she left to the other hand, the records to me. She
never asked either of us to kill anyone. She asked us to clear the route, and I interpreted
"clear" the way a family with a bought silence interprets a word, and I was wrong, and it is too
late, and I am putting it in writing because a reader is owed a reason and because a confession
is the only object I have left that she did not tag.*

*The blades belong to the room. The room is nearly closed. If you are reading this, you are
inside it, and I am sorry, and I am not coming.*
