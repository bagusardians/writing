# The Tea Set

*Live POV. Present tense. A narrator killing.*

---

I set the cups out while the water heats, because it matters that the cups are the right
way round, and the right way round is the side with the tag facing the wall.

Five cups. One for him, one for me, three for the people he buried, because I am not
superstitious, I am thorough, and a man should drink with the room he emptied.

The tea set came from the sale in Kiyosumi-Shirakawa in April, one of two hundred lots
tagged by a dead woman in a hand I have now learned to read. Mine said *TOYAMA — 1958 —
YANAKA*. Toyama was my grandfather. He was in the room that night. He was not the man on
the floor and he was not the woman in the doorway. He was the fourth person, the one
nobody counts, the one who was paid to keep the other three quiet, and he did keep them
quiet, for sixty years, until the quiet killed him.

[IMAGE 10.1 — a kitchen counter, close: a five-piece tea set arranged in a row, each cup
with a small handwritten tag tied to its handle, tags turned to face away from the camera.
Steam is just beginning to rise.]

---

I will tell the reader who I am, because the cup facing the wall has my grandfather's name
and the cup facing the room has mine, and I want to be clear about which is which.

I am Toyama Reiko. I am sixty-two. I work in a bank in Asakusa and I have never hurt
anyone, and this afternoon I am going to kill a man, and I have been preparing it the way
you prepare a transfer: in order, in silence, with everything reconciled.

My grandfather was Toyama Kenji. He was a driver for Kuroda Genji, who was a patron in
Yanaka, and who was a bad man in a way that no one in the family would name. My grandfather
told me one thing, once, when I was fourteen and he was dying, and he told it the way you
hand someone a live coal: quickly, with both hands, and then you let go.

He said: "In 1958 there was a night at the Kanzaki house. I drove Kuroda there. I drove
home alone. I was paid to say I was not there, and I have said it for twenty years, and I
am telling you now because I do not want to die with only his version."

He did not say what happened. He did not have to. A man who is paid to say he was not there
is a man who was there, and a girl of eleven who hides behind a chest is a girl who saw
something, and a patron who leaves a house at night and does not come out of the pond at
Inokashira until spring is a patron the world decided not to look at.

And my grandfather, who drove, and said nothing, and took the money, was the reason no one
ever looked.

The water is ready.

[IMAGE 10.2 — a hand tipping a measured spoonful of white powder into one cup, photographed
from above; the powder dissolves; four other cups are already filled; a tag hangs visible
on the poisoned cup's handle, reading TOYAMA, 1958, YANAKA.]

---

I have known the man I am going to kill since I was a girl.

His name is Kuroda Jun. He is the grandson. He came to my grandfather's house at New Year
and he brought the kind of sweets you bring when you want to be remembered, and he was kind
to me, and he was the reason my grandfather never slept, and when my grandfather died he
came to the funeral and stood at the back with his hands folded, and I watched him fold his
hands, and I understood, at fourteen, what hands are for.

He called me three weeks ago. That is what began it. He called me, after thirty years, and
he said he was gathering the pieces of his grandfather's story, and he asked whether my
family still had anything from 1958, and I said no, and he said, "That's a shame. Your
grandfather's tag came up in the sale. I'm buying it back. I thought you'd want to know."

He said it kindly. He said it the way he has said everything, for thirty years, kindly.

And I understood that he had been doing this the whole time — buying the pieces back,
quietly, keeping the story his grandfather's version — and that my grandfather had been a
piece, and I had been a piece, and that the kind man at the funeral with his folded hands
had been tidying up the family history the way you tidy a room before guests arrive.

The sale taught me the rest. I bought the tea set because of the tag, and I read the tags
of the whole estate online, all two hundred of them, and I saw the pattern: the names of
the dead, in order, over sixty years, arranged around a room in Yanaka. And I understood
that the dead woman had spent her life undoing exactly what Kuroda Jun was spending his
undoing. She was rebuilding the truth. He was dismantling it. And they had been doing it,
the two of them, for as long as I have been alive.

He arrives on time, which is the one honest thing about him.

---

He is old now and careful and he still folds his hands, and he tells me I have grown, and
I tell him the tea is from a tin my grandfather kept, which is true.

He asks about the cups with the tags. I tell him it is a new thing I do, an inventory, a
habit from my grandfather's trade — Kenji drove, and drove carefully, and kept a log of
every trip, and I have inherited the habit of recording. Kuroda Jun smiles and says the
Kanzaki sale has made everyone an archivist.

He drinks. He says it is very good.

I drink mine, which is clean, because I intend to be here for the rest of it.

He asks me, after the second cup, why the cups have tags. I tell him the truth. I tell him
that everything in this house is tagged now, because a dead woman in Yanaka spent sixty
years teaching the world to read. I tell him his name is not on any of them yet, and that by
tonight, in a manner of speaking, it will be.

He understands before the third cup. I watch it reach him the way a name reaches you across
a room — he sets the cup down, and then he reaches for it again, and then he stops, and he
looks at the tag, and he reads it, and he says one word, which is my grandfather's name.

"Yes," I say.

[IMAGE 10.3 — the low table seen from the doorway: two men's cups and three untouched cups;
one man seated, upright, both hands flat on the table; the narrator's hands are in the
frame, holding a teapot, steady; the room is warm and ordinary.]

---

He asks me if I am going to watch. I tell him I have been watching him since I was fourteen.

He asks me what I want. I tell him the truth, which is that I want the version. I want him
to say, out loud, in this room, that his grandfather attacked a child in 1958, and that the
family knew, and that they paid to bury it, and that my grandfather took the money and said
nothing, and that every kind New Year sweet for thirty years was the interest on a silence.

He does not say it. He sits with his hands flat on the table and the room goes quiet around
him the way rooms do. He says one thing, which is: "You'll be caught."

I point at the third cup, the one facing the wall, the one I did not fill for him.

"I'm not the only one with a motive," I say. "The whole estate is full of people the room
touched. There were thirty-one buyers in that hall, and four of them are dead, and everyone
who ever held a tagged thing has a reason to want the Kurodas gone. The police will spend a
year on the tags. Every tag points at the room, and the room points at no one, and a room with
no one in it is exactly what a curse is. Nobody looks at a tea set."

It was a good assumption, and it was wrong, and I did not live long enough to learn how wrong.
I did not know there was a folder. I did not know an appraiser had spent nine months training
herself to read a dead woman's hand, or that five deaths closed separately would one day be
laid on one table and made to match. I understood the room better than the man across from me,
and I understood the *police* not at all, and that is the whole of my mistake.

He looks at the five cups and understands, finally, that I have made him one of two hundred
and eleven objects, that his name will go on a blank tag tonight, that the room he spent
thirty years emptying has a space in it shaped exactly like him, and that I am not the
collection.

I am the collector now.

He does not finish the cup. He does not have to. He sits with his hands flat on the table
and the room goes quiet around him the way rooms do, and I pour the last of the tea into
the three cups for the people he buried, and I sit down, and I wait.

[IMAGE 10.4 — the tea set photographed after: four cups emptied, one full; the seated man is
slumped forward, face down; a hand is reaching in from the frame's edge to turn the tag on
his cup face-up, so the name shows — TOYAMA, 1958.]


---

I am going to write down what I knew and when I knew it, the way you write a bank ledger,
because I have spent my life making the numbers reconcile, and I will not stop now.

I knew the tea set's tag was my grandfather's in April, the day I bought it. I knew the estate
had two hundred and eleven lots before the end of April, because the listing was public. I
knew the pattern of the names before the end of May, because I read the tags the way the
killer read them: as a seating chart.

What I did not know until June was that the man I was going to kill had already begun to buy
the pieces back.

I found that out from a dealer. I had gone to a shop in Nezu, to a man who appraised sword
fittings, because a tea caddy from the estate had come to me through a cousin and I wanted it
valued, and the dealer told me, without prompting, that a Kuroda had been in and bought back
a stand with two blades, and had asked after every other tagged lot he could name.

The dealer said: "He's collecting the estate. He's buying it out from under whoever holds it.
He's been doing it for twenty years."

That was the fact that decided it. Not the room. Not the tags. The fact that the man was
buying the proof, one piece at a time, so that when the old woman died and the estate sold,
there would be nothing left to read but his version.

I could not out-collect him. I am a bank clerk. But I could take the one piece he could not
buy back after I was done: the man himself. You cannot buy back a cup you have drunk from.

[IMAGE 10.5 — a tea caddy on a counter in a shop in Nezu: a small ceramic caddy with a cloth
binding and a handwritten tag; a middle-aged woman's hand rests beside it; the shop shelves
behind hold dusty boxes.]


---

I have one more thing to write and then the tea is ready.

I wrote to her in 1989. Let me begin there, because the beginning is the only part of this that
anyone will be able to check.

My name is Toyama Reiko and I am a hand. There is more than one of us; that is a thing I did
not learn until this year, and it is the reason I am writing this down.

That is the thing I have not said to anyone, and I am going to say it here, because this
document will be found and because I am not going to be caught, and because a bank clerk knows
how a ledger is closed: last entry, no error, sign nothing.

My grandfather drove Kuroda Genji to that house in 1958 and was paid to say he was not there,
and I found out in 1989, from a letter, and the letter was from Kanzaki Mitsuko, and the letter
said: *I saw your grandfather that night. He was the only one who looked at me. He knew, and
he said nothing, and I have not blamed him, and I have kept his name for sixty years. Now I
need a hand.*

I wrote back. I was twenty-six. I was a bank clerk and I was good with lists and I had a
grandfather who had never slept. She asked me to do one thing: to follow the list, and to clear
each name when its date came, and to keep the room from being read by anyone who was not meant
to read it. She said the list was the room, and the room was a testimony, and a testimony is
read once and then it is true.

I said yes.

She divided the work the way you divide a ledger. The living names were mine: the ones who
had held a piece in their hands and could still be talked to, and talked out of it. The ones
who kept the *records* were handled by someone else.

A second correspondent. She never said his name, only that he was owed the same thing my family
was owed: a silence paid down. I never met him.

For thirty years I believed I was the only hand. This year I found his signature on the backs of
tags I had never written, in a hand steadier than mine, and I understood that the old woman had
kept us apart the way she kept everything apart — so that no one of us would ever know the whole
of it.

She kept the two lists in one book and never let the columns touch. I have drawn them the way she
ruled them.

[IMAGE 10.6 — a two-column chart drawn on the ruled flyleaf of a bank ledger, in the tea
narrator's hand: the left column headed "the ones who held a piece," names in a firm upright
hand, each struck through; the right column headed "the ones who kept the records," ruled off by
a vertical double line, with a single unreadable signature at the foot; the two columns never
join.]

I want to be exact about the deaths, because the exactness is the only thing I have left that
is mine.

They were not murders in the way the reader is expecting. Every one of them was a
*conversation*, and every one ended in an explanation, and the explanation is the same in
every case. I told them what the room was, what their tag said, what their family had done in
1958. And they died of the telling. Every one.

The doctor wrote heart, or fall, or illness. The doctors were not wrong. You can die of a truth
you have been avoiding for sixty years. I have been present for every one I was sent to, and
each one went quiet at the last, the way a person goes quiet when a sum finally comes out.

It is faster than poison and tidier.

Except the cup. I did use the cup, tonight, for Kuroda Jun, and I want to say why, because it
is the one death that was not clean.

He was not on the list. He was never on the list. He was the man who had been buying the pieces
back, and the old woman had not accounted for him, and he had begun, this year, to read the
room himself. He was going to expose all of it. He was going to take the testimony and use it
to clear his own family — to say, look, my grandfather was attacked, the Kanzakis lied, we are
the victims — which is the one reading of the room that would make it worth a death.

So I made him the thirteenth. I gave him a cup and a tag and a place in the room, and I did it
with the tea set from the estate, because the room should close with its own objects in its own
hands.

The tea is ready.

[IMAGE 10.7 — a two-column list in a steady adult hand on a bank ledger sheet: names, dates,
one a month; nine lines are struck through; the tenth line is a man's name; the sheet lies
beside a tea service.]

*[The following note is in a second steady hand, and was filed with the account. It is not in
the bank clerk's.]*

*One correction, for the reader, because a ledger should close clean. The bank clerk took the
man Kuroda as the room's last word. He was not. He was the room's loudest nuisance and the
Kurodas' last attempt to buy their name back, and she gave him a cup for it, and I let her,
because a hand should not know the whole of the list. The room's last word is a reader, not a
Kuroda, and the reader was not hers to serve and not mine. She died believing she had closed the
account, and that was the old woman's one mercy, and it was mine to keep.*
