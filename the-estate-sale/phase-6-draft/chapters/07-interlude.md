# Interlude — The Slip

---

I am writing this at Mikami's bench in Sendagi because my flat no longer feels safe and
because his shop has a lock I do not have to think about, and because he has gone home and
left me the kettle and the light.

I want to record the hour, because I have started to distrust my own sense of time. It is
just after one. The lane outside is empty. The shop smells of shellac and cold tea, which I
have described before in this account, and which I am describing again, because I find that
I want the ordinary things written down before I lose them.

Here is the thing I have not been able to say in the pages before this one, and it is the
reason I have taken the ledger home with me: I do not think I am going to survive this.

I do not mean that as fear. I mean it as an appraisal.

I have spent eleven years pricing things, and the trade gives you one gift, which is
judgement without attachment. You look at a lacquer box with a cracked lid and you see the
crack, and you see the box, and you do not confuse the two. I have been looking at this
account the way I look at a box. There is a crack in it. The crack is not me. But the crack
runs from the first page to this one, and I have begun to think it was there before I
started writing, and that I have merely been tracing it.

The ledger says my name. The tags say my name. The mirror came back to me. A woman I never
met, dead in March, arranged her whole estate around my mother, and my mother's mirror, and
my mother's death, and she has been dead for six months and she is still arranging.

I keep thinking about the boy at the estate. Kei. He said his mother never wrote anything
down about the room — the dimensions, she never wrote them. She only ever bought, and
tagged, and arranged. Which means the room only exists when someone stands in it and
measures. Which means she has been waiting, for sixty years, for someone to stand in it.

I think that someone is me. I think I have been walking into that room since the day I read
my mother's name on a dead woman's tag, and I think I will keep walking into it, because
that is what the account is. The account is a corridor. I am writing my way down it.

The kettle is boiling.

I do not remember standing up to fill it.

I am at the kettle now, and the water is going in, and the sound of it is the only sound in
the shop, and I am aware, with a clarity that has become ordinary this month, that I am
describing my own actions a half-second after performing them, as if I am reading a page a
moment before I write it.

Here is the sentence I have been circling:

The account is in the present tense now.

I did not decide to write it that way. I noticed it at the top of this page and I have not
been able to make it go back, and every time I try to write "I sat" it comes out "I sit,"
and every time I try to write "I was afraid" it comes out "I am afraid," and I have
decided, because I am a professional, to stop correcting the record.

You will have to take this as I take my inventory: the document says what it says. If the
tense has changed, then the tense has changed, and there is a reason, and I am no longer
sure that the reason is a grammatical one.

Somewhere in this shop, in a drawer I have not opened, are thirty years of repair tickets.
Mikami showed me one from 1991. He did not show me the rest. I have not asked to see them
because I already know what I will find, and I am not ready, tonight, to be right.

The water is in the pot. I am pouring it. The tag on the tea caddy is turned away from me,
the way Mikami turns the tags of his own things, out of respect or out of fear, I have never
asked which.

I am going to finish this account. I am going to find the room. I am going to measure it,
because measurement is the only thing I know how to do, and because a room that has waited
sixty years for someone to stand in it has, I think, already chosen who.

I am standing in it now. That is the sentence. I have written it, and it is true, and I am
not going to cross it out.


---

I am going to record one more thing before I stop writing in the past, because I have realised
that the past tense is where I have been hiding, and an appraiser does not hide; an appraiser
discloses.

The thing I have been hiding is this: I knew about the deaths before I understood the tags,
and I did nothing, and the reason I did nothing is that I had already begun to want the room.

I noticed the pattern in May. Four deaths, one a month, in the checkout order. I noticed it
and I did not call the police, because I did not have a crime, and because I did not want to
be a person who had called the police about a coincidence, and because — and this is the
disclosure — I did not want to be *removed from the room*. I wanted to be the one who
understood it. I wanted the valuation.

That is what the trade does to you, if you are good at it and not careful. It teaches you to
want the answer more than the object, and then it teaches you to want the answer more than
the people, and then one day you are standing in a dead woman's warehouse at midnight with a
tape measure, and you are not afraid of the room. You are *curious* about it. And curiosity,
in a room like that, is the same as consent.

I did not consent to the deaths. I want that on the record. But I consented to the *room*, and
the room had a place for me, and it had been waiting for someone who would come to it
*curious* and not *afraid*, and I had walked in on my own two feet, with a notebook.

That is the last honest thing I can write in the past tense. The rest is present, and the
present is where the room is.
