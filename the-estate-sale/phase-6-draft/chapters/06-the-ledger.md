# The Ledger

*Close third, past tense.*

---

Mikami read the ledger with gloves on, which was habit, not ritual; a bound account book
from the sixties is held together by its own dirt.

Two hundred and eleven entries. Mitsuko bought all her life, one object at a time, and she
wrote down for each the purchase, the price, the place, and, in a column she did not label,
a date that was not the purchase date.

[IMAGE 06.1 — the open ledger on a workbench, two facing pages: columns of purchases in a
small upright hand; one column, unlabelled, holds dates that do not match the purchases;
the earliest entry reads 1961.]

The purchases ran from 1961 to 2019. The unlabelled dates ran from 1961 to 2023, and the
last one was the year she died.

Mikami checked the first entry against the object. A lacquer tray, bought in 1961 in
Asakusa. The unlabelled date beside it was 1961 as well.

"It's not the purchase date," he said to Aoi, who was sitting on the workbench with a
notebook. "It's the date the seller died."

She looked at the ledger, then at him.

"You don't know that."

"Look at the pattern. She buys in 1961, the seller dies in 1961. She buys in 1974, the date
is 1974. She buys in 1991—"

He stopped, and turned the book, and pointed at the top of the page, where the columns were
ruled. The ledger ran to two columns on every line, and only one of them was ever labelled.
The ruled heading over the first column read *bought*; over the second, the heading was a year,
and the years in it ran one ahead of the purchases, and the year at the very top of the second
column was not a date at all.

"There are two years on every tag," he said. "The one the object remembers and the one the
person came to. The front is where the object was, in 1958, in the house. The back is when the
holder arrived — when they died, or when they were filed, or when they were left with the piece.
She ruled the column for it in 1961 and never named it, because naming it would have made it a
death list, and she needed it to be a distribution list."

He stopped.

[IMAGE 06.2 — a detail photograph of one ledger line, magnified: a purchase of a lacquer
comb in Sendagi, dated 1991; the unlabelled column reads 1991; in the margin, in pencil, a
single small character that looks like a tally.]

---

Aoi had been in the trade eleven years and she had learned to read a document the way she
read a room: not for what it said, but for what it was arranged around.

The ledger was arranged around a gap. Every purchase had an object, a price, a place — and
then the unlabelled column, which had nothing to do with the money. She turned the pages
and watched the gap widen. In 1961 the unlabelled dates matched the purchases. By 1970 the
purchases had begun to run ahead of the deaths, and by 1990 the deaths had begun to run
ahead of the purchases, and by 2000 there were entries with no object at all — a name, a
date, a place, and nothing bought.

"She wrote down people she never bought anything from," Aoi said. "Why."

Mikami did not answer. He was looking at the comb entry, and his face had gone the way a
face goes when a man has found his own name in a document he did not write.

"What happened in 1991," Aoi said.

"Nothing," said Mikami. "I restored a comb in 1991."

The shop was quiet. Outside, in Sendagi, a bicycle went past on the lane.

"So she knew you," Aoi said.

"She was a customer. She brought things to be mended. She never told me where they came
from, and I never asked, because you don't ask a customer, and because—"

He stopped again, and this time he did not start again for a while, and when he did, his
voice had changed.

"Because in 1991 I finished a repair on a comb, and the woman who owned it died the week I
returned it, and I told myself it was a coincidence, and I have told myself that every year
since."

[IMAGE 06.3 — a photograph of a workshop wall: dozens of repair tickets on strings, each
dated; one from 1991 is centred and legible; beside it, a small photograph of a woman
receiving a parcel, her face out of frame, her hands accepting the comb.]

---

Aoi wrote it down. She had been writing it down for two hours, and she realised she was
writing it down the way Mikami mended: to have a record, in case. In case of what, she did
not let herself finish.

She asked him to go through the ledger with her, page by page, and they did, and it took
until midnight, and by the end they had a table in her notebook: two hundred and eleven
objects, two hundred and eleven names, two hundred and eleven dates of death, and a
pattern.

Aoi drew it out as a chart and pinned it to the bench, because a pattern you can see is a
pattern you cannot argue with.

[IMAGE 06.4 — a hand-ruled chart in Aoi's notebook: a horizontal axis of years 1961 to 2023, a
vertical axis of names; two hundred small crosses; the crosses march in a single file, one per
year, and every seventh cross is circled; a red thread runs left to right along the file; the
last circle is empty, its margin note reading "the one with no name yet."]

The pattern was a chain. Each name on a tag was the previous holder of an object, and each
death cleared the object for the next purchase, and each purchase moved the object one step
closer to a place that was never written down.

"She's assembling something," Aoi said. "In order. Over sixty years. She has been buying
back the same room."

Mikami said nothing. He had known, Aoi understood later, since 1991; he had known and had
not let himself know, which is a different kind and a heavier kind.

She asked him one more question. She asks it now, in this account, knowing what it cost:

"Mikami. If the tags are the names of the dead, whose names are on them?"

He did not answer.

The ledger answered instead, on the last page, in the same small upright hand, a list of
twelve names that were not in the purchase columns at all.

They were not owners. They had bought nothing. They were there in a single column, dated
1958, with a place: Yanaka.

The twelfth was hers.

---

Aoi put her finger on the twelfth name and did not lift it for a long time.

The name was *SHIRAI*. Beside it, in the unlabelled column, a date that had not yet
happened, written in pencil, and rubbed out, and written again.

"She wrote my death date," Aoi said. "Before I was born."

Mikami shook his head. "She wrote a name and a date in a column of deaths," he said. "For
someone who was not dead yet. That's not a death date. That's a schedule."

"That's worse."

"Yes," he said. "That is considerably worse."

Aoi closed the ledger. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she had expected them not
to be. She looked around the workshop — the tickets on the wall, the bench, the drawers, the
tools — and she understood that she was standing in the middle of a document, and that the
document had her name in it, and that it had been there for twenty years, waiting for her to
open the drawer.

She said, aloud, to no one in particular, in the way she talked herself through a valuation:

"The room is not the point. The room is a clock. And I am the last hand."

Mikami looked up sharply, and Aoi, who had spent the day learning to read a dead woman's
handwriting, did not notice that she had said it in the present tense.


---

They came back the next morning, because Aoi had not slept and because Mikami had, and because
the ledger had a page neither of them had turned.

It was the inside cover, and it was not a page they had skipped; it was a page that had been
*glued down* and then, at some point, worked loose at one corner. Mikami found it when he
lifted the book to put it away: the cover board lifted slightly, and under it, on the board
itself, in the small upright hand, there was a line that had been written before the book was
bound.

It said: *if you are reading this, the room has been opened. I am sorry. I could not leave it
written on paper. So I wrote it in the room.*

Aoi looked at the line for a long time. Then she looked at Mikami.

"She wrote the room," Aoi said. "The room is the document. The ledger is only the index."

Mikami said nothing. He was looking at the line, and at the child's hand in it, which had been
eleven when it was written and had not changed in sixty years, which is the thing about a
hand: it is the last thing to change, and the first thing to be recognised.

[IMAGE 06.5 — the inside cover of the ledger, lifted from the board: a single line in a small
upright hand, written directly on the board, visible where the cover paper has come loose; a
magnifying glass rests on the page.]


---

Aoi went back to the ledger alone on the third night, because Mikami had gone home and because
she had begun to have a thought she did not want him to watch her have.

The thought was this: if the unlabelled column was the date the seller died, then the ledger
was not only a record of the room. It was a record of the *killings*. And if the killings had
begun in 1961 and continued to 2023, then either Mitsuko had killed a great many people over
sixty-two years, which is impossible for a woman in a house in Yanaka, or she had been told
the dates, by someone who knew them, which meant she had an *informant*, and the informant was
still alive, and the informant was the hand that had written the steadier lines on the backs
of the tags.

Aoi wrote the two hands side by side in her notebook, from the documents she had: the small
upright hand of the tags and the ledger, a child's hand, sixty years old on the first page and
steady by the last; and the second hand, steadier throughout, the one on the backs — *still
here?*, *the debt is not the name*, *she's you*, *thank you for keeping her*.

The second hand was not a child's. It was an adult's, and it was *not old*, because Aoi had
seen a great deal of old handwriting and the second hand had no tremor, no fading, no drift.
It was a hand in its forties or fifties and it had done all of the cruelty in the account.

Mitsuko had built a room. But Mitsuko had not written *still here?*. Mitsuko had not written
the line on Yano's photograph. Mitsuko had not ordered the deaths.

Someone had done all of that, in a steady hand, for thirty-five years, at the direction of a
woman who was called, by the district and by the trade, a harmless old collector of Yanaka.

Aoi sat with the ledger and understood that the book had one author and two hands, and that
the older hand was the *testimony* and the younger hand was the *enforcement*, and that the
whole thing had been a partnership, and that the partner — the hand — was still out there, and
was, by the dates, about to reach the end of the list.

upright hand, and the reverse of a tag in a steadier hand; a magnifying glass between them;
the two hands are visibly different in age and pressure.]
