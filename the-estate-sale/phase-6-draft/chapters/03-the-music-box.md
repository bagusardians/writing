# The Music Box

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The box played eight bars and stopped. Sakai let it stop. He had not asked it to play. He
had wound it because that is what you do with a cylinder box, and he had turned away to
write the lot number, and behind him the box had begun the tune a child would know.

He was forty-six and he had lived in Tokyo since university, and the tune was not a Tokyo
tune. He did not know how he knew that. It was the way the fourth bar went up where a
Tokyo tune would go down, the way the words would fit if there were words.

He knew it the way you know a smell.

[IMAGE 4.1 — a cylinder music box on a table, lid open, brass cylinder visible, a small
tag tied to the winding key. The comb and pins are in focus; the room behind is dark.]

The tag said *SAKAI — 1958 — YANAKA.*

He had bought the box at the estate in Kiyosumi-Shirakawa because it was cheap and the
wood was good. He had not read the tag until he got it home. He read it now and felt the
particular cold of a coincidence that is aimed at you.

---

Sakai was a sound engineer. He had spent twenty years recording things that other people
wanted remembered — wedding speeches, testimony, the last voicemails of the dying — and he
had a professional interest in the mechanics of memory. A music box was, to him, a
recording. A cylinder with pins, a comb with teeth, a tune that could not be changed. The
earliest sound recordings were cylinders. You could hold a cylinder in your hand and it
held four minutes of a dead person's voice, and you could play it, and the voice would come
out of a horn, and the person would be neither alive nor gone.

He had recorded his father once, at the end, for a project he never finished. He had not
played the file in nine years.

His father had been born in Yanaka. His father had never spoken about Yanaka. When Sakai
was a boy there had been a trip, once, on a day his father would not explain, to a lane
behind Yanaka Ginza where an old woman lived who gave them tea and did not smile, and
afterward his father had driven home without speaking and had never gone back. Sakai
remembered the tea more than the woman. The tea had tasted of the tin it came in.

He had asked, once, in the car on the way home, who the woman was, and his father had said,
"A customer," and Sakai had known, even at eight, that "customer" was the answer you give
when the true answer is a door you do not want a child to open.

The box played the tune again. He had not wound it.

faint hand-drawn diagram, lines and rectangles, the shape of a small room with a chest
marked against one wall.]

---

He turned the box over and opened the interior lid, which is where the maker's label
usually sits, and instead there were pencil lines: a small room, six mats, drawn with the
care of someone copying a memory. In one corner, a rectangle labelled with a single
character he could not read at first because the pencil was faint. He held it to the lamp.

*Tansu.*

He did not know why the room made him afraid. It was only a room. He had been in a hundred
six-mat rooms. But the drawing was from above, and slightly off-square, the way a child
draws a room they have looked at from the floor.

He set the box on the shelf and went to bed, and the tune came through the wall from the
sitting room, four bars at a time, patient.

---

His phone rang at two in the morning. It was the estate, Kanzaki Kei, and he sounded like a
man who had been awake for a long time.

"You bought the box," Kei said.

"Yes."

"You've played it."

Sakai did not answer.

"Don't play it again," Kei said. "I'll buy it back. Double. Whatever you paid."

"Why."

Kei was quiet. Then he said: "Because you've read it now."

Sakai, who had spent twenty years asking people questions for a living, heard the thing
Kei was not saying, and asked the question he would have asked any witness.

"What did your mother do with the box," he said. "In the room. In 1958."

Kei made a sound that was not a word. Then, in a voice that had gone very small, he said,
"She played it. She told me. She said it was the last thing that played in that room before
the men came, and she was behind the chest, and the box was on the shelf, and it played,
and she could hear it over everything. She said it was the only thing that stayed nice."

"Stayed nice."

"While the rest of it happened," Kei said. "She said the tune stayed nice. She's been
buying the boxes back for thirty years. There were four of them. You have the last one."

silhouette at the far end of the hall seen only as a shadow on the wall, the photographer's
own shadow missing.]

---

Sakai hung up and sat in the dark and did the thing he did with every recording: he
listened for the part that did not belong.

He did not play the box. He did not need to. He had heard it eight times now and he had it,
the way he had every sound he had ever recorded, in the part of memory that is not memory
but mechanics. And what he heard, now that he listened properly, was that the eighth bar
did not resolve the seventh. It went up where it should go down. It was not a mistake in
the cylinder; it was a different ending.

He had heard the tune before. Not from the box. From the trip to Yanaka. The old woman had
hummed it, at the door, when she said goodbye, and his father had stopped in the lane and
listened to the whole thing before he got in the car.

The woman had been telling them something. Sakai understood that now, thirty-eight years
late. She had not given them tea to be kind. She had been checking whether his father still
remembered, and the tune was the test, and his father had passed it by standing still in
the lane.

Sakai went into the sitting room. The box was on the shelf, closed.

He opened it, and wound it, and let it play.

Eight bars. Then a pause. Then, faintly, from the cylinder, in the way a cylinder sometimes
catches a voice if someone spoke over it when it was being cut, a woman's voice, old and
flat, saying a single word.

The word was a name. It was not his father's name. It was the name his father had been
given by the family that raised him, before he changed it, and it was the name Sakai had
never known, and hearing it come out of a wooden box in his own sitting room at two in the
morning, he understood that the box had not been waiting for the last owner.

It had been waiting for him.

Shrine, and a lane that runs behind them and is marked with a small tansu symbol and the
date 1958.]

---

He drove to Yanaka the next morning, before he could think better of it. He had the box in
a bag on the passenger seat. He found the lane. He found the house, or the place where the
house had been: a small lot with a new building on it, three floors, clean, no trace of the
old thing.

A woman was watering plants at the door. She was not old. She did not know the name. She
had bought the lot from a family that had sold it empty, years ago, and she was sorry, and
she hoped he found what he was looking for.

Sakai stood in the lane and looked at the new building and understood that the room was
gone, and that the objects were the only part of it left, and that someone had been
collecting the parts for sixty years, and that he now held one, and that this was the
reason the tune had come for him.

He went home. He put the box on the shelf and turned it so the tag faced the wall, the way
you do with a thing you have priced and cannot sell and do not want to look at.

The next morning it had turned itself around.

The tag, small and upright, faced the room, and the lid was open, and the comb was warm.


---

Sakai came to Aoi's flat in Tateishi in November, because Aoi had called him about a cylinder
box and because Sakai had spent a month listening to a tune and had decided, at last, that he
should hear it with someone else in the room.

He brought the box in a cloth. He set it on the low table. He did not wind it.

Aoi, who had spent the day pricing an estate in Nezu, made tea, and asked him what he wanted
to say, and he told her the story I have set down in full above: the tune, the father, the
lane behind Yanaka Ginza, the old woman at the door who hummed it, the voice on the cylinder
saying his father's first name.

Then he said the thing that he had not told anyone, and that he had come to Tateishi to say.

He said: "The tune isn't a lullaby. I finally placed it. It's a *counting* song. It's the song
children sing in some parts of the country while they count something they don't want to
look at. You sing four bars, you count one thing, you sing four bars, you count the next."

Aoi asked him what the box was counting.

"I don't know," Sakai said. "There were twelve people in that room and the tune has twelve
bars if you play it through, and the box only ever plays eight, and I think it's stuck. I
think someone stopped it at eight."

He wound it, then, once, at Aoi's nod, and the box played its eight bars, and stopped, and the
silence that followed was not empty.
