# The Mirror

*Live POV. Present tense.*

---

The app wants four photographs, so I am taking four photographs.

That is the whole job now. You stand in a stranger's emptied flat and you make a stranger's
furniture look like something a person would want, and you upload it, and somewhere a buyer
decides. I photograph the mirror from the front. I photograph it from the left, where the
crack catches the light. I photograph the maker's mark on the back, which is worn to a
smudge. Then I step in front of it to photograph the full height, and that is the fourth
photograph, and in the fourth photograph, behind me, the room is not the room.

I look at the screen. Then I look at the room. Then I look at the screen.

Behind my reflection there is a doorway that is not behind me. It is low and pale, and
through it there is a floor of six mats, and a chest against the wall, and the wall has no
window. I turn around. The wall behind me is a wall. There is no doorway and there is
nothing beyond it, only plaster and the ghost of where a shelf was.

The phone has forty-one percent. The room has no doorway. The photograph has both.

I take the fourth photograph again. The doorway is still there, smaller, as if it has
noticed me noticing it.

[IMAGE 2.1 — the fourth photograph: a woman's reflection in a cracked mirror; behind her,
where the wall should be, a low pale doorway into a windowless six-mat room.]

---

The buyer arrives at two.

He is early and he is polite and he asks about the mirror before he asks about anything,
which is how I know he has already seen the listing. He does not look at the glass. He
looks at the back, at the maker's mark, and he turns it with two hands the way you turn a
plate you intend to buy.

"You've had it long?" he says.

"It's a lot in an estate. It came in this morning."

"From the Yanaka house."

I don't answer. He doesn't need me to. He lifts the mirror an inch off the table and sets
it down, testing the weight, and the crack in the glass finds the light and throws it on
the wall, a thin bright line.

"There'll be a tag on it," he says.

I am holding the phone. I am thinking about the fourth photograph and the doorway that
does not exist, and I am aware, the way you are aware of a draught, that I should have
left when I first saw it.

"There's a tag," I say.

He asks to see it. I tell him the tag stays with the lot; that's the rule of the sale. He
smiles as if I have told a joke he has heard before.

"That's the rule," he agrees.

Then he walks around the table to stand behind me, and I understand, in the moment it
takes him to cross the four steps, that he has not come for the mirror.

The flash goes.

I am still holding the pose when I understand the photograph has taken something I cannot
get back. He asks me to tilt my chin. I tilt my chin. The crack in the glass runs down
between us, and in it, small, the room with no window, and a child standing in the
doorway, watching me price her house.

I set the phone down.

"You should have left the lot alone," he says.

It is the politest thing anyone has ever said to me.
