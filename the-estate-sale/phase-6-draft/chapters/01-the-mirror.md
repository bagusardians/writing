# The Mirror

*Live POV. Present tense.*

---

The app wants four photographs, so I am taking four photographs.

That is the whole job now. You stand in a stranger's emptied flat and you make a stranger's
furniture look like something a person would want, and you upload it, and somewhere a buyer
decides. I have done it eleven thousand times. I can tell you the reserve on a tansu before
I open the drawer, and the story a water stain is telling, and whether a lacquer is Taishō
or a good Meiji fake, and none of that helps me now, because the mirror is in the room and
the room is wrong.

The flat is in Nezu, third floor, a corner unit over a senbei shop. The owner died in
January. The family is in Osaka and wants it gone by the end of the month, and they have
told me to price everything and photograph everything and not to call them unless there is
an emergency, which is the standing instruction and means they do not want to hear from me
at all.

I photograph the mirror from the front. Black lacquer, Meiji, a dressing mirror on a stand.
The lacquer has gone soft at the edges the way old lacquer does, and the glass has a
hairline crack from the lower left corner up toward the centre, like a river on a map. I
photograph it from the left, where the crack catches the light. I photograph the maker's
mark on the back, which is worn to a smudge.

Then I step in front of it to photograph the full height, and that is the fourth
photograph, and in the fourth photograph, behind me, the room is not the room.

I look at the screen. Then I look at the room. Then I look at the screen.

Behind my reflection there is a doorway that is not behind me. It is low and pale, and
through it there is a floor of six mats, and a chest against the wall, and the wall has no
window. I turn around. The wall behind me is a wall. There is no doorway and there is
nothing beyond it, only plaster and the ghost of where a shelf was.

The phone has forty-one percent. The room has no doorway. The photograph has both.

I take the fourth photograph again. The doorway is still there, smaller, as if it has
noticed me noticing it.

where the wall should be, a low pale doorway into a windowless six-mat room.]

---

I should say who I am, because this account will be read by someone who never knew me, and the
reader will want to know whether I was anyone in particular. I was not. I am an appraiser and
estate photographer, the same trade as the woman whose account this belongs to — Shirai Aoi, who
I met twice at sales and who will one day read these pages — and we knew each other the way
people in a small trade know each other: by our work, and by a restorer in Sendagi we both used,
a man named Mikami, who repairs what the rest of us only price. This is my statement. It is not
hers, and it is not about the mirror, and I am writing it on a phone in a flat in Nezu while a
polite man cleans a mirror that does not need cleaning.

I am not, by temperament, a person who sees things.

I want that on the record, because the account that follows is full of things I saw, and a
reader is entitled to ask whether I was the kind of narrator who sees. I am not. I have
worked with antiques for eleven years and the trade has one iron rule: the object is what
it is. It is not what it reminds you of. I have sold a dead child's teddy bear without
tears because the bear was a 1950s Asahi and worth four thousand yen, and the tears were
not part of the transaction, and I have never once in my life seen a ghost.

So when I say the doorway was in the photograph, I am not telling you I believed it. I am
telling you it was in the photograph. I have the photograph. It is in the account.

I put the phone in my pocket and I finished the flat.

That is the thing I keep coming back to. I finished the flat. There were two more rooms and
a kitchen and a set of shelves, and I priced them all, and I uploaded the listing, and I
did the professional thing, which is to treat an anomaly as a lighting problem and move on.
It is possible to know two things at once — that a wall is a wall, and that a photograph of
the wall is not — and to choose, for eleven hours a day, to believe the wall.

I have thought about those eleven hours more than almost anything else. Because if I had
left then, I would never have met him, and I would be alive. I have decided that this is
not a comfort and not a regret but simply a fact, the way a value is a fact: eleven hours
of fixing the lighting by believing the wall.

---

The buyer arrives at two.

He is early and he is polite and he asks about the mirror before he asks about anything,
which is how I know he has already seen the listing. He does not look at the glass. He
looks at the back, at the maker's mark, and he turns it with two hands the way you turn a
plate you intend to buy.

"You've had it long?" he says.

"It's a lot in an estate. It came in this morning."

"From the Yanaka house."

I don't answer. He doesn't need me to. He lifts the mirror an inch off the table and sets
it down, testing the weight, and the crack in the glass finds the light and throws it on
the wall, a thin bright line.

"There'll be a tag on it," he says.

I am holding the phone. I am thinking about the fourth photograph and the doorway that
does not exist, and I am aware, the way you are aware of a draught, that I should have
left when I first saw it.

"There's a tag," I say.

He asks to see it. I tell him the tag stays with the lot; that's the rule of the sale. He
smiles as if I have told a joke he has heard before.

"That's the rule," he agrees.

Then he walks around the table to stand behind me, and I understand, in the moment it
takes him to cross the four steps, that he has not come for the mirror.

I want to describe him exactly, because I have learned that description is the only kind of
evidence I am still able to give. He is perhaps sixty. He wears a grey coat with a good
shoulder, and his shoes are polished the way a man polishes shoes when the shoes are older
than the man who sold them to him. His hands are folded at his waist. He does not smell of
anything. He is the most ordinary man I have ever been afraid of.

"You've read the tag," he says. It is not a question.

I say nothing.

"Read it again," he says.

standing on the boards; a man in a grey coat stands behind the woman who is photographing;
her phone is raised, and in the mirror the same man appears twice, once behind her and once
further back, in the doorway.]

---

I will tell the killing the way I told the reading, because it happened slowly enough to be
told.

He asked me to photograph the mirror once more. He said it in a way that sounded like an
appraisal instruction, and I am trained to obey appraisals, and so I raised the phone.

"Not that one," he said. "The tall one. Stand in front."

I stood in front. I looked into the glass. Behind me, in the glass, the doorway was open
and the six-mat room was there, and the chest, and on the chest a small white tag moved in
a draught that was not in the real room, because there was no draught in the real room.

"Closer," he said.

I took one step. The crack in the glass ran from the corner to the centre, and it divided
my face horizontally, and through the lower half of the crack I could see the room, and
through the upper half I could see the flat.

The flash goes.

I am still holding the pose when I understand the shutter has taken something I cannot get
back. He tells me to tilt my chin. I tilt my chin. The crack in the glass runs down between
us, and in it, small, the room with no window, and a child standing in the doorway, watching
me price her house.

I set the phone down.

"You should have left the lot alone," he says.

It is the politest thing anyone has ever said to me.

---

He did not hurry. That is what I have to say about the end of me: he did not hurry.

He took the tag off the mirror first. He did it carefully, sliding the cotton thread over
the top of the stand so as not to tear it, and he put the tag in his coat pocket, and then
he took a cloth from his other pocket, folded, and he began to clean the glass.

I did not understand. I said, "What are you doing," and he said, "A mirror should be clean
for the next person," and kept cleaning, and I stood there and let him, because I am
trained to hold still while a thing is handled, and because some part of me still believed
this was an appraisal.

The cloth was damp. It smelled faintly of something I could not name, and the smell stayed
in the room after he had finished, and I have smelled it once since, on a train, on a man's
coat, and I got off at the next station.

He cleaned the glass in small circles, working from the outside in, and as he worked the
crack began to widen, or I began to see it better, and the room in the glass began to
resolve — the six mats, the chest, the tag on the chest — and I understood, finally, that
he was not cleaning the mirror.

He was cleaning the window.

[IMAGE 01.3 — close: a man's gloved hand wiping a mirror's surface in slow circles; the
reflection in the glass shows a six-mat room with a chest; the cloth leaves a faint
milky smear.]

---

I am writing this on the phone, and the phone is at nineteen percent, and the room has
stopped being the room.

That is the clearest way I can put it. I am still standing in the flat in Nezu. The low
table is still there. But the doorway is open now in the wall, and the six-mat room is
beyond it, and the chest has a tag on it, and the tag has a name on it, and the name is
mine.

He has not hit me. I want to be clear. There is no weapon. He is standing by the door with
his hands folded, and the room is doing it, or the mirror is doing it, or the thing in my
head is doing it, and I have decided that the distinction no longer matters.

I am finding it harder to move. That is the physical fact. Not pain. A heaviness, as if the
air has become the kind of air you find at the bottom of a stairwell. My hand on the phone
is slower than it was. The sentences are taking longer to arrive.

He says one more thing before I stop being able to answer him.

He says: "Your mother took it from the house in 1958. The servant's daughter. I've been
waiting for it to come back to the family since I was a boy, and now it has, and now there
are only two pieces left, and you're one of them."

I do not know what that means. I am writing it down because I am a professional, and because
the account is the only thing I am still able to finish.

The doorway is very close now. I can see the mat edges. Six mats, and in the corner a
tansu, and behind the tansu, the place where a child would hide.

I am not going to finish this. I am going to type until the screen goes dark, and I am
writing for whoever finds the phone, and I want them to know two things, because they are
the only things I have.

The first is that he was polite the whole time.

The second is that the mirror was not the point. The mirror was never the point. The point
is the room, and I have seen it now, and the room is where I am going, and the room is not
empty.

[IMAGE 01.4 — the phone, later: a camera app open on the desk, screen showing the room with
no window; the desk chair is empty; a small white tag, blank, lies on the seat.]


---

I have to keep typing even though the words are coming slower, because the count is nearly
done and the phone is the only thing in this room that is still mine.

The room has taken on a smell. It is not a bad smell. It is the smell of old tatami and
shellac and cold tea, which is Mikami's shop, which is three kilometres away, and the room is
using it, the way a person uses a voice they have heard before.

I can see the tags now, all of them, on the walls, though the walls are the walls of a flat
in Nezu and there are no tags in the flat in Nezu. I can read them. I do not want to read
them. The ones nearest me are the ones I have to read.

They are the names of the people who have been in this account. I do not know how I know
that. I know it the way you know a valuation before you have finished the arithmetic.

There is one tag left blank, at the end of the row, at my eye level, and it is turned so I can
see it, and it has no name and no date and no place, and I understand, in the last of the
light, that it is waiting, and that it is not waiting for me.

It is waiting for whoever reads this.

I am going to put the phone down now. I am going to face the doorway, because that is what the
child did, and because after eleven years of pricing dead people's rooms I have learned that
the last honest thing you can do in a strange room is to stand in it the way it was built to
be stood in.

The flash goes one more time. I do not take it. The room takes it.
