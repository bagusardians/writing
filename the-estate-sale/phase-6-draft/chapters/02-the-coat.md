# The Coat

*Close third, past tense.*

---

Yano did not buy the coat. He stood in the doorway of his own shop and read its tag twice,
and then he read it a third time, and by then the shop had closed around him and the tag
had not changed.

He ran a reuse shop in Ueno, four racks and a counter, and he had been doing it for
nineteen years. He knew what a coat was. A coat is a person's decision to be warm, made
once, and then the coat outlives the decision. He had sold four hundred coats. He had
never once kept one.

The coat was heavy grey wool, Shōwa-cut, shoulders too wide for anyone alive. The tag was
tied to the hanger with cotton thread, small and upright:

*KURODA — 1958 — YANAKA*

He knew the hand. He had seen it three times that month, on three lots he had bought at the
Kanzaki estate, and he had sold two of them, and he had kept the third in the back because
he could not find a buyer who did not stop reading the tag.

[IMAGE 02.1 — a reuse-shop rack: a heavy grey overcoat on a hanger; a small handwritten tag
tied with cotton thread, reading KURODA, 1958, YANAKA.]

---

The three lots had come from the estate like everything else: cheap, clean, and strange.
Yano bought in bulk from estate clearers, sight unseen, fifty thousand yen for a van of
household goods, and he sorted and priced and sold, and the margin was thin and the work
was honest. He did not usually read the tags. Tags went in the bin with the packing
material, because a tag with a previous owner's name on it depresses the sale.

But this estate's tags were not previous owners' names. He had understood that in the first
week, and he had understood it the way you understand a smell in a room: without being able
to say what it was.

The first lot was a tea caddy. Tag: *TOYAMA — 1958 — YANAKA*. He sold it in a day to a
salaryman who collected tea utensils and did not read tags.

The second was a length of kimono silk, unused, Shōwa. Tag: *SADA — 1958 — YANAKA*. He sold
it to a dressmaker in Nezu.

The third was the daishō stand, and the third was where he stopped, because the tag on the
daishō stand said *YANO — 1958 — YANAKA*, and Yano is not a common name, and Yano was his
name, and his father's, and his grandfather's.

He had looked up the name. He had not meant to. It was the kind of thing you do at the
counter at closing time with your phone in your hand, hunting a dead man the way you'd
pick at a scab. Kuroda Genji had drowned in a pond at Inokashira in 1958, aged fifty-two,
and the record said accident, and the record did not say why a man from Yanaka was at
Inokashira in the winter.

Yano put the coat on the rack and priced it at twelve thousand and went home.

On the way he thought about his father, who had owned a coat like it, and who had died in
March, and whose coat he had sold in April because he could not stand to have it in the
house. He thought: everyone's father is in the coat. That is what a coat is.

---

The next morning the rack was empty of the grey coat.

He asked his one part-timer, a student named Emi who worked three mornings a week and
wanted to be a curator, and Emi said a man had come at nine, well-dressed, polite, and paid
the twelve thousand and taken the coat, and left no name, and asked about the tag. Emi had
given him the tag. It was attached to the coat. It was always attached to the coat.

Emi was troubled. She said the man had asked her, before he paid, whether she had read the
tag, and she had said no, and he had said, "Good," and she had felt, she told Yano, like a
child who has passed a test she did not know she was sitting.

Yano went into the back and stood where the third lot was, the thing he had kept, the old
daishō stand he could not sell. He had kept it because of its tag. The tag said *YANO —
1958 — YANAKA*.

His family had never lived in Yanaka. He had never heard the name in his life. Then he
thought of his grandfather, who had also been a Yano, and who had died before Yano was
born, and whose life was a set of facts his family had never once narrated. A grandfather
he never met could have stood in a room in Yanaka in 1958. A name on a tag is not a
person. It is an heir.

[IMAGE 02.2 — a separate lot in the back room: a dark wooden daishō stand, two sword hooks,
with a small tag reading YANO, 1958, YANAKA. It is photographed in dim light.]

---

He called the estate. The son answered, Kanzaki Kei, tired and quick. Yano asked whether
there were more tags. Kei said there were two hundred lots, all tagged the same, by his
mother, and he did not know what they meant, and he did not want to.

"Do you know what your mother did in 1958," Yano asked.

"She was eleven," Kei said. "She was a child. There's nothing she did."

Yano said thank you and hung up and looked at the tag on the daishō stand again. His own
name, in a dead woman's hand, dated before he was born. He went to the door and locked it,
though it was only eleven in the morning, and he did not know why until he heard, on the
other side of the glass, someone knock politely and wait.

He turned the tag over. On the back, in pencil, small: *still here?*

[IMAGE 02.3 — the reverse of a tag, held in a hand: in faint pencil, the words "still
here?" with a date, 1958.]

---

Yano did not open the door. He stood behind the glass with his hand on the bolt and looked
at the man on the other side, and the man looked back with the patience of someone who has
already been let in.

It was not the same man who had bought the coat; Yano was almost sure. This man was older,
or else Yano was only now willing to see the age. He wore a grey coat. He had his hands
folded.

"Yano-san," the man said, through the glass. "You have the stand."

Yano said nothing.

"You've had it three weeks. You've read the tag twice. You've looked up Kuroda." The man's
voice was reasonable. It was the voice of a man explaining a delivery. "That's how it
starts. It's not your fault. The tag does it."

"What do you want," Yano said, through the glass, and his voice came out smaller than he
intended.

"The stand," the man said. "And the tag. The tag most of all. It's the last one from that
shelf unless the mirror comes back, and the mirror is spoken for."

Yano understood, then, that the man had not come to rob him. You do not rob a shop by
knocking on the glass in daylight. You come to finish a transaction. And Yano, who had
spent nineteen years finishing transactions, knew the feeling of a customer who has
already decided and is only waiting for the seller to agree.

"I don't have the stand for sale," Yano said.

"Everything's for sale," the man said, not unkindly. "That's the trade."

[IMAGE 02.4 — the shop exterior at night from across the street: the closed sign, a figure
seen through the glass standing where the counter is, facing the racks; the photographer is
outside, at a distance, and the shop's reflection in the window shows one more person than
the room contains.]

---

Yano did the thing he had done with the coat, which was to do nothing, which was the
professional thing.

He went home. He locked his flat. He put the daishō stand in the corner of his bedroom
where he could see it, which he had never done with a stock item in nineteen years, and he
sat with his back to the wall and looked at it.

Around midnight he understood the thing that had been sitting under his skin for three
weeks.

The tag on the stand was his grandfather's name. But he did not have a grandfather on the
Yano side; his father had been raised by a stepfather, and the Yano name had come into the
family in a way no one had ever explained. Which meant the tag did not name his
grandfather.

It named whoever had first borne the name the family now carried. It named a man who had
been in a room in Yanaka in 1958, and paid for it, and passed the debt down.

"I'm an heir to a room I've never seen," Yano said aloud, to no one, in the dark, and the
sound of his own voice in the flat made him get up and check the door.

The door was locked.

The stand was not in the corner.

---

He found it in the front room, by the window, facing the street, and the tag was no longer
on it.

In its place, on the wall, someone had taped a photograph. It was a photograph of Yano,
taken that night, in his flat, asleep in the chair by the wall — or not asleep; Yano did
not remember closing his eyes; but the photograph showed his eyes closed, and the daishō
stand behind him in the corner, where he had put it, hours before he moved it.

He turned the photograph over.

On the back, in pencil, small and upright: *YANO — 1958 — YANAKA.*

And beneath it, in a steadier hand, the words: *the debt is not the name. the debt is the
silence. you have been silent three weeks. that is long enough.*

Yano looked up at the window. Outside, across the street, under the streetlamp, a man in a
grey coat stood with his hands folded, watching the flat, waiting, the way he had waited
outside the shop, the way he had waited outside every shop that had ever sold a tagged
thing, and Yano understood, with the particular clarity of the very tired, that he had
already read the tag more than four times.

He went to the phone to call the police. He dialled two digits.

Then he stopped, because the photograph on the wall was not a photograph of him asleep,
and he had known that from the first look. It was a photograph of him dead. It showed the
particular slackness a face takes when the person is gone, and the daishō stand behind
him, and the tag on the stand, and Yano had never seen his own face like that in his life,
and yet he recognised it, the way you recognise a value on a thing you have not priced.

He put the phone down, and he sat in the chair by the wall, because that was where the
photograph said he would be, and because it seemed, by then, like the only seat left.


---

Aoi found Emi four days later, at the reuse shop in Ueno, because a part-timer who has sold a
coat to a man who asked about a tag is a witness, and Aoi had begun to collect witnesses the
way she collected provenance: carefully, and in order.

Emi was twenty-three and wanted to be a curator and had not slept well since April. She sat
in the back room with the daishō stand, which she had been told not to touch, and she told Aoi
the whole of it, in the way a person tells a thing they have been holding alone.

She said the man had come at nine, before opening, and had knocked on the glass, and Yano had
not been in yet, and so she had opened the door herself, which she was not supposed to do.
She said the man had asked, before anything else, "Do you have a coat with a tag on it," and
she had said yes, many things have tags, and he had said, "A grey Shōwa coat. A tag in a
particular hand." And she had brought out the coat, and he had read the tag, and had smiled,
and had paid twelve thousand and asked her to leave the tag on.

She said: "He asked me whether I had read the tag. I said no. He said good."

Aoi asked her why she had not read it.

Emi said: "Because you don't read a tag with a customer's name on it. It's rude. And because
Mr Yano told me once that some tags are private." She looked at Aoi. "But I read it later. I
went back and read it, after Mr Yano got quiet. It said a name and a year and a place, and the
year was 1958, and the place was Yanaka, and the name was Kuroda."

Aoi wrote it down. Then she asked the question she asked every witness, which was the only
question she had that ever produced anything.

"Did he say anything else. Anything that didn't fit."

Emi thought about it for a long time. Then she said: "He said the coat had been well kept. He
said, 'Someone kept this well.' And then he said, 'That's the trouble with your trade. You
keep things you should give back.'"

Aoi asked who he had meant.

"I thought he meant Mr Yano," Emi said. "Now I think he meant all of us. Anyone who keeps a
thing another person died holding."

[IMAGE 02.5 — the back room of the reuse shop: a young part-time worker seated beside the
daishō stand, a notebook on her knee; the stand's tag is turned to the wall; the shop's front
window, out of focus, shows a street.]


---

Yano's account ends in the chair, and what follows is Aoi's, assembled from the notebook he
kept and from the ledger Aoi found on his counter, and it is short, because a shopkeeper in
Ueno does not leave much behind but a ledger and a chair.

The notebook was forty pages of stock. He wrote in it every day he owned the shop: what came
in, what went out, what he paid, what he sold it for. The last eleven pages are different.
They are the coat's chain — every owner of the grey Shōwa coat, traced backwards, from the
estate to 1958 — and Yano had found four owners, and all four were dead, and he had written
beside each one the year and the cause, in the same small hand a man uses for a price.

The causes were: illness, 1974; a fall, 1988; illness, 2003; illness, 2019. And then, in the
margin of 1958, where the chain began, he had written something that was not a cause and not a
price.

He had written: *the man the coat belonged to. the one who drowned at Inokashira. the coat was
his, and it came back, and it keeps coming back.*

Aoi sat in the reuse shop in Ueno with the notebook and understood that Yano had solved the
coat completely, three weeks before he died, and had done the thing every honest shopkeeper
does with a thing he cannot sell: he had priced it, written it down, and left it in the shop
for the next person.

He had also, on the last page, drawn a small diagram. It was a map of the coat's journey, and
the journey was a loop, and the loop began at Yanaka and ended at Yanaka, and the coat had
spent sixty-five years going around it, from hand to hand, while a woman in a house at the
centre of the loop bought it back every time it reached her.
