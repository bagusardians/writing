# The Photographs

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The envelope arrived at Aoi's flat on the first of November, six months and a half after the
sale, and it had been delayed, and the delay was the whole of the story.

It was addressed in a careful hand, to *Shirai Aoi*, and it had two stamps on it, and a
return address she did not recognise: a flat in Nezu, no name. Inside were eleven photographs
and a note. The note said:

*I proofread for a living. I found the album. I have photographed all of it and I am sending
it to you because you are the only name in the sale that seemed to be looking for the answer
rather than the price. Please look at photograph four. — S.*

Suzuki. The proofreader. Aoi recognised the flat, the paper, the precision. She had never met
him, and she would never meet him, because by the first of November Suzuki had been dead for
three weeks.

The police had closed it as a fall. A man of fifty-one, living alone, at the top of a
stairwell, on a wet night, no note, no struggle, no reason. His family in Shizuoka had
accepted it. His family had not known he had bought an album at an estate sale, or that he
had been photographing rooms for nine years, or that he had spent the last month of his life
reading the back of a photograph of a dead man and finding the one wrong word.

name; eleven photographs fanned out beside it; the top one is a black-and-white print of a

---

Aoi looked at the eleven photographs for four hours.

They were, as Suzuki had promised, the album. Fifty prints, reproduced in eleven composite
photographs, in order, 1962 to 1989, and the loose 1958 print, and the note on the back,
which Suzuki had photographed in raking light so the pencil showed.

But it was photograph four that held her, and she saw why within a minute, and that is the
moment the account stops being a detective story and becomes a thing about inheritance.

Photograph four was a single album page: three prints of the room. In the first, from 1962,
the room holds four objects — a chest, a lacquer box, a folding screen, and a mirror. In the
second, from 1971, there are more. In the third, from 1979, there are many.

And in all three, the mirror is in the same place, against the same wall, and it is Aoi's
mirror: black lacquer, a dressing mirror on a stand, with a crack down the glass.

Aoi had spent her life assuming the mirror came to her mother in 2003, when her mother died,
or before, as a family thing. It did not. The mirror was in the room in Yanaka in 1962. It
was in the room in 1971 and 1979. It was *in the collection* — and the collection was
supposed to have been assembled after the room was emptied.

The mirror was in the photographs before the mirror was supposed to be in the collection.

Which meant one of two things, and Aoi sat with both of them, and they were both terrible.

Either the album was photographed *in the room*, before it was emptied — in which case the
room was never emptied, and Mitsuko grew up in it, and the 1958 death happened among those
objects, not after they were gone. Or the mirror was in the Kanzaki house all along, and the
tag that said *SHIRAI — 2003 — TATEISHI* was not the mirror's provenance but the mirror's
*destination* — the place and the year it was supposed to end up, which was with Aoi's
mother, and then with Aoi.

Aoi put the photograph down and understood that the collection had a timeline that ran
forward as well as back. The tags named the dead. But the objects had a future too, and her
mother's name and death were in it, and hers was at the end.

[IMAGE 14.2 — the composite of album page four: three black-and-white prints of the same
six-mat room across three decades, the objects increasing; in all three, a black-lacquer
mirror stands against the same wall.]

---

She called Hoshino, who was the only one left who would still answer, and asked her the
question she could not ask Mikami.

"Can an object be photographed in a place it was never in?"

Hoshino, who had spent her life reading documents, said the most useful thing anyone said to
Aoi in the whole account.

"Any document can be made to say anything if the person making it controls the record," she
said. "You've been asking whether the room is real. Wrong question. The room is a *filing
system*. The album is a file. The photographs are the file's contents. If you want to know
whether the room was emptied in 1958, don't look at the album. Look at what the album
*replaced*."

"What did it replace?"

"An inventory," Hoshino said. "Somewhere there is a list of what was in that room when it was
searched in 1958. The police would have made one, or the family, or the temple. A room where
a man died gets inventoried. Find the inventory, and you find out what was really in the room
that night, and what Mitsuko has been buying back ever since — and what she *added*."

Aoi sat in her flat in Tateishi with eleven photographs of a room and no inventory, and
understood that she had been reading the wrong document for seven months, and that the
album, which Suzuki had died for, was not the proof. It was the *cover*. Someone had made a
fifty-print album of a room over twenty-seven years so that anyone who found it would
believe the room had been assembled slowly, by a collector, after the fact — when in truth
the mirror, and perhaps other objects, had never left.

Mitsuko had made the album to be found. It was a witness statement written in photographs,
folded and unfolded for sixty years.

She went to Nezu that night to thank Suzuki, or to find him, and she stood outside his flat,
at the top of the stairwell, in the wet, and the police tape was gone and the door was
sealed and a neighbour told her, kindly, that the gentleman had fallen and that it was very
sad and that he had been a quiet man who kept photographs.

Aoi asked whether anyone had come to the flat, in the weeks before.

The neighbour thought about it. She said yes, actually. A gentleman had come, perhaps a month
ago, well-dressed, polite, and had asked after Mr Suzuki, and had been told he was out, and
had left a card, and the neighbour had thought nothing of it because he was so polite.

Aoi asked what the card said.

The neighbour did not remember. But she said the gentleman had come back, and had waited on
the landing, in the evening, more than once, and that the last time she had seen Mr Suzuki
alive he had been coming up the stairs with a bag of shopping, and there had been a
gentleman at the top of the stairs, and they had spoken, and the neighbour had gone inside
because it was late.

Aoi stood on the landing and looked down the stairs, and she understood the shape of it. Not
a fall. A conversation at the top of a stairwell, in the wet, with a polite man, and then a
man who had spent a month finding the one wrong word in a dead woman's photograph, and who
had found it, and who had been going to post an envelope.

The envelope had been delayed because Suzuki had held it, and then because his flat had been
sealed, and then because the police had gone through his things slowly, and by the time it
reached Aoi it was November and the account was nearly over.

Aoi went home and added Suzuki to the folder, as the fourth death, and wrote in her notebook
the line that became the spine of everything after:

*the collector did not kill the witnesses. the collector kept them. someone else has been
killing the witnesses to keep the room from being read, and the someone else has been doing
it since before the sale, and the someone else is not the collector, and that is why he is
still here.*

She underlined *he*.

Then she underlined it again.

shopping on the floor, an envelope halfway under the door that has not been posted; the
camera is at the top of the stairs looking down.]


---

Aoi did the thing an appraiser does with a document that has been tampered with, which is to
look for the *original*, because a copy tells you what someone wanted you to see and an
original tells you what was there.

The album's originals had been in the Kanzaki house. Kei had cleared the house. So Aoi went
back to Kei, for the third time, and asked him the question she had not asked: what had he
thrown away.

Kei did not understand, at first. He said he had not thrown anything away; he had sold
everything. Aoi asked whether he had thrown anything away while he was *clearing* — not
selling, not keeping, clearing. He said no, and then he stopped, and then he said the thing
Aoi had come for: that there had been one box he had not been able to sell and had not been
willing to throw out, and had left in the house, because it was too heavy to carry and because
it was full of papers, and papers do not sell.

Aoi asked what the box held.

"Household papers," Kei said. "Insurance. Old bills. A deed with a name on it that made no
sense. Photographs, maybe. I didn't look."

Aoi asked whether the box was still in the house.

He said he thought so. He said the house was empty and locked and no one had been in it since
March except, apparently, whoever had plastered the wall and left the keys.

Aoi went to the house that night, with the keys from the hook, and let herself in.

[IMAGE 15.4 — the entrance hall of an emptied Yanaka house at night, torchlight: a box of
household papers on the floor where it was left; on the wall, the plastered rectangle; the
ring of keys in the narrator's hand, the oldest brass.]

---

The box was where he said. It had four decades of household paper in it, and at the bottom,
under the insurance and the bills, there was a folded sheet of heavy paper that was not a
bill and not a deed.

It was a *list*. In the small upright hand, older than the ledger, in a hand that was not yet
steady. Thirty-one numbered lines. The objects of the room, as a child of eleven had set them
down, from memory, the year after the night.

Aoi read the list in the empty house, with the torch, and it was the inventory that the temple
priest had made, written independently, from the other side of the wall: not what a priest
saw, but what a child remembered. And the two lists matched, line for line, except for the two
objects that were already gone when the priest came.

The child had listed the chest first. Number one, before the mirror, before the screen,
before anything else. *The chest, in the corner, where I sat.* And beside it, in the margin,
in a hand that was already trying to be small and upright, a note:

*it was taken the same night. I don't know where. I have to find it.*

Aoi stood in the empty house and understood that the search had begun the *year after* the
night, when Mitsuko was twelve, and that the chest had been missing for longer than the
collection had existed, and that the whole sixty years had been one child trying to get back
the object she had hidden behind.

[IMAGE 15.5 — a torchlit floor in an emptied house: a heavy paper sheet with thirty-one
numbered lines in a child's hand; the first line reads "the chest, in the corner"; a margin
note; around it, insurance papers and old bills.]

---

She called the priest, Ono, from the house, at eleven at night, and asked him one question:
whether the temple's record showed who had *taken* the chest.

Ono said it did not. But he said there was a second note, filed with the inventory, that he
had not read to her because he had not understood it until now.

He read it.

It said: *a servant girl came for the chest, and the family gave it to her, and she was seen
carrying it toward the river. Ask after the servant.*

Aoi stood in the empty house with the torch on the list and the phone to her ear, and put the
last pieces together, the way an appraiser closes a provenance.

The chest went out with Hana, the servant, the same night the mirror did. The mirror went to
Hana as payment. The chest went too, because the chest was where the child hid.

Hana could not carry the mirror *and* the chest. She chose — or was told — to take the chest.

The chest went down the Sumida and into the hands of whichever family member kept it for sixty
years. It surfaced in a warehouse in Kuramae in the autumn, placed by someone who knew exactly
what it was, in the corner of the room, where it had always stood.

Somebody had kept the chest. Somebody had brought it back. And the only person who could have
had it, if it went out with Hana, was the person on the other end of the mirror's line.

Aoi looked at her own hand, holding the keys, in the torchlight, in her mother's story.

Her grandmother had carried the chest out of Yanaka. It had passed to Aoi's mother. It had
passed, somehow, to someone, and it had come back to the room, and Aoi was holding the keys to
the house where the room had been.

She understood that she had been carrying the chest her whole life without knowing it, in the
only form a thing can survive when no one will speak its name: as a fact in a family that had
been turned into a story, and then into a silence, and then into nothing at all.

woman's silhouette moving behind it; the lane behind Yanaka Ginza wet and empty; a tall,
cloth-wrapped bundle, mirror-shaped, glimpsed in the porch.]
