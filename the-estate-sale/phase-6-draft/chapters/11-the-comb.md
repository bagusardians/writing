# The Comb

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The comb had been mended in 1991 and the woman who owned it had died in 1991, and Mikami
had known both facts for thirty-three years without ever putting them in the same sentence,
which is the way a person carries a thing they do not want to look at.

He opened the ticket drawer on a Tuesday.

Aoi was not with him. He had not told her he kept the tickets. It was one thing to read a
ledger and another to have your own name in it, in your own hand, in a drawer you have
opened every week for thirty years without reading.

string, stacked; the camera isolates one ticket, dated 1991, for a lacquer comb, with the
owner's surname legible as HANA in a small upright hand.]

---

Mikami had mended for thirty-eight years and he had a rule, which was that he wrote a name
on every ticket. He wrote the name of whoever brought the thing, because a thing without a
name is a thing you cannot return, and the whole trade of mending is returning. He had
tickets going back to 1986. He could open the drawer and read the last four decades of
Sendagi in his own hand.

He had never once looked for Mitsuko's name in it. He had looked for it that morning, and
found it twenty-two times, and found beside it, on one ticket from 1991, a name he had not
thought about in thirty-three years: Hana.

Not a customer's name he remembered. A name in the same hand as the tags, which meant the
ticket had been written not by him but by the person who brought the comb, and left, and
returned for it, and died.

He called Aoi. He said: "Your grandmother's name was Hana."

Aoi was quiet. She had known the name for six days, since the album, and had not said it,
because saying it would make it real.

"She brought the comb to me in 1991," Mikami said. "She paid in cash. She asked me to fix
the tooth. She asked me to write nothing on the ticket. I wrote HANA because I always
write a name; it's how I know a job is done."

"And the owner died."

"The week I gave it back. Hana brought it in and Hana came for it, and a week later the woman
it belonged to was dead, and Hana was the one who had to carry it back to her. That's what the
comb is. It's a thing Hana kept for someone who was going to die."

Aoi asked whether Hana had seemed like a woman in mourning.

"No," Mikami said. "She seemed like a woman doing a job. That's worse."

[IMAGE 11.2 — a lacquer comb on a bench, restored, one tooth replaced in slightly different
lacquer; beside it, a receipt book open to a page where the name HANA appears in pencil, in
the workshop's own hand.]

---

Aoi asked him the question she had been circling for a week.

"Why did she bring it here. Why your shop, in Sendagi, out of all of Tokyo."

Mikami had thought about that for thirty-three years, on and off, the way you think about a
draft in a room. He said: "Because your mother lived in Sendagi then. With Hana. Three
streets from me. I didn't know. I didn't know any of it, then."

Aoi did the arithmetic. If her mother lived in Sendagi with Hana in 1991 — the year the comb
was returned, the year its owner died — then her mother had been a child in that flat on the
street Mikami walked to work for thirty years, and Hana had been raising her there, and the
whole of it had been three streets from the shop the whole time.

"They knew where to look," Mikami said. "That's what frightens me. Whoever wrote those tags
knew where everyone would be. She knew where I was. She knew where your family was."

Aoi did not answer. She was looking at the comb, and she had begun to see a thing about the
trade that Mikami, who was a mender and not an appraiser, had never had to see: a collection
of two hundred and eleven objects is also a collection of two hundred and eleven addresses.
Every name on a tag was a place someone lived, in a year, and the places were all within a
few kilometres, and the years had been chosen, one by one, so that no two people who held a
single object's parts ever met.

Except in that estate sale. Except for the thirty-one buyers. Except for the four who died.

"She didn't just reconstruct a room," Aoi said. "She reconstructed a neighbourhood. Sixty
years of it. She put every witness in a place where the others couldn't reach them, and
then she waited, and then she sold it all at once, and the room met itself."

timber row-house to a small apartment block three streets away; a date, 1991, and two
names, MIKAMI and HANA, at either end.]

---

He took the comb out of the drawer and turned it over.

On the back of the comb, in the same hand, small and upright, there was a tag tied with
cotton thread. It was the only tag among the two hundred he had never seen, because it had
never been in the sale.

It read: *MIKAMI — 1991 — SENDAGI.*

The date was not the date he mended it. It was the date the owner died.

"I've been tagged," Mikami said, and set the comb down very carefully, as if the bench
might take his name off it if he moved too fast.

Aoi picked up the comb. She held it the way she held everything, at the edge, as if it were
already priced, and she read the tag, and then she read the back of it, where there was one
line in pencil.

It said: *he kept the ticket. he will keep the room.*

Mikami saw it and put both hands on the bench and stood there for a while, the way a man
stands when he has been told the date of something and has decided not to argue with it.

"She knew I'd keep it," he said. "She knew I'd keep every ticket. She knew I'd keep the
ledger. She wasn't tagging me to kill me. She was tagging me to file me. I'm not a victim."

"You're a record," Aoi said.

"I'm a record," he agreed, and there was something in his voice that Aoi had not heard
before and would hear only once more, in Kuramae, at the end, which was the sound of a man
who has just discovered that his whole careful life was a piece of evidence someone else
was relying on.

[IMAGE 11.4 — the underside of the comb box: a label pasted inside, in the small upright
hand, listing eleven dates between 1991 and 2023, each with a name; the last line is blank
except for a place — SENDAGI — and no date.]


---

Aoi went to Sendagi alone the next week, to the address the comb had come from, because Mikami
had given it to her and because a comb with a name on it is a place as much as a thing.

The address was a small apartment block three streets from Mikami's shop, and it had been
rebuilt since 1991, and the woman in the ground-floor flat had lived there for eleven years
and knew nothing. But the caretaker was old, and he remembered, because caretakers do.

He remembered a household in the nineties: a grandmother and a daughter and, for a while, a
small girl. He remembered that the grandmother was a seamstress and the daughter worked at a
laundry in Tateishi and the small girl used to play in the stairwell. He remembered — this was
the thing Aoi had come for — that a woman used to visit, an older woman, well-dressed, once
or twice a year, and would sit with the grandmother in the ground-floor flat, and the
grandmother would not let the small girl in, and after the visits the grandmother would be
quiet for a week.

Aoi asked whether he remembered the visitor's name.

The caretaker said he did not, but that the visitor always left with a wrapped bundle, and
that it was tall, about the height of a mirror.

Aoi stood in the stairwell of a rebuilt apartment block in Sendagi and understood that the
room had not found her at the estate sale. The room had found her twenty years earlier, in
this stairwell, when she was nine, when the visitor came and sat with her grandmother and
left with the mirror, and she had been inside the room's route her whole life, and the estate
sale had only been the moment the route named her.

[IMAGE 11.5 — a stairwell in a small Sendagi apartment block: worn stone steps, a handrail;
on the landing, a child's drawing in chalk, faded; a caretaker's door ajar; light from a
high window.]


---

Mikami went to the dry cleaner's in Nezu in December, because Aoi had told him about the
receipt, and because a man who has kept every ticket for thirty-eight years wanted to see the
shop that had kept a receipt for twenty.

The shop was small and old and the daughter of the man who wrote the receipt was behind the
counter, and she remembered the mirror cover, and she remembered the woman who brought it.

She remembered one thing that no document had. She said the woman always came on the same day,
the sixteenth, and she always paid in cash, and she always asked the same question before she
left. The question was not about the cleaning.

She asked, every time, for twenty years: "Is there any thread that will hold a tag that has
been cut off?"

The daughter's father had never known what to say. He had said what a dry cleaner says, which
is that you can sew a tag back on, and the woman had always nodded and taken the cover and
left, unsatisfied, and come back the next year and asked again.

Mikami stood in the shop and understood the question and the account at the same time. The
woman was not asking about the mirror. She was asking whether a thing that has been *severed*
from its record can be *reattached* — whether a tag cut off in 1958 can be sewn back on sixty
years later — and the answer, in the dry-cleaning trade as in the room, is the same: you can
sew it back, but the thread will always show, and the mark of the cut will always be there,
and that is what a repair is.

He told the daughter what the tag had said, and she wrote it down, and gave it to him, and it
is the only copy of the mirror cover's tag that survives: a name, a year, a place, in a small
upright hand, sewn into the lining of a cloth cover, cut off and sewn back on, twenty times
over, and never once thrown away.
