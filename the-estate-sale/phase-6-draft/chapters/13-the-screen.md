# The Screen

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The screen was six panels and one of them was not the same age as the others, and Yamamura
noticed that in the first minute, because noticing is what he did.

He was a restorer of painted screens and sliding doors, in a workshop in Kagurazaka, and he
had been given the screen by a dealer who had bought it at the Kanzaki estate and could not
sell it, because the tag on it named a family that had gone quiet, and because the fifth
panel had been replaced.

Tag: *KURODA — 1958 — YANAKA*. The screen was old, late Edo, a six-panel byōbu, gold leaf
and ink, a scene of a river and a bridge. The first four panels and the sixth were original.
The fifth was a later replacement, plain paper, darkened, with no painting on it at all.

A person does not replace one panel of a screen with a blank. A person replaces a panel
because the panel was *damaged*, and a repaired panel is repainted. A blank panel means the
panel was replaced and the painting was never restored, which means whoever owned it wanted
the screen whole but did not want the fifth panel to show anything.

[IMAGE 17.1 — a six-panel folding screen in a workshop, lit from the side: five panels
painted with a river and bridge scene in gold and ink; the fifth panel is blank, later paper,
visibly a different age; a small tag hangs from the frame.]

---

Yamamura laid the screen flat and looked at the blank panel under raking light.

There was a stain on it. A large one, dark, in the centre of the panel, the shape of a hand
if a hand had been pressed there and dragged. It had soaked into the paper and the paper had
been replaced over it, but the stain had bled through the new paper from behind, faintly, the
way a mark does when it is in the wood and not the leaf.

He knew what the mark was. He was fifty-three and he had restored screens for thirty years
and he had seen the mark three times, always on a panel that had been replaced without
repainting, and every time it had been a death.

Someone had bled on the fifth panel of the Kuroda screen in Yanaka in 1958, and someone had
replaced the panel and never painted it, and the screen had been kept by the Kanzakis for
sixty-five years with a blank fifth panel, and Mitsuko had bought it back and tagged it, and
it had come to Yamamura, who was now looking at the mark of a man's death on a screen that
was supposed to be a river.

Yamamura called the estate. He got Kei, who was tired, and who said the screen had been in
the storage room his whole childhood and that he had been told never to open it, and that he
had opened it once, at twelve, and had seen the blank panel, and had been beaten for it.

"Beaten," Yamamura said.

"Not beaten," Kei said. "Held. She held me by the arm and made me look at it for an hour.
She said, 'Now you've seen it, you carry it.' I was twelve. I didn't understand."

[IMAGE 17.2 — a raking-light detail of the blank fifth panel: the faint dark stain bleeding
through later paper from behind, roughly the shape of a pressed and dragged hand; the paper
grain is visible; a scale rule lies at the edge.]

---

Yamamura did the thing a restorer does, which is to look at the back.

He lifted the paper from the frame — carefully, because paper remembers — and on the reverse
of the blank panel, under the old paper, in a hand he did not know, there was writing.

Brush, small, and it had been written on the *back* of the panel, so that it would not be
seen from the room, only from behind, only by someone who took the screen apart.

It said: *he fell against the panel. I did not move. I was behind the chest. I did not move
until they carried me out. Do not repaint this panel. Let it stay blank. The blank is the only
honest thing in this house.*

Yamamura sat back.

The screen had stood between the room and the corridor. It was a wall, and a screen is a wall
you can fold, and the fifth panel had caught the dying man, and a child behind the chest had
watched him fall against it, and had not moved, and had written, at some point, a note to
herself on the back of the panel, in the one place no one would ever look.

And then, in the margin, smaller, in a hand that had steadied with age:

*the river on the front was painted by my father. he painted it the year I was born. I have
kept the screen so the river stays next to the blank, and so that the last thing the room
held is the thing I look at. I am not afraid of it. I am keeping it company.*

[IMAGE 17.3 — the reverse of the detached fifth panel: a brush-written note on the raw back
of the panel, dark ink, in a careful hand, with a smaller marginal line in an older,
steadier hand; the panel rests on a workbench beside its frame.]

---

Yamamura called Aoi, because the estate had given him her number, and because a restorer who
finds a note like that does not keep it.

He read it to her. She asked him to read the marginal line again, and then she was quiet for
a long time.

"She wasn't building a shrine," Aoi said at last. "She was sitting with it. The room, the
screen, the blank panel — she wasn't preserving a crime. She was keeping a vigil. Sixty years
of it. The tags aren't evidence. They're company."

"The tags are names," Yamamura said.

"The names are the people who were in the room with her," Aoi said. "She's been keeping them
together. That's why they can't all be dead. If they're dead, she's alone in it."

Yamamura looked at the screen, the river on five panels and the blank on the sixth, and
understood the thing that Aoi had understood at the temple, which was that the whole
collection was an act of loyalty to a room that no one else had stayed in.

"She kept the room so she wouldn't have to be the only one who saw it," he said.

"Yes," Aoi said. "And someone is killing the others, one by one, so that she will be."

---

Yamamura did not repaint the panel. He reattached the paper, and set the fifth panel back the
way it was, and stood the screen against the wall of his workshop, the river facing the room.

He went home. In the morning his workshop door was open, though he had locked it, and the
screen was gone, and on the workbench, where the screen had been, there was a single tag, in
the small upright hand, and it said his name and his district and the year, and beneath it, in
the steadier hand, one line:

*you read the back. thank you. but the river has to face the wall now, because the last
person who is going to see the blank is almost here.*

Yamamura stood in his open workshop and understood, as Kubo had understood, and as Hoshino had
understood, that he had been filed — that reading the back of a panel is a way of being
counted, and that the room had been counting readers, not owners, all along.

[IMAGE 17.4 — a workshop at dawn: the space where the screen stood is empty; on the
workbench, a single white tag with a name and a date; the door is open onto an empty
Kagurazaka street; a cup of tea has gone cold.]


---

Aoi went to Kagurazaka after the screen was taken, because Yamamura had called her, and because
she wanted to see the empty place where a screen had stood.

Yamamura showed her the workbench, the tag, the open door. He said he had called the police
and that they had taken a statement and that nothing would come of it, because nothing had
been stolen except a screen, and because a screen that leaves a workshop by itself is not a
crime the police can write down.

Then he told her the thing he had not told the police, because it was not a fact, and Yamamura
was a man of facts.

He said that in the night, before the screen was gone, he had dreamed of the room. He had
never seen the room. He had seen only the fifth panel, the blank one, and the stain. But in
the dream he was lying on the floor of a six-mat room, low, at the height of a child, and the
room was full of objects, and through the doorway a woman was standing, and she was not
looking at him.

She was counting.

He said she counted to twelve and then she stopped, and she looked at the place where he was
lying, and she said, without moving her lips, a number that was not twelve.

"The thirteenth," Yamamura said. "She said thirteen. And then I woke, and the screen was
gone."

Aoi stood in the empty workshop and looked at the place against the wall where the screen had
stood, and thought about the fact that the room had begun to give the same dream to everyone
who read it, and that the dream was not a warning. It was a *census*.

[IMAGE 13.5 — an empty workshop wall in Kagurazaka; a single tag on the workbench; a teacup;
the door open onto the street; on the wall, a faint rectangular discolouration where a screen
stood for years.]


---

Aoi went back to the Kanzaki house in December, alone, with the keys from the hook, and she
took the folder and the tape measure and a torch, because she had understood, by then, that the
house was the last page of the account she had not read.

The house was empty. The wall was there, replastered. She put her hand on it and it was cold in
a rectangle. She did not open it, because the room behind it was gone; the storage room had
been emptied in April and the arrangement had moved to Kuramae, and what was left in the house
was the *door*, which is all a room leaves when it moves.

She went through the front rooms with the torch. She found, in the back of a cupboard, a
single object Kei had missed: a wooden box, small, with a lid, containing a set of *paper
patterns*.

Aoi spread them on the floor. They were floor plans. Hand-drawn, in the small upright hand, on
tracing paper, dozens of them, dated across sixty years, and each was an attempt to draw the
room.

None of them matched. Not two of the sixty matched each other. Each was the room, drawn from
memory, in a different year, by a person trying to remember the dimensions of a space she had
seen once, at eleven, from behind a chest — and none of them was right, and she had known none
of them was right, and she had kept trying for sixty years because *she could not remember and
she would not guess*.

The last plan, dated 2019, was the closest. It was nearly seven by twelve, and the gap was set
back toward the corner, and in the corner there was a small rectangle labelled *tansu*. Beside
it, in pencil, in the small upright hand, a note: *still not right. it is not the plan. it is
the seeing. I will have to stand in it again.*

Aoi sat on the floor of an empty house in Yanaka with sixty wrong plans and understood the whole
of the collection at last. Mitsuko had not been trying to *remember* the room. She had been
trying to *reproduce the view*, and a view cannot be drawn from memory; it can only be measured
from the position, and the position was the gap, and the gap was for a person, and the person
had to be someone who could read what the view contained.

The plans were not plans. They were sixty years of a child trying to get back behind the chest,
and failing, because the chest was gone, and the only way back was to build the view and stand
in it, and to do that you need someone to stand in the gap.
