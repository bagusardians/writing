# The Keys

*Live POV. Present tense.*

---

The keys are on a ring the way a janitor keeps them, oldest in front, and the oldest is
brass and worn smooth, and I am holding it up to the door of a room that is not on the plan
of this house.

I am a locksmith. That is not a confession; it is a skill. I am here because a client in
Yanaka called and said there is a room in my house I have never been able to open, and I am
here because I have been doing this for twenty-two years and a room you cannot open is the
only interesting thing left in the trade.

The client is behind me. He is polite and he smells of money. He has not told me his name.

The house is old, a timber row-house off a lane behind Yanaka Ginza. The plan the client
gave me shows a storage room off the kitchen. The wall where the storage room should be is
a wall. There is no door in it, and no seam, and when I put my palm on it the plaster is
cold in one long rectangle the size of a doorway, as if the cold remembers where the hole
used to be.

[IMAGE 6.1 — photograph of a narrow timber passage in a Yanaka row-house: a plaster wall,
a hand pressed flat against it; beside the wall, a floor plan drawn by hand with a storage
room marked, and the wall on the plan crossed out in ink.]

---

I have opened a thousand doors and I can tell you that a wall is not a door.

That is the first thing they teach you. A wall is a wall; a door is a hole with a lid. You
cannot pick a wall, and if a client tells you a wall is a door, the client is confused, or
the client is lying, or the client has lived in a house a long time and has begun to
remember a version of it that was never built.

I told him this. I said the plan and the wall did not agree, and that plans are often
wrong, and that the honest thing was to call an architect, not a locksmith.

He said, "Try the keys."

I said the keys were keys to other doors, presumably, and he said, "Try them in order. I
want to see which one fits."

I want to record that I knew, even then, that this was not a locksmithing job. But a client
with money and a story and a ring of old keys is the whole of my trade, and I have a
curiosity that has cost me, over the years, exactly nothing, until today.

So I tried the keys, in order, oldest first, and I want the reader to understand what that
is: a man standing alone in a cold passage of an empty house, pressing key after key
against a wall that has no hole, and finding, each time, nothing. Twenty-two keys. Nothing.

Then the twenty-third.

---

The key went in.

I want to describe this exactly, because I have described it to the police and to my wife
and to a man in a bar, and every time I describe it I lose something, so I will write it
here while it is intact.

The key went into the plaster where there was no hole. I felt the wards turn — that small
resistance, the give, the click that is the whole of my life and the only sound I love. And
the wall, which had no seam, opened inward on a hinge I could not see, and the cold came
out of it like a held breath.

Behind it was a room of six mats with no windows.

The walls were lined, floor to ceiling, with objects, each with a tag. Lacquerware, a
mirror, a coat on a hook, boxes, fans, a tea set, a doll, a comb, dozens and dozens of
things, and every one of them tagged in the same small upright hand, and every one of them
placed, not stored — placed, like a room that someone had left and locked and intended to
come back to.

And in the middle of the floor there was a place where a person was meant to stand.

I know how that sounds. A mat is four surfaces. There are no places, in a mat room, where a
person is meant to stand. But I understood it the instant I looked in — the objects were
arranged around a gap, and the gap was the size of a standing person, and the objects all
faced it, the way furniture faces a bed, or a stage, or a grave.

[IMAGE 6.2 — the interior of the room: walls lined floor-to-ceiling with tagged objects
(mirror, coat, boxes, tea set, doll, combs); in the centre of the six mats, an empty
standing-place; the doorway's light falls on the floor in a long rectangle.]

---

I said, "There's a room."

The client did not answer.

I said it again, and turned, and he was closer than he had been. He had taken off his
shoes, which I had not heard him do, and he was standing at the threshold with his hands
folded, looking past me at the room the way a man looks at a house he has bought and not
yet entered.

I am going to do the thing I have trained my whole life not to do: I am going to step
backward out of a strange room.

The client stepped forward. I stepped back. He said my name — my actual name, not the name
on the job sheet — and I understood that the call had not come from a client, and that the
house had not been on the market, and that the ring of keys had been waiting on a hook by
the front door, all twenty-three, exactly as I found them.

"You found it," he says, pleased. "Good. I couldn't, when it was my turn."

"The turn," I say.

"Everyone gets a turn," he says. "Yours is the keys."

I do not know what that means, and I am not going to find out, because he is reaching past
me now — his arm, past my shoulder, to the inside of the door — and the oldest key, the
brass one, is still in the lock, and he takes it.

He steps back into the passage. He says, kindly, the way you speak to a man who has done a
small job well:

"Thank you. You can go."

The door closes.

The key turns from the outside.

[IMAGE 6.3 — from inside the room: the closing door, a slice of corridor light narrowing;
a man's hand at the lock; beyond him, in the corridor, the client's folded hands; on the
wall beside the door, tags beginning to catch the last light.]

---

I want to tell the reader what the room does, because a locked room is not, as it turns
out, a puzzle. It is a place.

The first thing it does is go dark, and then the dark stops being dark, and you can see
again, by no light you can name, and the objects on the walls are all around you and they
are all looking at the gap where you are standing, which is the gap you have walked into,
which means the gap was for you.

The second thing it does is get smaller. Not the walls; the room. The six mats contract.
You can watch the mat edges move, in the way you can watch a clock tick if you stare, until
the room is the size of a person, and then the person is the room.

I am still holding the ring of keys. Twenty-two of them are useless and one of them is
gone, and I am talking aloud, because if I stop talking I will have to listen, and I have
begun to hear the thing I would have to listen to: not a noise. A count. Something in the
room is counting, and it is counting the objects, and it has not finished, and I understand
that when it finishes, the count will be the number of people the room is for, and I am one
of them, and the gap I am standing in was always the last entry.

My hand is slower than it was. The keys in my fist have gone cold, all of them, at once,
like a fist of water.

I am writing this on the phone, and the screen is the only light that is not the other
light, and the phone shows the room the way it was when the door opened: six mats, walls of
tagged things, and in the centre, me.

I am in the photograph. That is the thing. I am in the photograph now.

The client, on the other side of the door, says through the wood:

"Read the tag on the mirror when you can. It'll explain the rest. It explains all of you."

Then I hear him walk away down the passage, unhurried, and I hear the front door of the
house open, and close, and lock, and after that there is nothing in the house but the
counting, and the objects, and the gap, which is shrinking.

[IMAGE 6.4 — the phone's screen in the dark: a live camera view of the six-mat room from
where the narrator stands; the walls tagged; the gap in the centre now occupied by the
narrator's own silhouette; the doorway behind is closed.]


---

I want to describe what the room counted, because I have begun to understand that the
counting is the whole of the account and not a detail of it.

It counted the objects. That was first. It went around the walls in a set order — I could hear
the order, because the count came from each object in turn, like a person touching each thing
and saying its name — and the order was not the order of the walls. It was the order of the
twelve.

Twelve names. The twelve names on the tags. And as the count passed each object, I understood,
in the way you understand a filing system, that each object was one of the twelve, filed
under a name, and that the twelve had been in the room together in 1958 and had not been
together since, and that the count was putting them back.

Then the count reached the end of the twelve and started again, and the second pass was
different.

The second pass counted *me*.

I am standing in the gap. The gap is the thirteenth. The count went to twelve and then it
reached me, and the number it said was not a name, because I do not have a name in this room.
The number it said was a *number*. Thirteen. And when it said thirteen, everything in the room
was quiet, and stayed quiet, and I understood that a counting that reaches thirteen has
finished, and that a finished count is a kind of permission.

The client, through the door, said something I could not hear.

I am writing this in the gap, with the phone at six percent, and I am going to write until the
count does whatever a finished count does, and I want whoever reads this to know that I did
not run, not because I was brave, but because a locksmith knows a thing about doors that no
one else knows: a door that has been opened from the outside is not, by the time you are on
the inside of it, a door any more. It is a wall with a history. And you do not run at walls.

[IMAGE 6.5 — the phone screen in the dark, low battery: a notes app open, the text of a first-
person account; behind the screen, in the room, the walls of tagged objects are in shadow; one
blank tag lies on the mat in front of the narrator's feet.]


---

I have to keep writing while I can, because the count is in me now and I can feel it moving the
way you feel a price settle.

I want to say one thing about locks, because I am a locksmith and this is my only chance, and
because the thing I know about locks is the thing the room is.

A lock is not a secret. A lock is a *promise*, and the promise is: this door will hold until
someone who knows how it works comes along. Every lock in the world is defeated the same way,
which is not force but *patience* and *the right shape*. You do not break a lock. You find the
shape it was made to admit, and you become that shape, and it opens, and it thanks you.

The room is a lock. That is what I have understood in the gap. It was made to admit a shape,
and the shape is a person who reads, and the room has been waiting sixty years for the right
shape, and every person who touched a tagged thing was a *key* — tried, and some tried, and
some turned partway. The dead were keys that broke. The filed were keys that fit a false
tumbril and held. And the last key, the one the lock was cut for, is the one who can read the
whole room, and that is not a locksmith and it is not a dealer.

It is a person who prices the dead for strangers, because that person has learned, over eleven
thousand flat, to hold a thing without wanting it, and to read the history of a thing without
needing to be in the story, and that is the only shape the room was ever cut to admit.

I am not the key. I have known that since the door closed. I was the *pick* — the one used to
test the shape, the one that fits a moment and then is set down. And I am writing this so that
whoever comes next knows what the lock is, and does not mistake the pick for the key.
