# The Comb

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The comb had been mended in 1991 and the woman who owned it had died in 1991, and Mikami
had known both facts for thirty-three years without ever putting them in the same sentence,
which is the way a person carries a thing they do not want to look at.

He opened the ticket drawer on a Tuesday.

Aoi was not with him. He had not told her he kept the tickets. It was one thing to read a
ledger and another to have your own name in it, in your own hand, in a drawer you have
opened every week for thirty years without reading.

[IMAGE 10.1 — a workshop drawer open to the light: hundreds of repair tickets on loops of
string, stacked; the camera isolates one ticket, dated 1991, for a lacquer comb, with the
owner's surname legible as HANA in a small upright hand.]

Hana. Not a customer's name he remembered. A name in the same hand as the tags, which meant
the ticket had been written not by him but by the person who brought the comb, and left,
and returned for it, and died.

He called Aoi. He said: "Your grandmother's name was Hana."

Aoi was quiet. She had known the name for six days, since the album, and had not said it,
because saying it would make it real.

"She brought the comb to me in 1991," Mikami said. "She paid in cash. She asked me to fix
the tooth. She asked me to write nothing on the ticket. I wrote HANA because I always
write a name; it's how I know a job is done."

"And she died."

"The week I gave it back."

[IMAGE 10.2 — a lacquer comb on a bench, restored, one tooth replaced in slightly different
lacquer; beside it, a receipt book open to a page where the name HANA appears in pencil, in
the workshop's own hand.]

Aoi asked him the question she had been circling for a week.

"Why did she bring it here. Why your shop, in Sendagi, out of all of Tokyo."

Mikami had thought about that for thirty-three years, on and off, the way you think about a
draft in a room. He said: "Because your mother lived in Sendagi then. With Hana. Three
streets from me. I didn't know. I didn't know any of it, then."

Aoi did the arithmetic. If Hana died in 1991 and Aoi's mother lived in Sendagi with her in
1991, then Aoi's mother had been there when the comb was returned. When Hana died. On the
street Mikami walked to work for thirty years.

"They knew where to look," Mikami said. "That's what frightens me. Whoever wrote those tags
knew where everyone would be. She knew where I was. She knew where your family was."

[IMAGE 10.3 — a street map of Sendagi and Yanaka with a route drawn in pencil: from a
timber row-house to a small apartment block three streets away; a date, 1991, and two
names, MIKAMI and HANA, at either end.]

He took the comb out of the drawer and turned it over.

On the back of the comb, in the same hand, small and upright, there was a tag tied with
cotton thread. It was the only tag among the two hundred he had never seen, because it had
never been in the sale.

It read: *MIKAMI — 1991 — SENDAGI.*

The date was not the date he mended it. It was the date the owner died.

"I've been tagged," Mikami said, and set the comb down very carefully, as if the bench
might take his name off it if he moved too fast.
