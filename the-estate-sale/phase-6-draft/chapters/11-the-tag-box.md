# The Tag Box

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The tag box was in Setagaya, on a folding table at Bōro-ichi, in December, among the
kitchen knives and the dead clocks, and it was Aoi who found it, because she had by then
trained her eye to see one thing in a market of ten thousand: a small upright hand.

Nineteen loose tags in a lacquered box, unused, blank on the front. On the back of each,
in pencil, a name and a date, and the dates were not 1958.

They were this year.

[IMAGE 12.1 — a flea-market table at Bōro-ichi, December: a lacquered box open among
knives, clocks, and ceramics; inside, a stack of small blank inventory tags, string still
coiled; one tag is lifted slightly, showing pencil on its reverse.]

Aoi bought the box for three hundred yen and did not open it again until she was home in
Tateishi, with the door locked, which she had started doing in October without saying why.

She laid the tags out on the low table. Nineteen. On the reverse, the names.

KANZAKI KEI — this year.
SHIRAI AOI — this year.
MIKAMI TORU — this year.
And sixteen others, all living, all tagged, all with dates still to come.

She knew then what the tags were. They were not a list of the dead. They were a list of
the living, and every tag that had a *past* date on the front had once had a *future* date
on the back, and the two hundred lots of the estate were, all of them, the completed
versions of these.

The collection was not a memory. It was a schedule.

[IMAGE 12.2 — a diagram: a two-column chart, drawn by hand. Left column: the twelve 1958
names and their front dates. Right column: the nineteen living names and the blurry
future dates on the reverse. Arrows connect a 1958 name to a living name, showing the
tags pair the dead with the living in a chain.]

There was one tag with no name at all. It was in the bottom of the box, older than the
others, the pencil so faint it had to be held to the lamp, and it carried only a date and a
place and a symbol Aoi had seen exactly once before, in the interior lid of a music box:
a small drawing of a tansu.

Place: YANAKA. Date: 1958. No name.

That was the first tag. That was the tag Kanzaki Mitsuko had made for herself, at
eleven, for the person she had been in that room, and had never been able to write a name
on, because she did not know whose name it should be.

Aoi put the tags back in the box. She understood, finally, that she had been assembling the
collection all along — every object she had held, every case she had chased, had been a
piece moving toward the warehouse in Kuramae, where two hundred lots waited in a room of
six mats.

She called Mikami. Her voice, she noticed, did not shake, and that frightened her more than
the tags.

"Bring a tape measure," she said. "We're going to Kuramae."
