# The Album

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The album was not in the sale. That is what made Suzuki keep it.

Everything else from the Kanzaki house had a tag. The album had none, and it was the only
thing in the whole estate that the dead woman had not priced, and Suzuki had bought it for
two hundred yen because he collected photographs of old rooms and because an untagged thing
in a tagged house is a hole in the record.

He was a proofreader. He spent his days finding the one wrong word in a page, and he
recognised the feeling of a document with something missing.

[IMAGE 08.1 — an open photograph album on a low table: black-and-white prints corner-mounted
on card; the album's board cover is water-stained; a slip of paper between two pages is
blank.]

---

Suzuki had collected photographs of rooms for nine years. He was not interested in people.
He was interested in wallpaper, and mat edges, and the way a household arranges a shelf,
which is the most honest document a family ever produces, because no one thinks anyone is
reading.

He had four hundred photographs. He kept them in boxes, by prefecture. He could date a
photograph to within five years by the height of the light switch and the shape of the
door handles.

The album broke his system. It was fifty prints of the same room, and the room was not in
any Tokyo he knew. The prints had been taken over twenty-seven years, from 1962 to 1989,
and in every one the room was the same: six mats, no window, walls lined with objects. What
changed was the objects. In 1962 there were four. In 1989 there were dozens.

That is not a room. That is a collection. And the collection grew exactly the way a
collection grows when a person is buying one thing at a time and placing it, not storing
it, for a purpose.

[IMAGE 08.2 — a single album page: three photographs of the same six-mat, windowless room,
taken from slightly different angles; the objects on the walls differ between the three
photographs.]

---

Suzuki laid the prints in order, on his own kitchen table, and he did what a proofreader
does, which is to set up a comparison and wait to be told he is wrong.

He was told he was wrong within an hour.

The camera did not move. Across twenty-seven years, fifty prints, the viewpoint was
identical — same corner, same height, same angle. A collection photographed by its owner
over decades would drift; a person stands in different places on different days. This was
not drift. This was a fixed point.

Suzuki measured it. He was a proofreader; he had a ruler, and a good one, and he counted
the mats in the earliest print: six, standard, and he knew the standard dimensions because
he had spent nine years photographing rooms.

On the earliest print, the horizon of the floor cut the frame at a particular height, and
that height, against the six mats he could count, put the camera between forty and fifty
centimetres above the tatami.

A child of eleven, sitting behind a tansu, looking into a room she was not supposed to see.

[IMAGE 08.3 — a hand-annotated contact sheet: the same print reproduced, with ruled overlay
lines and a handwritten calculation in the margin dating the camera height to a child's eye
level; the tansu is circled.]

Suzuki sat back and looked at his kitchen, and then at the photographs, and then at his
kitchen again.

An adult does not photograph her own collection from forty-five centimetres for
twenty-seven years. If the photographer was the collector, the collector was a child. If
the collector was a child in 1962, she was born around 1951 — which made her eleven, if the
room dates from 1958, and eleven is exactly the age at which a child hides behind the
tansu, and exactly the age at which the tag list in the ledger begins.

Suzuki had not seen the ledger. He did not need to. The album was the ledger's other side:
one recorded what she bought, the other recorded what it looked like from the floor.

---

There was one photograph that was not from 1962 or from 1989. It was loose at the back, and
it had been folded and unfolded many times, and it showed the room the way it looked on a
night, before it was emptied: the objects gone, the walls bare, and in the doorway, two
people, and on the floor between them, a man who was not standing.

Suzuki turned the print over. On the back, in the hand he had spent three days learning:

*He was going to. I stopped him. Do not look for the blame in her.*

[IMAGE 08.4 — the folded 1958 print: a bare six-mat room; a man on the floor; in the doorway,
the blurred figure of an adult and, behind her, partially hidden, the small outline of a
child; the print is visibly creased and repaired with tape.]

Suzuki read those two lines perhaps twenty times. He was a proofreader. He could not stop
himself from doing what he did with every sentence: he looked for the wrong word.

The wrong word was *her*.

*I stopped him*, she wrote. Not *we*, and not *my mother*. *I* stopped him. And then: *do
not look for the blame in her.* Which is what you write when you have taken the blame
yourself, or when you are trying to move it.

Suzuki had spent his life finding the one wrong word. He found it, and it told him that a
child of eleven had written a note on the back of a photograph of a dead man, and had kept
it, and had folded and unfolded it for sixty years, and had been buying the room back ever
since.

He did the thing that he has never been able to explain to himself. He photographed the
album, all fifty prints, in order, and he photographed the note, and he put the photographs
in an envelope, and he addressed the envelope, in his careful proofreader's hand, to the
one name he had found in the sale that seemed to be alive and looking for an answer: the
estate buyer, Shirai Aoi, whom he had met once, at the Kanzaki hall, when she priced a
cabinet and he stood behind her and said nothing.

He never posted it. He bought a stamp, and he put the envelope on the shelf by the door,
and he told himself he would post it in the morning.

The morning, when it came, was not one he was able to use.

[IMAGE 08.5 — a proofreader's flat at night: an envelope on a shelf by the door, stamped; on
the table behind it, the fifty prints laid out in a grid; the camera's viewpoint print is
centred and circled; a chair is pushed back from the table.]


---

Aoi went to see Suzuki's flat in November, after the envelope, and the neighbour let her in
because Aoi said she was a colleague and because neighbours in Nezu are kind to people who
ask about the dead.

The flat was small and clean and had four hundred photographs in boxes, sorted by prefecture.
The police had taken the album. They had taken the loose 1958 print and the note. They had not
taken the boxes, because four hundred photographs of other people's rooms are not, on their
face, evidence.

Aoi went through them anyway, because she had learned by then that the thing the police take
and the thing that matters are often two different objects.

She found it in the Shizuoka box, at the back, where it should not have been, because Suzuki
sorted by prefecture and a Tokyo room does not belong in Shizuoka.

It was a photograph of a woman in a doorway. Young, maybe nineteen, in work clothes, standing
in the doorway of a house she did not own, and beside her on the floor, wrapped in cloth and
tall, something the height of a standing mirror.

On the back, in Suzuki's precise proofreader's hand, the one line he had written before he
died:

*This is the only photograph he kept of his mother. I asked him why there were no others. He
said she kept the mirror instead.*

Aoi sat in a dead man's flat in Nezu with a photograph of a servant in a doorway and
understood, at last, whose grandmother the nurse next door had been.

[IMAGE 08.6 — a photograph held in two hands: a young woman in the doorway of an old house,
work clothes, no smile; beside her a cloth-wrapped bundle the height of a mirror; on the
reverse edge of the print, a line in a careful hand.]


---

Aoi wrote to Shizuoka, to Suzuki's family, because a proofreader who found the one wrong word
deserves a letter even if he cannot receive it, and because Aoi had begun to write to the dead
as a way of keeping the account straight.

The family wrote back. They were kind and confused. They said Suzuki had been a quiet man who
photographed rooms and had never married and had called his mother once a week until she died
in 2019. They said he had seemed, in the last year, to be working on something, but that he had
never said what, and that they had found, among his things, one box labelled in his careful
hand with a single word.

The word was *UNREAD*.

The family did not know what it meant and had thrown the box in the move, and Aoi asked, in the
letter, whether anything from the box had survived, and they said no, and then, in a second
letter, they said actually yes, one thing, because it had been on the shelf not in the box: a
single sheet of paper, ruled, on which Suzuki had written a list.

They copied it out and sent it to her. It was a list of ten sentences, and each sentence was a
thing Suzuki had read in the Kanzaki documents and could not unread, and he had written them
down in order of how much they frightened him, and the last one, at the bottom, in the most
careful hand in the whole account, was this:

*The room has a place for the reader. I am a reader. I have not gone to Kuramae. I will not go
to Kuramae. But I have read the room, and the room knows.*

Suzuki had known, three weeks before he died. He had known and had written it down and had not
gone, and it had come to him anyway, at the top of a stairwell, in the wet, in the shape of a
polite man with folded hands, and Aoi understood that the room did not require you to come to
it. It required only that you read it, and then it came to you.

underlined twice; the paper is held against a window; behind it, the blurred light of a Nezu
street; a stamp lies on the table beside it, unused.]
