# The Visitor

*Live POV. Present tense.*

---

I have two minutes, so I am going to write the whole thing in two minutes, and if it stops
mid-sentence, that is the ending.

My name is Sakaguchi Rie. I am a nurse. I am not a collector and I am not a witness and I did
not buy anything at the Kanzaki sale. I am in this account because I answered a door.

I want that on the record, because everyone else in this book was looking for the room. I was
not looking. I was at home.

I am at home now. It is just after nine. The bell rang and I opened the door, because that is
what you do, and a man is standing on the step with his hands folded, well-dressed, polite,
and he has asked me one question.

He asked me whether I am Hana's granddaughter.

I said no. I said Hana's granddaughter is my neighbour. I said it before I thought, because it
is true, and because he looked at me the way you look at a door you intend to open.

And then I understood, standing in my own doorway, that the man had not asked because he
wanted to know who I was. He had asked because he wanted me to tell him who she was, and I
had, and I had done it in one sentence, on my own step, at nine at night, to a stranger with
his hands folded.

[IMAGE 18.1 — a doorway at night from inside: a well-dressed older man on the step, hands
folded, politely inclined; the narrator's hand is on the edge of the door; a hallway mirror
behind her reflects the man at an angle that does not match the doorway.]

---

He did not leave. He said thank you, and asked whether he might step in for a moment, and I
said no, and he said of course, and then he said something that I have not been able to stop,
which is: "She'll be told you tried."

I said, tried what.

He said, "You tried to warn her. That's what he'll write. It's better that way."

I do not know who *he* is. I do not know who *she* is, except that Hana's granddaughter lives
in the flat beside mine and is an appraiser and has been quiet for a month and
has a mirror in her front room that she turns to the wall. I have heard her through the wall,
some nights, moving furniture.

I am writing this because my hands have started to feel slow. That is the thing, in case
anyone reading this has felt it: not pain. Slowness. As if the air has thickened in the room,
and the door is a long way off, and your own front step has become a place you remember
rather than a place you are standing.

He is still on the step. He has not moved. He says the thing about Hana's granddaughter, and
then he says the other thing, the one I am going to write down because it is the only fact I
have:

He says, "Your grandmother worked in that house. Hana. She took the mirror out, and she was
paid, and she never spoke. That makes you family. And family is the only thing left to
collect."

[IMAGE 18.2 — the doorway from the hall: the visitor's shadow, long, crossing the threshold
into the flat; on the wall of the hall, the narrator's shadow is short and shrinking,
smaller than she is.]

---

I told him he had the wrong woman. My grandmother was a seamstress. She never mentioned a
house in Yanaka, or a mirror, or a payment, and she died when I was small, and I have one
photograph of her, and I am looking at it now while I type, because it is on the shelf by the
door, and the man on the step can see it.

He does not argue. He just waits, the way a man waits for you to finish a sum, and I look at
the photograph again, properly, for the first time in years. My grandmother, young, in a
doorway, with something wrapped in cloth beside her.

He has the wrong woman. I am not Hana's granddaughter; Hana's granddaughter lives next door.
But he does not need the right woman. He needs a woman within a wall of her, and a photograph
he can point at, and a door that opens when he knocks.

He can see it. He has looked at it twice.

In the photograph, my grandmother is young, and she is standing in a doorway she does not
own, and there is something on the floor beside her, wrapped in cloth, tall, about the height
of a standing mirror.

I have looked at that photograph every day of my adult life and I have never once wondered
what was wrapped in the cloth.

The man on the step asks me to hand it to him.

I am not going to hand it to him.

I am going to write his likeness instead, because a description is a document and a document
is the only thing I have left that he cannot fold.

He is sixty or so. Grey coat, good shoulder. Shoes polished past their age. Hands folded. He
does not smell of anything. He has the face of a man who is kind to strangers as a matter of
policy.

[IMAGE 18.3 — a shelf by a doorway: a single framed photograph of a young woman in a doorway,
beside a wrapped, mirror-tall bundle leaning in the corner; the photograph is slightly out of
focus and the narrator's hand is reaching toward it.]

---

My hands are slow now. The typing is slow. I am not going to make the end of the paragraph
and I am going to write it anyway.

Here is the thing I want whoever finds this to know. The man on the step did not come for me.
He came because I am next door to her. He is clearing the street. He is clearing every person
within a wall of Hana's granddaughter, one at a time, so that when she finally goes to the
room, there will be no one left who knew her, and no one left who could say what the room is.

That is what the deaths are. Not the collection. Not the curse. A man with a good coat,
tidying a neighbourhood the way you tidy a house before guests arrive, so that the last
person walks into the room alone.

I am going to knock on the wall now. That is my closing act as a document: I am going to
knock, three times, on the wall between my flat and hers, and I want her to hear it, and I
want her to know that the polite man in the grey coat has been to my door, and that she
should not open hers.

Knock.

That was one.

Knock.

That was two, and my hand is very slow now, and the door is very far.

Knock.

If she hears this, she knows. If she does not, then this page is the only knock that ever
reached her.

[IMAGE 18.4 — a wall between two flats, close: a hand against the wall, three faint marks
where knuckles struck it; through the wall's other side, unseen, a black-lacquer mirror
stands turned to face the room.]


---

The neighbour's account was found the next morning, in her flat, by the police, and it is the
document that made them reopen five deaths.

She had not written it on a phone. She had written it on the back of a photograph, the one on
the shelf by the door, the one of her grandmother in the doorway with the wrapped bundle, and
she had written it in pencil, in a hand that went from steady to unsteady across the sheet,
and she had put it under the statue by the door as if she were filing it.

The police read it. They read the eleven stories in Aoi's folder, which Aoi had left on her
table, which were found the same morning. They read the attendance sheet, and the ledger, and
the inventory. And for the first time since April, five deaths that had each been closed
separately were laid on one table, and they matched.

The handwriting on the back of the photograph is the only thing in the whole account that was
written by someone who was not looking for the room. It says, at the end, in the hand that is
no longer steady:

*I am not a witness. I am a neighbour. I am writing this because the woman next door has been
quiet for a month and because a polite man came to my door, and because I have understood, in
the last hour, that being a neighbour is a kind of being family, and that family is what he is
collecting.*

*If you find this, do not file it. Take it next door.*

The police took it next door. But next door was empty. The door of Aoi's flat was open. The
mirror was turned to the wall. The flat was clean, and in the dust on the floor there was a
room drawn, six mats, a doorway, a gap, a chest in the corner with a blank tag.

And on the kitchen table, where Aoi had left it, the folder with eleven stories in it, and on
top of the folder, a single sheet of paper in Aoi's hand, headed with a date in December, and
on it, four words.

*Kuramae. Bring the tape.*

sheet, a bound ledger, a temple inventory, a box of tags, laid side by side; a photograph of
a woman in a doorway, its reverse covered in pencil script; a single sheet headed "Kuramae.
Bring the tape."]


---

The police report on Sakaguchi Rie is short and Aoi has a copy of it, and it is included here
because it is the only document in the whole account that is written by someone with no stake
in the room.

It says: *Female, 44, nurse, found deceased at residence, no signs of violence, no signs of
forced entry. Deceased had opened the front door to a visitor; a photograph of the deceased's
grandmother was found on the floor, removed from its frame. Deceased's phone was found on the
hall table, screen on, a note app open containing an unfinished first-person account of an
encounter with an unidentified male. The account is coherent and detailed and names the
deceased's neighbour, Shirai Aoi. Neighbour's flat was entered and found empty. Neighbour's
whereabouts unknown. No further action pending identification of the male described.*

No further action pending identification of the male described. That sentence is the whole
failure of the account, in the language of the police: a woman wrote down a description of her
own killer, in a steady hand that went unsteady, and the description is *good* — grey coat,
sixty, folded hands, polite, no smell — and it fits a hundred thousand men in Tokyo, and none
of them is on file, because the hand was never a criminal until 1989 and has never been
arrested, and a description without a name is a description of everyone.

Aoi read the report in December and understood that Sakaguchi Rie had done the one thing that
could have caught the hand, and had done it perfectly, and that it had not been enough, because
the hand's whole method was to be *unremarkable*, and a perfect description of an unremarkable
man is a description of the city.

She kept the report in the folder, after the eleven stories and before the tags, and on the
front of it, in her own hand, she wrote the line that the police could not write:

*He is not on the list. He is the one who walks the list. Describe him to everyone. Someone
has seen him.*
