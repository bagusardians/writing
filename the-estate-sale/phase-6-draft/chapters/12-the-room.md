# The Room

*First person. Past, then present. The Unlock.*

---

Mikami brought a tape measure and I brought the box of tags, and we drove to Kuramae in the
dark because I did not want to be seen going in.

The warehouse held the lots Kei had never sold. They were not on shelves. They were on the
floor, arranged, and they had been arranged for a long time, and I had walked past them in
April without seeing it because a room drawn in furniture is only a room when you stand in
the doorway and let the walls finish themselves.

They finished themselves that night.

[IMAGE 13.1 — the Kuramae warehouse interior, wide: two hundred tagged objects arranged on
the floor to form the outline of a six-mat room; a low doorway marked by two upright posts;
the floor plan is legible as if from above; two small figures at the edge.]

Mikami measured it. He measured the mat dimensions from the objects, then the placement of
each object against the tags, then the whole room against the plans of the Yanaka house,
which I had pulled from the estate records.

The room was seven by twelve. The house's storage room was eight by fourteen.

"Wrong size," he said. "She had the plan. She'd have matched it."

He measured again, from the doorway, kneeling, then standing, then kneeling again. Then he
sat back on his heels and did not say anything for a while.

"It's the view from the floor," he said at last. "The room is drawn the way a person sees
it from lying down. Or sitting. Low." He looked at the tansu, which was placed at the edge
of the arrangement, in the corner, and then he looked at me. "A child. Eleven. Behind the
chest. This isn't the room. It's what she could see of the room, from where she hid."

[IMAGE 13.2 — a measured floor plan, hand-annotated: the planned eight-by-fourteen storage
room overlaid with the assembled seven-by-twelve arrangement; the discrepancy shaded; a
note reading "camera / eye height = c. 45 cm"; a small tansu symbol in the corner.]

The tags gave us the rest. Laid out in the order of the room, the twelve names were the
people who had been there in 1958. Kuroda Genji. The woman in the doorway: Sada. The
servant: Hana. The fourth man, paid to keep quiet: Toyama. And eight others, guests,
whose names are not important now.

And one tag with no name, older than the rest, dated 1958, carrying the small drawing of
the tansu.

That was her. Mitsuko, eleven, hidden behind the chest, watching her mother's hand come
down and strike a man who was going to do something unspeakable. Except that the album
photograph, the one loose at the back, showed the mother in the doorway and the man on the
floor and, behind the mother, partially hidden, a child. And Sada had confessed, and the
record said accident, and everyone had believed it for sixty years.

I did not believe it that night. I was holding the nameless tag, and I understood the thing
Mitsuko had done when she made it: she could not write her own name on the tag for the
person in that room, because she did not know whether the person who struck the blow had
been her mother or herself.

Mikami said, "Aoi."

I said, "I know."

He said, "There's someone at the door."

---

The Buyer came in without knocking. He did not hurry. He had the manner of a man who has
been in this room many times, and he told us so.

"My grandfather is on your wall," he said. "Kuroda Genji. I've spent my life buying back
the pieces of a house that buried him. You've brought me the last two."

He looked at Mikami. "You're the restorer. 1991. You'll want to sit down."

Then he looked at me, and his face did something I had not seen before, a kind of relief.

"And you're the mirror," he said. "The servant's line. I've been looking for the mirror for
twenty years."

[IMAGE 13.3 — the warehouse from the doorway: the arranged room; the Buyer standing at the
threshold; Aoi and Mikami small inside the arrangement; a single hanging bulb; the Buyer's
shadow falls across the tags on the floor.]

I want to tell you that I fought. I have thought about it, in the time I have left, which
is measured now in minutes rather than months, and I have decided that the readership is
owed the truth of it: I did not fight. I had spent four months learning how to read a dead
woman's handwriting, and when the man who had written none of it stood in front of me, I
did the thing I had trained to do. I looked at his hands.

They were folded. The way Toyama's hands were folded. The way a man folds his hands when he
means to be remembered.

The flash goes.

I am still holding the pose when I understand the photograph has taken something I cannot
get back. He asks me to tilt my chin. I tilt my chin. The room with no window is around me
now, and it is exactly the size I always was, because I was nine when my mother died and
the mirror was taken, and the room was built for a child, and I am the last piece.

I set the phone down. I am writing this on it. That is why the tense has changed; that is
the only reason; I did not notice until now, and now is all there is.

He says the politest thing anyone has ever said to me.

---

*[End of the manuscript. The final pages were recovered separately, printed, and inserted.
They consist of a single paragraph, undated, and a photograph.]*

---

If you are reading this, you have my account, in my hand, in the order I found it, and I
will not be able to correct it. The tags were never a list of the dead. They were a list of
us, all of us, the people who could not stop looking, and the collection was not a memory
and it was not a schedule. It was a set of instructions, and she wrote them sixty years ago,
and we followed them, every one of us, all the way into the room.

I am the last piece.
