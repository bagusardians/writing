# The Inventory

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The inventory was in a temple, and the priest had kept it for sixty-five years because no one
had ever come to collect it.

Ono was the third priest of a small Jōdo temple in Yanaka, and he had inherited the temple's
papers the way you inherit a garden: without asking, and with the expectation that you would
keep it alive. In the back of the storehouse, in a wooden box, were the records of the
funerals the temple had performed and the rooms it had been asked to bless and, once, in
November of 1958, a room where a man had died.

The blessing was a courtesy. When someone died suddenly in a house, the family sometimes
asked a priest to come and purify the room, and the temple kept a note: the date, the house,
the room, and—because the priest of that year had been a careful man—an inventory.

Not an inventory of value. An inventory of *presence*. The priest had listed what was in the
room when he arrived, because a careful priest lists what he blesses, and the note had
survived, and no one had ever read it, because no one had ever known it existed.

Ono read it in October, on a hunch, because a woman had come to the temple asking about a
house on the lane, and because he was the kind of priest who answered a question by going to
the record.

[IMAGE 16.1 — a temple storehouse: a wooden box open on a low bench, papers in bundles,
incense in the cold air; one sheet is unfolded, in a careful brush hand, headed with a date
in 1958 and the name of a house.]

---

The inventory listed thirty-one objects.

Ono read it twice, because thirty-one was not a number he had expected. He had heard, in the
neighbourhood, the story of the Kanzaki woman who had spent her life collecting, and he had
assumed the collection was the room. It was not. The room, when the priest blessed it in
1958, had held thirty-one things, and the rest — the two hundred and eleven of the estate —
were not the room.

They were the *replacements*.

Mitsuko had not been buying back the room. She had been buying back the room *and* its
absence: every object that was in it when the man died, and every object that had been taken
out of it after, and every object that had touched the room's edges in the years between. The
thirty-one were the original. The other one hundred and eighty were the years of the room
being emptied and refilled, and Mitsuko had pursued all of them, because a room is not a set
of things. It is a set of things *and* the space where the missing ones were.

The priest's list gave the original thirty-one. And beside each, in the margin, the temple's
careful priest had noted one more thing: whether the object was still in the room when he
left, or whether it had already been taken.

Twenty-nine were still there. Two were already gone.

Ono looked at the two, and one of them was the mirror.

[IMAGE 16.2 — a brush-written inventory sheet, numbered lines, each object named in a column;
two lines are circled in later pencil, one of them reading "kagami" (mirror), with a margin
note in a different hand indicating it was already removed.]

---

Ono called the estate, and the estate gave him Aoi's number, and Aoi came to the temple on a
wet afternoon in November and sat in the cold office and read the list.

"Two were gone before the priest arrived," Ono said. "The mirror, and a chest."

Aoi looked up. "A chest."

"A tansu. A large one. It was in the corner of the room, against the wall, and it was gone
when the priest came. He noted it because he remembered it from a previous visit, when the
house was whole."

Aoi sat with that for a long time, because it fitted, and because it was terrible.

The chest was not Mitsuko's. It was not in the ledger, or the album, or the sale. It had
appeared in the Kuramae warehouse, in the corner of the arrangement, with a blank tag. And
now the priest's inventory said that the chest had been in the room in 1958, and had been
removed, and that the child who hid behind it had hidden behind a chest that was being taken
away.

"The chest is where she hid," Aoi said. "And it was taken. And she's been looking for it her
whole life, and it turned up in the warehouse, and no one knows who put it there."

"The chest is the witness," Ono said. "The chest is the thing that stood between the child
and the room. It's the only object that was on both sides of the wall."

Aoi looked at the two circled lines — the mirror and the chest — and understood, finally, the
structure of the whole collection. Every object Mitsuko bought was a *replacement* for one of
the original thirty-one, bought after its original was lost, so that the room could be
reassembled in the only form available to her: a copy. The room she built was a replica,
built from memory, one object at a time, because the originals were gone.

Except the two that were removed while she watched: the mirror, which went to the servant,
and the chest, which went to no one, and which had come back.

She had been doing what the priest did. She had been keeping a record of presence in a room
she could not enter, and the only way she could hold the record was to buy the room's copies,
and the copies were, to her, the room.

[IMAGE 16.3 — Aoi's hand-drawn comparison: the priest's thirty-one objects on the left,
the estate's two hundred and eleven on the right, lines joining each original to the
replacement she bought for it; two originals have no replacement, and one replacement, the
chest, has no origin; the chest is drawn at the crossing.]

---

Ono gave her a copy of the inventory, and she took it home, and that night she laid it beside
the album and the ledger and the tags, and the four documents together told the whole truth
of the room, and she saw the last thing she had been missing.

The priest's inventory did not list a *person*. It listed thirty-one objects and a room where
a man died. But at the bottom of the sheet, in the margin, the careful priest had written one
line that was not an object and was not a room.

He had written: *one child present. would not speak. sat behind the chest until carried out.*

Aoi read that line perhaps thirty times.

*Sat behind the chest until carried out.* Not *hid*. Not *watched*. The child had sat behind
the chest and had not spoken, and had been carried out, and the priest had noted it because
a child carried out of a room where a man died is a thing a careful priest notes.

And Aoi understood, reading it, that the priest had seen the child, and the child had seen
him, and neither had spoken, and that this was the first silence — the original one, from
which all the other silences had been cut.

She wrote in her notebook:

*the room has thirty-one things and one child and one death, and the collection has two
hundred and eleven things and no child and no death, and that is the whole of it. she spent
her life replacing the room so she would not have to be the child in it. but you cannot
replace the child. you can only put another one in the gap.*

She underlined the last line.

And then, because she had by then understood what the chest in the warehouse was for, and who
the blank tag belonged to, she went to Kuramae.

[IMAGE 16.4 — a low table by lamplight: four documents laid side by side — a brush inventory,
a bound ledger, a photograph album, a box of tags — and a notebook open below them with a
handwritten line; a black-lacquer mirror stands against the wall behind, turned to face the
room.]


---

Aoi asked Ono whether she might see the room, and he took her there, because a priest can take
you anywhere in his parish and because he had come to trust her, and they stood in the lane
behind Yanaka Ginza in the cold and looked at the house.

The house had a new building on the lot. Ono had not known that. He had thought it was still
the old row-house, because the parish records said it was, and because a parish record is
sometimes the last thing in the world to admit a change.

Aoi asked whether the old house had been demolished.

Ono said the record did not say, and then he said the thing that mattered: that the family had
kept the *grave rights* and that there was a family grave in the temple, with three names on
it. Kanzaki Sada, Kanzaki Mitsuko, and a third name that Ono had always assumed was a child
who died young.

Aoi asked what the third name was.

Ono looked at the new building on the old lot and said: "It isn't a Kanzaki. It's a Kuroda.
The grave holds a Kuroda child, aged eleven, who was never buried in it. The stone is a
cenotaph. The family paid for a stone for a living child and left the death date blank."

Aoi stood in the lane and felt the whole account turn over one more time.

Mitsuko had put a stone in the family grave for a Kuroda child of eleven. Not for the man she
had watched die. For a *child* of her own age, in the enemy family, with the death date left
blank, which is what you do when you are waiting for a death, or when you do not believe in
one.

Aoi asked Ono who had paid for the stone.

He said the record showed it was paid in 1961. By cash. By a woman.

The same year as the first tag.

Mitsuko had been thirteen in 1961. She had bought a lacquer tray in Asakusa and written a name
on it, and she had also bought a stone for a child in the family that had killed her room, and
she had left the death date blank, and she had kept it blank for sixty years.

Aoi understood, standing in the lane, that the collection was not only a testimony against
Kuroda. It was an *obligation to him*. The two families had each buried half the night, and
Mitsuko had spent her life buying the room back so that the other family would not be left
holding only the silence. She had built a room that would hold *both* versions, and the stone
with the blank date was the room's other half, and the blank date was not waiting for the
Buyer.

It was waiting for her.

[IMAGE 17.5 — a temple graveyard in Yanaka, winter: a family grave with three names on the
stone, the third a Kuroda child with no death date; a priest and a woman stand before it; the
stone is clean and recently tended.]


---

Ono gave Aoi one more thing before she left the temple, and it was not a document and it was
not an object. It was a sentence his predecessor had left in the parish notebook, in 1958,
in the week after the blessing, and it is the earliest thing in the whole account written by
someone who was not a Kanzaki and not a Kuroda.

The priest had written: *Blessed the room. The mother was calm and the child would not speak.
The mother asked me to bless the chest, which was already gone, and I blessed the place where
it had been. I have never been asked to bless a place before. I did it.*

Aoi sat with that. The mother had asked the priest to bless the *place where the chest had
been*, in 1958, in the week the chest went out with the servant, and the priest had done it,
and had noted it because it was strange, and the note had sat in a parish book for sixty-five
years.

Sada had blessed the absence. That was the first act of the whole collection — not a purchase,
not a tag. A mother, a week after her daughter watched a man die and was carried out, asked a
priest to bless the empty place where the chest had stood, because the chest was gone and the
child would not speak and there was nothing else to do.

And Mitsuko, sixty years later, had spent her life *reversing* that blessing: filling the
absence, object by object, until the place where the chest had been was a chest again.

Aoi asked Ono whether the parish still had the power to bless an absence.

Ono said it did, and said it was called something else now, and that people mostly asked for it
at funerals, for the space a person leaves, and that he had done it four times that year, and
that it was the same rite Sada had asked for, and that it had never once, in sixty-five years,
been asked for a chest.
