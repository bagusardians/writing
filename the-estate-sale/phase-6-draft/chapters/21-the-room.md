# The Room

*First person. Present tense. The Unlock.*

---

Mikami brought a tape measure, the ledger, the inventory, the album, and the nineteen tags
in the box, and we drove to Kuramae in the dark because I did not want to be seen going in.

The warehouse was exactly as I had left it in my flat, drawn in dust: the two hundred
objects laid out in the shape of a room, the mats sketched, the doorway marked by two posts,
the gap in the centre, and in the corner, against the wall, the chest that no one could
account for, with the blank tag on its handle.

Only now I knew what the chest was. The inventory had told me. The chest was the original.
The chest was in the room in 1958, and was taken out while the child watched, and had come
back, and the room had been built around it, not the other way round.

Mikami put the lamp on the floor and the room came up out of the dark.

[IMAGE 21.1 — the Kuramae warehouse interior, wide: two hundred tagged objects arranged on
the floor to form the outline of a six-mat room; a low doorway marked by two upright posts;
the arrangement is legible as a floor plan; one hanging bulb; two small figures at the edge.]

---

He measured it, and I wrote the numbers, and the numbers were the whole of the account.

The arrangement was seven by twelve. The storage room in the Yanaka plan was eight by
fourteen. The gap in the centre was not at the centre; it was set back toward the chest by
about half a mat.

"It's not a room," Mikami said. "It's a view. From here—" he crouched beside the chest,
"—seven by twelve is what eight by fourteen looks like to a person lying on the floor in this
corner."

He measured the height of the light in the album, the camera height, forty to fifty
centimetres, and it matched the height of the arrangement's horizon, and he sat back on his
heels and said the thing that I had paid him to say.

"She didn't build the room she remembered. She built the room she saw. From behind the chest,
on the night, and she never corrected it, because she never wrote it down, and this is the
only true record of where everyone was standing."

Then he measured the gap. And the gap, when he measured it, was exactly one person wide, and
it was set at the angle of the child's sightline, and it faced the doorway.

"It's a witness stand," he said. "She built a witness stand and left it empty for sixty
years."

---

Mikami laid the four documents side by side on the floor: the inventory, the ledger, the
album, the tags.

The inventory gave the original thirty-one objects and the two that were already gone. The
ledger gave sixty years of replacements. The album gave the view from the floor. The tags
gave the names.

And when the four were laid together, the room resolved the way a floor plan resolves when
you finally look at it from the right height, and I saw the whole of it, and I am going to
set it down now, in the order an appraiser sets down a provenance, because it is the last
valuation I will ever make and I want it to be complete.

**In 1958**, in the room in Yanaka, there were twelve people: Kuroda Genji; Kanzaki Sada;
the servant Hana; the driver Toyama; and eight others, guests and hangers-on, whose names
are the tags. And there was one child, Kanzaki Mitsuko, aged eleven, behind the chest.

Kuroda Genji had been drinking. He had come to the house that night in the Kuroda family's
formal pair of swords, which is what a patron wears when he goes to be important. He did
something to Mitsuko that the record does not name and does not need to name. Her mother,
Sada, came in. There was a struggle. Kuroda fell. He struck the fifth panel of the screen and
bled there and did not get up. And Mitsuko, behind the chest, did not move, and the music box
played, and the priest was called, and the child was carried out.

That much I had known since the album. What I did not know until the four documents were laid
together was *who struck the blow*.

**Because the ledger has a first entry.** Not 1961. The 1961 tray was the first *purchase*.
But at the front of the ledger, on the inside cover, in the small upright hand, there is a
line dated 1958, and it is not a purchase, and it says:

*I hit him first. Mother finished it. She said it was her, and I let her, because I was
eleven. Every object in this room I have bought to say it back.*

Mitsuko hit him first. Her mother took the blame, confessed to the whole act, absorbed it
into the household register, and told the world that a child had done nothing. And the child,
who had hit a man and watched him fall and watched her mother carry it, had spent sixty years
buying the room back one object at a time, tagging each with the name of a person who had been
there, building a testimony out of furniture, because an eleven-year-old's word was worth
nothing and an object you can hold is worth everything.

[IMAGE 21.3 — the inside cover of the ledger, photographed at raking light: a single
handwritten entry dated 1958, three lines, a confession; below it, sixty years of purchase
columns; the handwriting on the top line is a child's.]

---

I stood in the gap. I know now that I should not have. Mikami told me not to, twice, and I
did it anyway, because I am an appraiser and I had spent nine months pricing the room, and
the only way to finish a valuation is to stand in the thing and let it be.

The room came together the way a room does when the last piece goes in.

The gap was the last piece.

I stood where the child had stood, behind the chest, facing the doorway, and the objects on
the walls — the mirror, the screen, the box, the comb, the doll, the blades, all two hundred
of them — turned toward me the way they had been arranged to turn, and the count in the room
finished, and the count was thirteen, and I was the thirteenth.

And I understood, standing in it, the two words on the back of the blank tag.

*She's you.*

Not *she was you*. Not *she is like you*. *She's you.* The child behind the chest had spent
her whole life building a room that would hold one witness in the one place the room was
built to be seen from, and the witness had to be someone who could read the room, and the
only person who could read the room was the person who had spent her life learning to read
dead women's handwriting, and that person was me.

I was not the last victim of a curse. I was the last *word* of a sentence that had been
being written for sixty-five years. Mitsuko did not kill me. Mitsuko *finished* me, the way
you finish a valuation, and she could not have done it without me, and she had been waiting
for someone like me to walk into her estate for six decades.

I heard the door of the warehouse open.

[IMAGE 21.4 — from inside the arrangement: the narrator standing in the central gap facing
the doorway; the two hundred tagged objects turned toward her; a man's silhouette entering
the doorway, backlit by the corridor; the chest in the foreground corner with the blank tag.]

---

The Buyer came in without hurrying, because he never hurried.

He was not what I had expected. I had expected a man in a grey coat with his hands folded,
and he had that, but he also had, that night, something I had not seen in any of the eleven
stories.

He had a *reason*.

He sat down on the floor at the edge of the arrangement, the way an old man sits down who has
been carrying something for too long, and he said, before I could say anything, the first true
thing anyone had said to me about the room.

"I'm not a killer," he said. "I want that on your record, because you're writing it. I'm
Hana's grandson. I'm the came-after. Everything your tags say about the deaths is true. Every
one of them is true. But I didn't do it for the collection. I did it because she asked me
to."

And then Kuroda Jun — the grandson, the polite man, the one who had bought the coat and the
sit and stood at the top of a stairwell in the wet — told me the thing that the whole
account had been missing, and I am going to write it down exactly, because it is the reason
this book is not a murder story.

He said: "Kanzaki Mitsuko hired me in 1989. She was sixty-two. She had been collecting for
thirty years, and she had realised that the collection was not going to be enough, because
the collection is only the room. The room is only the room. What she needed was the *route* —
the people who held the pieces, and the people who held the paperwork, and the person who
would finally stand in the gap. She could not kill them herself; an old woman in a house in
Yanaka cannot travel the city. So she wrote to my father, who was a Kuroda, and she told him
the truth about his father, and she asked him for help, and my father would not. And then in
1989 my father died, and she wrote to me."

He said: "She asked me to be the hand. She said she would do the rest. She said she would
build the room and she would leave the tags, and all I had to do was clear the route, one
person a month, quietly, and file the ones who mattered, and leave the last one alone until
the room was finished. And I said yes."

I asked him why.

He said: "Because she was right about my grandfather. He did what she said he did. And
because she paid me in the only currency that matters to a Kuroda, which is the truth about
our own name, told to us by the one person who saw it. She gave me the room, and the room gave
me back my grandfather as he actually was, and I have spent thirty-five years making sure no
one ever reads it differently."

[IMAGE 21.5 — two figures at the edge of the arranged room: the narrator standing in the
central gap; the Buyer seated on the floor, hands still folded; between them, on the floor,
the four documents laid side by side; the doorway open behind him.]

---

And there it was. The whole account, turned over one last time.

Kanzaki Mitsuko, the collector. The woman who bought a room back one object at a time. The
witness who could not speak. And — the thing no one had seen, not Aoi, not Mikami, not me —
the *author*. She had not just built the room. She had built the *route*. She had spent
thirty-five years, through a hired hand, moving the pieces of her testimony into the only
arrangement that could deliver it: a chain of people who each held one document, and one
person, at the end, who held none, and who would have to walk into the room and read it, and
the room would be complete, and the truth would be told.

The deaths were not the crime. The deaths were the *delivery*. And the man in the grey coat
was not the villain. He was the messenger, and he had been carrying the message for
thirty-five years, and he was tired, and he had come to the room, that night, to hand it over.

He said the last thing, and it was the politest thing anyone has ever said to me.

He said: "She left you a tag. It's in your hand."

I looked down. The blank tag, the old one, the one with the drawing of the chest, the one
with *she's you* on the back — it was in my hand. I had picked it up and never put it down,
the way you hold a thing you have already priced.

On the front, which had been blank for sixty-five years, there was a name now.

*SHIRAI AOI — 1958 — YANAKA.*

Not my year. Her year. She had put me in the room, in the year of the room, in the place the
room was. She had not killed me and she had not collected me. She had *entered* me, as a
witness, in the register of the room, in the only column that had ever mattered, the column
of the people who were there.

I am the last piece. I am the last piece because I am the last reader, and a testimony is not
complete until it has been read, and I have read it, and I am writing it down, and I am the
document.

---

Mikami tried to stop me. I want that in the record. He put his hand on my arm and he said my
name, and he said the tape measure, and he said we could leave, and I did the thing I had
trained my whole life to do, which was to hold still while a thing is handled, and let the
room finish the valuation.

The Buyer did not touch me. He sat on the floor with his hands folded and he watched, and he
said nothing, and that was his mercy, and I understood it as mercy though I cannot prove it
was.

The room became the room. The mats contracted the way they had contracted for the locksmith,
and the air thickened the way it had thickened for the nurse on her step, and my hand on the
phone slowed, and the doorway of the warehouse — the real one, the one the Buyer had come
through — became the doorway of the room in 1958, and through it I could see a child being
carried out, and a mother in the doorway, and a priest with his inventory, and the fifth panel
of a screen, blank.

And I wrote, in the last minutes, the sentence that I had come there to write, which was not
for the police and not for Kei and not for the room.

I wrote: *she was eleven and she was right and no one ever let her say it. I have said it.*

Then I set the phone down.

If you are reading this, you have my account, in my hand, in the order I found it, and I
will not be able to correct it. The tags were never a list of the dead. They were a list of
us — all of us, the people who could not stop looking — and the collection was not a memory
and it was not a schedule.

It was a *sentence*. She wrote it sixty years ago, one word at a time, and we were the words,
and I was the last one, and the sentence has now been read.

I am the last piece. I am the last piece because a room is not a room until someone stands in
it and a testimony is not a testimony until someone reads it, and I have stood in it, and I
have read it, and I am writing it down for you.

She was eleven. She hit him first. Her mother finished it and took the blame, and the family
buried it, and the child was carried out, and no one ever let her say what she saw.

I am saying it.

I am going to write the part that no one else can write, which is what it is like inside the
count, because I have read every account in the folder and every one of them stops at the same
place: the moment the room becomes the room. The locksmith stops. The nurse stops. Suzuki
stopped before he started. And I have understood, reading them, that they stopped because they
could not write the inside, and that the reason they could not write it is the reason I can:
I am not trying to get out.

The inside is not a place. That is the first thing. It is a *rate*. Everything inside the count
happens at the speed of being remembered, which is faster than thought and slower than light,
and so you experience your whole life at once and also one object at a time, which should be
impossible and is not.

The second thing is that the room shows you your own provenance. That is what it is for. Every
object on the wall is a holder in a chain, and when you stand in the gap, the chain resolves,
and you see the whole line of people who touched the thing that is at the centre of your life,
and the thing at the centre of my life was the mirror, and the chain of the mirror ran: a
lacquer workshop in Meiji; the Kanzaki house; Hana; my mother; my grandmother's flat; a
landlord's lock; twenty years of not being thought about; an estate sale in Kiyosumi-Shirakawa;
and me.

And at the end of the chain, past me, there was one more holder, and it was not a person.

It was the room. The room is the last holder of everything in it, because a collection that is
built to be read is a collection whose final owner is the act of reading. I was not a victim
and I was not a witness. I was the room's *last holder*, entered in the register the way a
provenance enters a final line: *acquired by the collection, in situ, 1958.*

The third thing is the thing I have to write carefully, because it is the only thing that
frightens me even now.

Inside the count, I could see the child. Not in the mirror; in the room. She was sitting behind
the chest, at the height the album had photographed, and she was counting, and she was not
counting the objects. She was counting the people. Twelve, and then a pause, and then she
looked at the gap and she did not count.

She *waited*.

She waited for sixty-five years, inside the room she built, for someone to stand in the gap so
that the count would come out even, and she did not do it for revenge and she did not do it to
kill. She did it because she had been carried out of the room at eleven and had never been
allowed to say what she saw, and a child who is not allowed to speak will build, if she has
sixty years, a room that speaks for her.

The room does not want a body. The room wants a *reading*.

And that is why, in the end, I did two things and not one. I stood in the gap — because I had
promised the account, and because I am an appraiser and I finish a valuation — and I also did
the thing Mikami told me to do, which was to *measure it and write it down and hand it to
someone else*, so that the reading would not end with me.

She counted thirteen. And I let the count finish.

But I wrote it down first.
