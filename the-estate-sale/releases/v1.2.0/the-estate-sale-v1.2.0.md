# Frame One — The Tag

*[Editor's note, found with the manuscript: This was recovered from a flat in Tateishi. The
pages were numbered in a hand that is small and upright, the way a person writes when they
expect the writing to outlast them. I have transcribed them as they were. I have not
corrected the places where the tense changes.]*

---

The hall smelled of wet cardboard and floor polish. Two hundred lots on eight trestle
tables, each tagged in the same hand, small and upright. I had come for one lot. I told
myself that.

The sale was in Kiyosumi-Shirakawa, in a warehouse someone had swept out and strung with
bulbs. The canal was two streets over and you could feel it in the floor, a coldness that
came up through the concrete the way water does. Kanzaki Mitsuko had died in March, at
seventy-eight, in a house in Yanaka she had not left in eleven years. Her son had emptied
the house into this room and was selling it by the piece.

I buy estates. I price the dead for strangers, and I am good at it, and I have never once
told myself it was anything else. You learn the trade the way you learn a language you
will never be fluent in: valuation, condition, provenance, reserve. You learn to hold a
thing without wanting it. The ones who cannot do that leave the trade, or they keep
everything, and their flats fill up, and I have seen both.

[IMAGE 1.1 — wide photograph of the estate hall: eight trestle tables, hanging bulbs,
cardboard, a crowd of anonymous buyers. One tag, on the nearest table, in focus.]

The tag on the mirror said:

*SHIRAI — 2003 — TATEISHI*

A dressing mirror, black lacquer, a hairline crack down the glass. Reserve eight thousand
yen. It was not worth eight thousand yen. It was worth more to me than the whole table,
because it had been my mother's, and it had disappeared from her flat in Tateishi in 2003,
the year she died, and I had spent twenty years deciding that memory was a liar.

I did not buy it. That is the thing I want on the record. I stood in front of it for a long
time with a stub of pencil and I priced everything else in the hall, and I did not buy it,
and by the time I left, someone had.

A man was watching me from the door. Older, well-cut coat, the kind of politeness that
costs money. He did not approach. He was doing what I was doing: reading tags. He read them
the way a man reads a menu he intends to order from — not idly, but with his hand already
half-raised.

---

Let me put down what I know about the sale itself, because everything after it depends on
this room, and I have learned that the room is always the evidence.

Two hundred and eleven lots, by Kei's own count. Every lot tagged. Every tag the same hand.
Every tag carrying three things: a name, a year, and a place. Not the object's name; the
maker's mark was often there too, on the object. The tag named something else. A person.

At the time I assumed, as anyone would, that the tag named the previous owner. Estate
sellers do this. *Tanaka — 1974 — Ueno.* Who had it, when they had it, where they had it.
It is a courtesy and a sales tool at once: an object with a history sells faster than an
object with none.

I spent three hours in that hall and I read perhaps sixty tags, and I did the thing I am
paid to do, which is to notice patterns without yet knowing what they mean.

The places were all Tokyo. Ueno, Asakusa, Sendagi, Nezu, Kuramae, Tateishi, and one that
repeated more than any other: Yanaka. Thirty-one tags said Yanaka. Thirty-one objects, out
of two hundred and eleven, had come through a house in the same small quarter of Taitō
ward.

The years ran from 1961 to 2023. Not one tag was dated earlier than 1961. I noticed that
and filed it and did not think about it again for two months, which is the single largest
mistake of my professional life.

And the names.

I have a good memory for names because names are the currency of my trade; you cannot
appraise provenance if you cannot hold a chain of names in your head. So when I say I did
not recognise the names on the tags, I mean something precise. I did not recognise them as
*owners*. But twice that afternoon, a name on a tag produced in me the small electric jolt
of a word heard in the wrong context — a name I knew from somewhere that was not an
object, and could not place.

One was Kuroda. The other was my own.

[IMAGE 1.2 — a photograph of a table of lots: lacquerware, a ceramic vase, a stack of
folding fans, a small brass clock; in the foreground, three or four tags legible, all in
the same small upright hand, each showing a name, a year, and a district.]

---

That night I called Mikami.

He restores furniture out of a timber row-house in Sendagi, and he has the manner of a man
who thinks in materials. He will not tell you what a thing is worth. He will tell you what
it is made of, and when, and who has repaired it, and from that you can price the world.
His shop smells of shellac and dust and cold tea. He had known Kanzaki Mitsuko as a
customer for thirty years and he had never once been inside her house.

"I found my mother's mirror in a dead woman's sale," I said.

"Then it wasn't your mother's," he said. "It was the dead woman's, and your mother had it
before her, and someone had it before your mother. That's all a mirror is. A queue."

"There was a tag. It had my name on it. And a date. And a place."

There was a pause long enough that I heard him put something down — a chisel, by the sound,
set flat on the bench, the way he sets everything down as if it might run.

"Read me the tag," he said.

I read it to him. *Shirai. 2003. Tateishi.*

"Don't buy the mirror," he said.

"I didn't."

"Someone did," he said. "Don't find out who."

I asked him why, and he said a thing I wrote down in my notebook, because I did not yet
understand that he was telling me the truth and not being poetic.

He said: "Because a tag is a name and a date and a place, and if she put your mother's name
on something, then she knew your mother, and she knew the year, and she knew the place, and
your mother has been dead twenty years. A dead woman in Yanaka knew a dead woman in
Tateishi well enough to write it down. That's the part you should be afraid of. Someone
kept track."

I said it was probably nothing. Old women keep things.

"Old women keep things," he agreed. "But they don't tag them with the year somebody died."

---

I want to be honest here, at the start, about the thing I have been least honest about.

When Mikami told me not to find out who bought the mirror, I hung up and I opened my laptop
and I found out. It took four minutes. The sale listing had the buyer's initial and a
deposit date. That was all. But four minutes was enough to learn that the buyer had paid in
cash, in person, an hour after I left, and had asked, the seller told me later, for the tag
to be left on the mirror, which is not a thing buyers ask, because a tag on a mirror is
ugly.

I did not know what it meant. I told myself I was pursuing a valuation. I told myself I was
protecting an interest — that if the mirror had been stolen from my mother, I had a stake.
Those were both true and both false.

What I actually felt, sitting in the dark in my flat in Tateishi with the laptop open, was
the thing the whole trade trains you never to feel, and which is why they say you should
not price your own possessions: I wanted it. Not the mirror. The answer. I wanted to know
who had come for my mother's mirror in 2003, and who had come for it again, twenty years
later, in a dead woman's sale.

That want is the engine of everything that follows. I am writing it down plainly because
the reader is owed the cause, and the cause is older than the mirror.

When I was nine, and my mother died, the landlord changed the lock while I was at my
grandmother's. I came back to a door I could not open, and a man with a key I had never
seen, and a two-room flat that had been emptied while I stood outside it. The mirror was
already gone; so was everything. I did not cry. I did the thing I have done ever since,
which is to make an inventory of what is missing. And I made a decision that year, the
way a child decides, which was that I would be the one who held the key. It was not a
child's wish to be let in. It was a child's decision never to be the one locked out.

I have spent twenty years pricing other people's rooms so that I would never have to
stand outside my own. I have never once noticed that the door I keep opening is the same
door.


---

I went back to the warehouse in June, which I have not yet described, and which belongs here
because it is where the trade taught me the first real lesson of the account.

The lots were in Kuramae, on shelving, and Kei let me read them without a sale and without a
buyer, which is the only way to read a collection. I spent four days there. I read all two
hundred and eleven tags, and I wrote them in a book, and I sorted them, which is what an
appraiser does when she cannot afford to feel.

Here is what four days of reading told me, before I understood any of it.

The names were not random. Forty-one of the two hundred and eleven names appeared more than
once, across different objects, in different years. A name would appear on a lacquer box
bought in 1974 and again on a pair of tongs bought in 1988, and the two objects had nothing
in common, and the two purchase years had nothing in common, and the only thing they had in
common was that the same person had held both, fourteen years apart, and had died between.

I wrote that down as a pattern and left it. I should have written it down as an *address*.

The places were not random either. I mapped them, because I map things; it is the only
unambiguously useful habit I have. Every place on every tag fell inside a rough circle three
kilometres across, centred not on the Kanzaki house but on a point between Yanaka and Nezu,
and there was one name — a family name, a name that appeared seven times — whose every
appearance was farther out, at the edge of the circle, as if the family had been moving away
from the centre for sixty years and had never quite escaped.

That name was Kuroda.

[IMAGE 1.3 — a hand-drawn map of central Tokyo with two hundred and eleven place-names
plotted as dots; the dots form a dense ring around Yanaka and Nezu, with a scatter of seven
points trailing off to the north-east; the map is annotated in pencil.]

I asked Kei, on the fourth day, whether the name Kuroda meant anything to him.

He was quiet for longer than the question deserved.

"No," he said. "Not to me. My mother never said it. But the house had a deed in the back of
a cupboard with a Kuroda listed as a former resident, and I asked her once, and she said the
deed was wrong and burned it."

"Burned it," I said.

"She burned a deed," he said. "The deed to her own house. I was eighteen. I thought she was
losing her mind." He looked at the shelving, at two hundred tagged things. "Now I think she
was filing it. The way she filed everything. In a place no one could read."

I asked him where the ashes went.

He said he didn't know, and then he said something that I have thought about every day since,
and that I am going to put here, because it is the first sentence in this account that was
aimed at me and I did not know it.

He said: "She swept them into the storage room. She swept them under the wall."

Under the wall. Into the one room in the house that had no door.


---

I am going to add the thing I learned about my mother, because this account began with a
mirror and the mirror was never the first object.

My mother's name was Naoko. She worked at a laundry in Tateishi. She was thirty-four. And
there is one thing about her that I did not put in this account the first time, because I did
not know it until November, and because it is the reason I am in the room at all.

She kept a notebook. Not a diary; a notebook, the way a person keeps accounts. I found it in
the box of her papers, the same box as the dry cleaner's receipt, and it is the most ordinary
document in the whole account and it is the one that made me certain.

In the notebook, in her hand, in the year before she died, my mother wrote a list. It is not a
list of anything. It is a list of objects, and beside each object, a name and a place, and the
names are not family names and the places are not places she ever went.

There are nineteen objects on the list. Nineteen. And every one of them is in the Kanzaki
estate.

My mother spent the last year of her life making a list of the things a woman in Yanaka was
going to sell two decades later. She could not have known. Unless she had been given the list
— unless someone had been sending her the record, one piece at a time, the way a person sends
a will to its heirs in advance.

The last entry on the list is the mirror. And beside it, in her hand, in the final year, a
note to herself, which is the first sentence in this whole account that was written to me.

It says: *this one comes to the child.*

[IMAGE 1.4 — a small soft-cover notebook, open, in a woman's hand: a numbered list of
household objects with names and districts beside them; the last entry, the mirror, is
underlined; below it, one short line; the notebook is creased and water-stained.]


---

*Note on the documents, added by the finder.*

The account that follows was not written as a book. It was written in three places: in a
notebook, in a folder of statements, and on a phone, and the three were assembled by me after
the fact, in the order the writer intended, and I have changed nothing but the order of the
folder.

The notebook pages are the frame — the writer's own account of how she came to the room. The
statements are the eleven stories, which she collected from the people who held the pieces, in
her own hand or theirs, and which she labelled in the folder as the collection. The phone pages
are the end, and the phone pages are in the present tense, and the writer never explained why,
and I have decided that she did not need to.

I have put the statements where she put them. I have kept her headings. Where a statement is
in the third person, it is because the writer took it down that way, and where a statement is
in the first person and present tense, it is because the phone was still running.

I am not the writer. I did not know her. I found the folder on a table in a flat in Tateishi
with the door open and the mirror turned to the wall, and I am the person the last page of the
account is addressed to, and I have done the only thing a reader can do with a thing like this,
which is to put it where someone else will find it.

If you are reading this, it has been found twice.

The second finder is you.

face up; a mirror in the corner, turned to the wall; morning light through a window with a
laundromat sign beyond it.]

# The Mirror

*Live POV. Present tense.*

---

The app wants four photographs, so I am taking four photographs.

That is the whole job now. You stand in a stranger's emptied flat and you make a stranger's
furniture look like something a person would want, and you upload it, and somewhere a buyer
decides. I have done it eleven thousand times. I can tell you the reserve on a tansu before
I open the drawer, and the story a water stain is telling, and whether a lacquer is Taishō
or a good Meiji fake, and none of that helps me now, because the mirror is in the room and
the room is wrong.

The flat is in Nezu, third floor, a corner unit over a senbei shop. The owner died in
January. The family is in Osaka and wants it gone by the end of the month, and they have
told me to price everything and photograph everything and not to call them unless there is
an emergency, which is the standing instruction and means they do not want to hear from me
at all.

I photograph the mirror from the front. Black lacquer, Meiji, a dressing mirror on a stand.
The lacquer has gone soft at the edges the way old lacquer does, and the glass has a
hairline crack from the lower left corner up toward the centre, like a river on a map. I
photograph it from the left, where the crack catches the light. I photograph the maker's
mark on the back, which is worn to a smudge.

Then I step in front of it to photograph the full height, and that is the fourth
photograph, and in the fourth photograph, behind me, the room is not the room.

I look at the screen. Then I look at the room. Then I look at the screen.

Behind my reflection there is a doorway that is not behind me. It is low and pale, and
through it there is a floor of six mats, and a chest against the wall, and the wall has no
window. I turn around. The wall behind me is a wall. There is no doorway and there is
nothing beyond it, only plaster and the ghost of where a shelf was.

The phone has forty-one percent. The room has no doorway. The photograph has both.

I take the fourth photograph again. The doorway is still there, smaller, as if it has
noticed me noticing it.

[IMAGE 2.1 — the fourth photograph: a woman's reflection in a cracked mirror; behind her,
where the wall should be, a low pale doorway into a windowless six-mat room.]

---

I am not, by temperament, a person who sees things.

I want that on the record, because the account that follows is full of things I saw, and a
reader is entitled to ask whether I was the kind of narrator who sees. I am not. I have
worked with antiques for eleven years and the trade has one iron rule: the object is what
it is. It is not what it reminds you of. I have sold a dead child's teddy bear without
tears because the bear was a 1950s Asahi and worth four thousand yen, and the tears were
not part of the transaction, and I have never once in my life seen a ghost.

So when I say the doorway was in the photograph, I am not telling you I believed it. I am
telling you it was in the photograph. I have the photograph. It is in the account.

I put the phone in my pocket and I finished the flat.

That is the thing I keep coming back to. I finished the flat. There were two more rooms and
a kitchen and a set of shelves, and I priced them all, and I uploaded the listing, and I
did the professional thing, which is to treat an anomaly as a lighting problem and move on.
It is possible to know two things at once — that a wall is a wall, and that a photograph of
the wall is not — and to choose, for eleven hours a day, to believe the wall.

I have thought about those eleven hours more than almost anything else. Because if I had
left then, I would never have met him, and I would be alive. I have decided that this is
not a comfort and not a regret but simply a fact, the way a value is a fact: eleven hours
of fixing the lighting by believing the wall.

---

The buyer arrives at two.

He is early and he is polite and he asks about the mirror before he asks about anything,
which is how I know he has already seen the listing. He does not look at the glass. He
looks at the back, at the maker's mark, and he turns it with two hands the way you turn a
plate you intend to buy.

"You've had it long?" he says.

"It's a lot in an estate. It came in this morning."

"From the Yanaka house."

I don't answer. He doesn't need me to. He lifts the mirror an inch off the table and sets
it down, testing the weight, and the crack in the glass finds the light and throws it on
the wall, a thin bright line.

"There'll be a tag on it," he says.

I am holding the phone. I am thinking about the fourth photograph and the doorway that
does not exist, and I am aware, the way you are aware of a draught, that I should have
left when I first saw it.

"There's a tag," I say.

He asks to see it. I tell him the tag stays with the lot; that's the rule of the sale. He
smiles as if I have told a joke he has heard before.

"That's the rule," he agrees.

Then he walks around the table to stand behind me, and I understand, in the moment it
takes him to cross the four steps, that he has not come for the mirror.

I want to describe him exactly, because I have learned that description is the only kind of
evidence I am still able to give. He is perhaps sixty. He wears a grey coat with a good
shoulder, and his shoes are polished the way a man polishes shoes when the shoes are older
than the man who sold them to him. His hands are folded at his waist. He does not smell of
anything. He is the most ordinary man I have ever been afraid of.

"You've read the tag," he says. It is not a question.

I say nothing.

"Read it again," he says.

[IMAGE 2.2 — the flat's front room, wide: a low table, a shelf, the black-lacquer mirror
standing on the boards; a man in a grey coat stands behind the woman who is photographing;
her phone is raised, and in the mirror the same man appears twice, once behind her and once
further back, in the doorway.]

---

I will tell the killing the way I told the reading, because it happened slowly enough to be
told.

He asked me to photograph the mirror once more. He said it in a way that sounded like an
appraisal instruction, and I am trained to obey appraisals, and so I raised the phone.

"Not that one," he said. "The tall one. Stand in front."

I stood in front. I looked into the glass. Behind me, in the glass, the doorway was open
and the six-mat room was there, and the chest, and on the chest a small white tag moved in
a draught that was not in the real room, because there was no draught in the real room.

"Closer," he said.

I took one step. The crack in the glass ran from the corner to the centre, and it divided
my face horizontally, and through the lower half of the crack I could see the room, and
through the upper half I could see the flat.

The flash goes.

I am still holding the pose when I understand the shutter has taken something I cannot get
back. The photographer asks me to tilt my chin. I tilt my chin. The crack in the glass runs
down between us, and in it, small, the room with no window, and a child standing in the
doorway, watching me price her house.

I set the phone down.

"You should have left the lot alone," he says.

It is the politest thing anyone has ever said to me.

---

He did not hurry. That is what I have to say about the end of me: he did not hurry.

He took the tag off the mirror first. He did it carefully, sliding the cotton thread over
the top of the stand so as not to tear it, and he put the tag in his coat pocket, and then
he took a cloth from his other pocket, folded, and he began to clean the glass.

I did not understand. I said, "What are you doing," and he said, "A mirror should be clean
for the next person," and kept cleaning, and I stood there and let him, because I am
trained to hold still while a thing is handled, and because some part of me still believed
this was an appraisal.

The cloth was damp. It smelled faintly of something I could not name, and the smell stayed
in the room after he had finished, and I have smelled it once since, on a train, on a man's
coat, and I got off at the next station.

He cleaned the glass in small circles, working from the outside in, and as he worked the
crack began to widen, or I began to see it better, and the room in the glass began to
resolve — the six mats, the chest, the tag on the chest — and I understood, finally, that
he was not cleaning the mirror.

He was cleaning the window.

[IMAGE 2.3 — close: a man's gloved hand wiping a mirror's surface in slow circles; the
reflection in the glass shows a six-mat room with a chest; the cloth leaves a faint
milky smear.]

---

I am writing this on the phone, and the phone is at nineteen percent, and the room has
stopped being the room.

That is the clearest way I can put it. I am still standing in the flat in Nezu. The low
table is still there. But the doorway is open now in the wall, and the six-mat room is
beyond it, and the chest has a tag on it, and the tag has a name on it, and the name is
mine.

He has not hit me. I want to be clear. There is no weapon. He is standing by the door with
his hands folded, and the room is doing it, or the mirror is doing it, or the thing in my
head is doing it, and I have decided that the distinction no longer matters.

I am finding it harder to move. That is the physical fact. Not pain. A heaviness, as if the
air has become the kind of air you find at the bottom of a stairwell. My hand on the phone
is slower than it was. The sentences are taking longer to arrive.

He says one more thing before I stop being able to answer him.

He says: "Your mother took it from the house in 1958. The servant's daughter. I've been
waiting for it to come back to the family since I was a boy, and now it has, and now there
are only two pieces left, and you're one of them."

I do not know what that means. I am writing it down because I am a professional, and because
the account is the only thing I am still able to finish.

The doorway is very close now. I can see the mat edges. Six mats, and in the corner a
tansu, and behind the tansu, the place where a child would hide.

I am not going to finish this. I am going to type until the screen goes dark, and I am
writing for whoever finds the phone, and I want them to know two things, because they are
the only things I have.

The first is that he was polite the whole time.

The second is that the mirror was not the point. The mirror was never the point. The point
is the room, and I have seen it now, and the room is where I am going, and the room is not
empty.

[IMAGE 2.4 — the phone, later: a camera app open on the desk, screen showing the room with
no window; the desk chair is empty; a small white tag, blank, lies on the seat.]


---

I have to keep typing even though the words are coming slower, because the count is nearly
done and the phone is the only thing in this room that is still mine.

The room has taken on a smell. It is not a bad smell. It is the smell of old tatami and
shellac and cold tea, which is Mikami's shop, which is three kilometres away, and the room is
using it, the way a person uses a voice they have heard before.

I can see the tags now, all of them, on the walls, though the walls are the walls of a flat
in Nezu and there are no tags in the flat in Nezu. I can read them. I do not want to read
them. The ones nearest me are the ones I have to read.

They are the names of the people who have been in this account. I do not know how I know
that. I know it the way you know a valuation before you have finished the arithmetic.

There is one tag left blank, at the end of the row, at my eye level, and it is turned so I can
see it, and it has no name and no date and no place, and I understand, in the last of the
light, that it is waiting, and that it is not waiting for me.

It is waiting for whoever reads this.

I am going to put the phone down now. I am going to face the doorway, because that is what the
child did, and because after eleven years of pricing dead people's rooms I have learned that
the last honest thing you can do in a strange room is to stand in it the way it was built to
be stood in.

The flash goes one more time. I do not take it. The room takes it.

[IMAGE 2.5 — the mirror at night, alone in the flat: the glass shows a six-mat room with a
child in the doorway and a row of tags on the wall; the final tag in the row is blank; the
photographer is gone from the reflection.]

# The Coat

*Close third, past tense.*

---

Yano did not buy the coat. He stood in the doorway of his own shop and read its tag twice,
and then he read it a third time, and by then the shop had closed around him and the tag
had not changed.

He ran a reuse shop in Ueno, four racks and a counter, and he had been doing it for
nineteen years. He knew what a coat was. A coat is a person's decision to be warm, made
once, and then the coat outlives the decision. He had sold four hundred coats. He had
never once kept one.

The coat was heavy grey wool, Shōwa-cut, shoulders too wide for anyone alive. The tag was
tied to the hanger with cotton thread, small and upright:

*KURODA — 1958 — YANAKA*

He knew the hand. He had seen it three times that month, on three lots he had bought at the
Kanzaki estate, and he had sold two of them, and he had kept the third in the back because
he could not find a buyer who did not stop reading the tag.

[IMAGE 3.1 — a reuse-shop rack: a heavy grey overcoat on a hanger; a small handwritten tag
tied with cotton thread, reading KURODA, 1958, YANAKA.]

---

The three lots had come from the estate like everything else: cheap, clean, and strange.
Yano bought in bulk from estate clearers, sight unseen, fifty thousand yen for a van of
household goods, and he sorted and priced and sold, and the margin was thin and the work
was honest. He did not usually read the tags. Tags went in the bin with the packing
material, because a tag with a previous owner's name on it depresses the sale.

But this estate's tags were not previous owners' names. He had understood that in the first
week, and he had understood it the way you understand a smell in a room: without being able
to say what it was.

The first lot was a tea caddy. Tag: *TOYAMA — 1958 — YANAKA*. He sold it in a day to a
salaryman who collected tea utensils and did not read tags.

The second was a length of kimono silk, unused, Shōwa. Tag: *SADA — 1958 — YANAKA*. He sold
it to a dressmaker in Nezu.

The third was the daishō stand, and the third was where he stopped, because the tag on the
daishō stand said *YANO — 1958 — YANAKA*, and Yano is not a common name, and Yano was his
name, and his father's, and his grandfather's.

He had looked up the name. He had not meant to. It was the kind of thing you do at the
counter at closing time with your phone in your hand, hunting a dead man the way you'd
pick at a scab. Kuroda Genji had drowned in a pond at Inokashira in 1958, aged fifty-two,
and the record said accident, and the record did not say why a man from Yanaka was at
Inokashira in the winter.

Yano put the coat on the rack and priced it at twelve thousand and went home.

On the way he thought about his father, who had owned a coat like it, and who had died in
March, and whose coat he had sold in April because he could not stand to have it in the
house. He thought: everyone's father is in the coat. That is what a coat is.

---

The next morning the rack was empty of the grey coat.

He asked his one part-timer, a student named Emi who worked three mornings a week and
wanted to be a curator, and Emi said a man had come at nine, well-dressed, polite, and paid
the twelve thousand and taken the coat, and left no name, and asked about the tag. Emi had
given him the tag. It was attached to the coat. It was always attached to the coat.

Emi was troubled. She said the man had asked her, before he paid, whether she had read the
tag, and she had said no, and he had said, "Good," and she had felt, she told Yano, like a
child who has passed a test she did not know she was sitting.

Yano went into the back and stood where the third lot was, the thing he had kept, the old
daishō stand he could not sell. He had kept it because of its tag. The tag said *YANO —
1958 — YANAKA*.

His family had never lived in Yanaka. He had never heard the name in his life. Then he
thought of his grandfather, who had also been a Yano, and who had died before Yano was
born, and whose life was a set of facts his family had never once narrated. A grandfather
he never met could have stood in a room in Yanaka in 1958. A name on a tag is not a
person. It is an heir.

[IMAGE 3.2 — a separate lot in the back room: a dark wooden daishō stand, two sword hooks,
with a small tag reading YANO, 1958, YANAKA. It is photographed in dim light.]

---

He called the estate. The son answered, Kanzaki Kei, tired and quick. Yano asked whether
there were more tags. Kei said there were two hundred lots, all tagged the same, by his
mother, and he did not know what they meant, and he did not want to.

"Do you know what your mother did in 1958," Yano asked.

"She was eleven," Kei said. "She was a child. There's nothing she did."

Yano said thank you and hung up and looked at the tag on the daishō stand again. His own
name, in a dead woman's hand, dated before he was born. He went to the door and locked it,
though it was only eleven in the morning, and he did not know why until he heard, on the
other side of the glass, someone knock politely and wait.

He turned the tag over. On the back, in pencil, small: *still here?*

[IMAGE 3.3 — the reverse of a tag, held in a hand: in faint pencil, the words "still
here?" with a date, 1958.]

---

Yano did not open the door. He stood behind the glass with his hand on the bolt and looked
at the man on the other side, and the man looked back with the patience of someone who has
already been let in.

It was not the same man who had bought the coat; Yano was almost sure. This man was older,
or else Yano was only now willing to see the age. He wore a grey coat. He had his hands
folded.

"Yano-san," the man said, through the glass. "You have the stand."

Yano said nothing.

"You've had it three weeks. You've read the tag twice. You've looked up Kuroda." The man's
voice was reasonable. It was the voice of a man explaining a delivery. "That's how it
starts. It's not your fault. The tag does it."

"What do you want," Yano said, through the glass, and his voice came out smaller than he
intended.

"The stand," the man said. "And the tag. The tag most of all. It's the last one from that
shelf unless the mirror comes back, and the mirror is spoken for."

Yano understood, then, that the man had not come to rob him. You do not rob a shop by
knocking on the glass in daylight. You come to finish a transaction. And Yano, who had
spent nineteen years finishing transactions, knew the feeling of a customer who has
already decided and is only waiting for the seller to agree.

"I don't have the stand for sale," Yano said.

"Everything's for sale," the man said, not unkindly. "That's the trade."

[IMAGE 3.4 — the shop exterior at night from across the street: the closed sign, a figure
seen through the glass standing where the counter is, facing the racks; the photographer is
outside, at a distance, and the shop's reflection in the window shows one more person than
the room contains.]

---

Yano did the thing he had done with the coat, which was to do nothing, which was the
professional thing.

He went home. He locked his flat. He put the daishō stand in the corner of his bedroom
where he could see it, which he had never done with a stock item in nineteen years, and he
sat with his back to the wall and looked at it.

Around midnight he understood the thing that had been sitting under his skin for three
weeks.

The tag on the stand was his grandfather's name. But he did not have a grandfather on the
Yano side; his father had been raised by a stepfather, and the Yano name had come into the
family in a way no one had ever explained. Which meant the tag did not name his
grandfather.

It named whoever had first borne the name the family now carried. It named a man who had
been in a room in Yanaka in 1958, and paid for it, and passed the debt down.

"I'm an heir to a room I've never seen," Yano said aloud, to no one, in the dark, and the
sound of his own voice in the flat made him get up and check the door.

The door was locked.

The stand was not in the corner.

---

He found it in the front room, by the window, facing the street, and the tag was no longer
on it.

In its place, on the wall, someone had taped a photograph. It was a photograph of Yano,
taken that night, in his flat, asleep in the chair by the wall — or not asleep; Yano did
not remember closing his eyes; but the photograph showed his eyes closed, and the daishō
stand behind him in the corner, where he had put it, hours before he moved it.

He turned the photograph over.

On the back, in pencil, small and upright: *YANO — 1958 — YANAKA.*

And beneath it, in a steadier hand, the words: *the debt is not the name. the debt is the
silence. you have been silent three weeks. that is long enough.*

Yano looked up at the window. Outside, across the street, under the streetlamp, a man in a
grey coat stood with his hands folded, watching the flat, waiting, the way he had waited
outside the shop, the way he had waited outside every shop that had ever sold a tagged
thing, and Yano understood, with the particular clarity of the very tired, that he had
already read the tag more than four times.

He went to the phone to call the police. He dialled two digits.

Then he stopped, because the photograph on the wall was not a photograph of him asleep,
and he had known that from the first look. It was a photograph of him dead. It showed the
particular slackness a face takes when the person is gone, and the daishō stand behind
him, and the tag on the stand, and Yano had never seen his own face like that in his life,
and yet he recognised it, the way you recognise a value on a thing you have not priced.

He put the phone down, and he sat in the chair by the wall, because that was where the
photograph said he would be, and because it seemed, by then, like the only seat left.


---

Aoi found Emi four days later, at the reuse shop in Ueno, because a part-timer who has sold a
coat to a man who asked about a tag is a witness, and Aoi had begun to collect witnesses the
way she collected provenance: carefully, and in order.

Emi was twenty-three and wanted to be a curator and had not slept well since April. She sat
in the back room with the daishō stand, which she had been told not to touch, and she told Aoi
the whole of it, in the way a person tells a thing they have been holding alone.

She said the man had come at nine, before opening, and had knocked on the glass, and Yano had
not been in yet, and so she had opened the door herself, which she was not supposed to do.
She said the man had asked, before anything else, "Do you have a coat with a tag on it," and
she had said yes, many things have tags, and he had said, "A grey Shōwa coat. A tag in a
particular hand." And she had brought out the coat, and he had read the tag, and had smiled,
and had paid twelve thousand and asked her to leave the tag on.

She said: "He asked me whether I had read the tag. I said no. He said good."

Aoi asked her why she had not read it.

Emi said: "Because you don't read a tag with a customer's name on it. It's rude. And because
Mr Yano told me once that some tags are private." She looked at Aoi. "But I read it later. I
went back and read it, after Mr Yano got quiet. It said a name and a year and a place, and the
year was 1958, and the place was Yanaka, and the name was Kuroda."

Aoi wrote it down. Then she asked the question she asked every witness, which was the only
question she had that ever produced anything.

"Did he say anything else. Anything that didn't fit."

Emi thought about it for a long time. Then she said: "He said the coat had been well kept. He
said, 'Someone kept this well.' And then he said, 'That's the trouble with your trade. You
keep things you should give back.'"

Aoi asked who he had meant.

"I thought he meant Mr Yano," Emi said. "Now I think he meant all of us. Anyone who keeps a
thing another person died holding."

[IMAGE 3.5 — the back room of the reuse shop: a young part-time worker seated beside the
daishō stand, a notebook on her knee; the stand's tag is turned to the wall; the shop's front
window, out of focus, shows a street.]


---

Yano's account ends in the chair, and what follows is Aoi's, assembled from the notebook he
kept and from the ledger Aoi found on his counter, and it is short, because a shopkeeper in
Ueno does not leave much behind but a ledger and a chair.

The notebook was forty pages of stock. He wrote in it every day he owned the shop: what came
in, what went out, what he paid, what he sold it for. The last eleven pages are different.
They are the coat's chain — every owner of the grey Shōwa coat, traced backwards, from the
estate to 1958 — and Yano had found four owners, and all four were dead, and he had written
beside each one the year and the cause, in the same small hand a man uses for a price.

The causes were: illness, 1974; a fall, 1988; illness, 2003; illness, 2019. And then, in the
margin of 1958, where the chain began, he had written something that was not a cause and not a
price.

He had written: *the man the coat belonged to. the one who drowned at Inokashira. the coat was
his, and it came back, and it keeps coming back.*

Aoi sat in the reuse shop in Ueno with the notebook and understood that Yano had solved the
coat completely, three weeks before he died, and had done the thing every honest shopkeeper
does with a thing he cannot sell: he had priced it, written it down, and left it in the shop
for the next person.

He had also, on the last page, drawn a small diagram. It was a map of the coat's journey, and
the journey was a loop, and the loop began at Yanaka and ended at Yanaka, and the coat had
spent sixty-five years going around it, from hand to hand, while a woman in a house at the
centre of the loop bought it back every time it reached her.

[IMAGE 3.6 — a stock notebook open on a shop counter: eleven pages of a traced chain, each
owner dated and caused; a margin note at 1958; on the last page, a rough drawn loop from
Yanaka back to Yanaka; the shop's grey light.]

# The Music Box

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The box played eight bars and stopped. Sakai let it stop. He had not asked it to play. He
had wound it because that is what you do with a cylinder box, and he had turned away to
write the lot number, and behind him the box had begun the tune a child would know.

He was forty-six and he had lived in Tokyo since university, and the tune was not a Tokyo
tune. He did not know how he knew that. It was the way the fourth bar went up where a
Tokyo tune would go down, the way the words would fit if there were words.

He knew it the way you know a smell.

[IMAGE 4.1 — a cylinder music box on a table, lid open, brass cylinder visible, a small
tag tied to the winding key. The comb and pins are in focus; the room behind is dark.]

The tag said *SAKAI — 1958 — YANAKA.*

He had bought the box at the estate in Kiyosumi-Shirakawa because it was cheap and the
wood was good. He had not read the tag until he got it home. He read it now and felt the
particular cold of a coincidence that is aimed at you.

---

Sakai was a sound engineer. He had spent twenty years recording things that other people
wanted remembered — wedding speeches, testimony, the last voicemails of the dying — and he
had a professional interest in the mechanics of memory. A music box was, to him, a
recording. A cylinder with pins, a comb with teeth, a tune that could not be changed. The
earliest sound recordings were cylinders. You could hold a cylinder in your hand and it
held four minutes of a dead person's voice, and you could play it, and the voice would come
out of a horn, and the person would be neither alive nor gone.

He had recorded his father once, at the end, for a project he never finished. He had not
played the file in nine years.

His father had been born in Yanaka. His father had never spoken about Yanaka. When Sakai
was a boy there had been a trip, once, on a day his father would not explain, to a lane
behind Yanaka Ginza where an old woman lived who gave them tea and did not smile, and
afterward his father had driven home without speaking and had never gone back. Sakai
remembered the tea more than the woman. The tea had tasted of the tin it came in.

He had asked, once, in the car on the way home, who the woman was, and his father had said,
"A customer," and Sakai had known, even at eight, that "customer" was the answer you give
when the true answer is a door you do not want a child to open.

The box played the tune again. He had not wound it.

[IMAGE 4.2 — the same box photographed from directly above: the interior lid carries a
faint hand-drawn diagram, lines and rectangles, the shape of a small room with a chest
marked against one wall.]

---

He turned the box over and opened the interior lid, which is where the maker's label
usually sits, and instead there were pencil lines: a small room, six mats, drawn with the
care of someone copying a memory. In one corner, a rectangle labelled with a single
character he could not read at first because the pencil was faint. He held it to the lamp.

*Tansu.*

He did not know why the room made him afraid. It was only a room. He had been in a hundred
six-mat rooms. But the drawing was from above, and slightly off-square, the way a child
draws a room they have looked at from the floor.

He set the box on the shelf and went to bed, and the tune came through the wall from the
sitting room, four bars at a time, patient.

---

His phone rang at two in the morning. It was the estate, Kanzaki Kei, and he sounded like a
man who had been awake for a long time.

"You bought the box," Kei said.

"Yes."

"You've played it."

Sakai did not answer.

"Don't play it again," Kei said. "I'll buy it back. Double. Whatever you paid."

"Why."

Kei was quiet. Then he said: "Because you've read it now."

Sakai, who had spent twenty years asking people questions for a living, heard the thing
Kei was not saying, and asked the question he would have asked any witness.

"What did your mother do with the box," he said. "In the room. In 1958."

Kei made a sound that was not a word. Then, in a voice that had gone very small, he said,
"She played it. She told me. She said it was the last thing that played in that room before
the men came, and she was behind the chest, and the box was on the shelf, and it played,
and she could hear it over everything. She said it was the only thing that stayed nice."

"Stayed nice."

"While the rest of it happened," Kei said. "She said the tune stayed nice. She's been
buying the boxes back for thirty years. There were four of them. You have the last one."

[IMAGE 4.3 — a hallway at night: the music box on a shelf, the tag legible, a child's
silhouette at the far end of the hall seen only as a shadow on the wall, the photographer's
own shadow missing.]

---

Sakai hung up and sat in the dark and did the thing he did with every recording: he
listened for the part that did not belong.

He did not play the box. He did not need to. He had heard it eight times now and he had it,
the way he had every sound he had ever recorded, in the part of memory that is not memory
but mechanics. And what he heard, now that he listened properly, was that the eighth bar
did not resolve the seventh. It went up where it should go down. It was not a mistake in
the cylinder; it was a different ending.

He had heard the tune before. Not from the box. From the trip to Yanaka. The old woman had
hummed it, at the door, when she said goodbye, and his father had stopped in the lane and
listened to the whole thing before he got in the car.

The woman had been telling them something. Sakai understood that now, thirty-eight years
late. She had not given them tea to be kind. She had been checking whether his father still
remembered, and the tune was the test, and his father had passed it by standing still in
the lane.

Sakai went into the sitting room. The box was on the shelf, closed.

He opened it, and wound it, and let it play.

Eight bars. Then a pause. Then, faintly, from the cylinder, in the way a cylinder sometimes
catches a voice if someone spoke over it when it was being cut, a woman's voice, old and
flat, saying a single word.

The word was a name. It was not his father's name. It was the name his father had been
given by the family that raised him, before he changed it, and it was the name Sakai had
never known, and hearing it come out of a wooden box in his own sitting room at two in the
morning, he understood that the box had not been waiting for the last owner.

It had been waiting for him.

[IMAGE 4.4 — a hand-drawn map of the Yanesen lanes, in pencil, showing Yanaka Ginza, Nezu
Shrine, and a lane that runs behind them and is marked with a small tansu symbol and the
date 1958.]

---

He drove to Yanaka the next morning, before he could think better of it. He had the box in
a bag on the passenger seat. He found the lane. He found the house, or the place where the
house had been: a small lot with a new building on it, three floors, clean, no trace of the
old thing.

A woman was watering plants at the door. She was not old. She did not know the name. She
had bought the lot from a family that had sold it empty, years ago, and she was sorry, and
she hoped he found what he was looking for.

Sakai stood in the lane and looked at the new building and understood that the room was
gone, and that the objects were the only part of it left, and that someone had been
collecting the parts for sixty years, and that he now held one, and that this was the
reason the tune had come for him.

He went home. He put the box on the shelf and turned it so the tag faced the wall, the way
you do with a thing you have priced and cannot sell and do not want to look at.

The next morning it had turned itself around.

The tag, small and upright, faced the room, and the lid was open, and the comb was warm.


---

Sakai came to Aoi's flat in Tateishi in November, because Aoi had called him about a cylinder
box and because Sakai had spent a month listening to a tune and had decided, at last, that he
should hear it with someone else in the room.

He brought the box in a cloth. He set it on the low table. He did not wind it.

Aoi, who had spent the day pricing an estate in Nezu, made tea, and asked him what he wanted
to say, and he told her the story I have set down in full above: the tune, the father, the
lane behind Yanaka Ginza, the old woman at the door who hummed it, the voice on the cylinder
saying his father's first name.

Then he said the thing that he had not told anyone, and that he had come to Tateishi to say.

He said: "The tune isn't a lullaby. I finally placed it. It's a *counting* song. It's the song
children sing in some parts of the country while they count something they don't want to
look at. You sing four bars, you count one thing, you sing four bars, you count the next."

Aoi asked him what the box was counting.

"I don't know," Sakai said. "There were twelve people in that room and the tune has twelve
bars if you play it through, and the box only ever plays eight, and I think it's stuck. I
think someone stopped it at eight."

He wound it, then, once, at Aoi's nod, and the box played its eight bars, and stopped, and the
silence that followed was not empty.

[IMAGE 4.5 — a low table in a Tateishi flat: the open music box between two cups of tea; a
woman's hand rests beside it; the box's cylinder is visible through the lid, its pins catching
the lamplight; outside the window, the alley and a laundromat sign.]

# Frame Two — The Estate

---

I took the job because the mirror told me to.

That is not true. I took the job because Kanzaki Kei called me three days after the sale
and said he had two hundred lots still in the warehouse in Kuramae, unsold, and would I
appraise them, and I said yes before he finished the sentence, because a mirror had walked
into my mother's flat in 2003 and walked out again wearing a stranger's name.

Mikami told me not to. He said it twice, once on the phone and once in the workshop, where
he was repairing a joint on a tansu and would not look up.

"You thought this was about a mirror," he said. "It's about a queue. You don't want to know
where the queue leads."

"I know where it leads," I said. "Yanaka. 1958."

He put the tansu down.

---

I have not written down, until now, what I know about my mother, and I find that I have
been avoiding it, and that the avoidance has a shape, which is the shape of the year 2003.

My mother was named Shirai Naoko. She was thirty-four when she died. She worked in a
laundry in Tateishi and we lived in a two-room flat above a bar, and the bar was loud until
two and then it was not, and I learned to sleep in the space after two. She was not ill
that I remember. She was tired, which I took for granted, because everyone in Tateishi was
tired, and then one week she did not get up, and then she did not wake up, and the doctor
said a word I did not know and would not explain to a child of nine.

What I remember is the mirror. Black lacquer, a dressing mirror on a stand, with a crack
down the glass that I was told was there before I was born. It stood in the corner of her
room. She used it every morning. When she died, the landlord came and changed the lock
while I was at my grandmother's, and by the time I was allowed back, the mirror was gone,
and so was everything else, and I was told that things like that happen and that I should
not ask.

I asked for one year. Then I stopped. I have not thought about the mirror as a fact for
twenty years; I have thought about it as a mood, the way you think about a smell.

My grandmother raised me. Her name was Hana. She died when I was fourteen, and she never
once spoke about my mother's death, and the only thing she ever told me about the mirror
was that it was old and came from her family, which told me nothing, because I did not know
what her family was.

She used to say one thing, though. When I asked where things came from, she would say:
"Everything in this flat came from somewhere, and everything that came from somewhere came
from someone, and some of the someones you do not want to meet."

I thought it was a saying. Grandmothers have sayings.

---

Kei was waiting at the warehouse.

He is forty-nine and he looks like a man who has spent his life moving furniture out of a
room he was not allowed into. He showed me the lots. Two hundred tags. Every lot tagged,
every tag the same hand, and every tag carrying a name, a date, and a place, and none of
them the name of anyone I had ever met, until he handed me a cassette recorder and said his
mother kept ledgers too, and did I want to hear the last one.

On the tape, Kanzaki Mitsuko, seventy-eight, reads a purchase from 1991. Then she stops
mid-sentence. Then, very quietly, she says one line to herself, not for the tape:

*"It was me. It has always been me. But she said it was her, and I let her."*

[IMAGE 5.1 — photograph inside the Kuramae warehouse: two hundred tagged lots on shelving,
a single hanging bulb, the tags catching light; in the foreground, a cassette recorder on a
crate, its counter at 000.]

I rewound it. I played it again. I played it a third time and wrote the words down, because
that is what I do with things that do not fit: I put them in a column and wait for the
column to make a shape.

The shape was this. A confession, made to no one, by a woman who had spent her life
collecting. *It was me.* And then: *she said it was her.* Someone had taken the blame.

Behind me, Kei said the thing that turned the book over in my hands.

"Your name's in there," he said. "On a lot you already sold. That means my mother had
already met you. Before you were born."

---

I told him that was impossible. He agreed with me. He said it in a way that suggested he had
been waiting a long time for someone to whom he could say the impossible thing out loud.

"She started in 1961," he said. "That's the first tag. She was fourteen. She bought a tray
in Asakusa, and she wrote a name on it, and she never stopped. Fifty-eight years. Two
hundred and eleven things. I grew up in that house and I was not allowed to touch any of
them, and I was not allowed to ask why, and when I did ask, she would say the same thing."

"What thing."

Kei looked at the floor.

"She'd say, 'Because it's not finished.'" He looked up. "It's finished now. She's dead. So
why are the buyers dying?"

I asked him what he meant, and he told me, and I have written it below exactly, because from
that moment the account stops being about a mirror.

He said the sale was in April. He said there were thirty-one buyers, and that he had kept
their names because he is the kind of man who keeps sales records. And he said that in the
six months since, four of the thirty-one had died.

"Four," I said.

"Four," he said. "And they were all in the hall at the same time. I checked. I have the
attendance sheet."

He handed it to me. Thirty-one names, in his hand, with the amounts paid. Four of them had
a small mark beside them, a dot, and I asked what the dot was.

"The date they died," he said. "I started marking it in June. My mother marked her ledger
the same way. I didn't understand why I did it until I saw the marks in her book."

[IMAGE 5.2 — the attendance sheet: a handwritten list of thirty-one buyer names with
amounts; four names have a small dot beside them with a date; the sheet is photographed on
the warehouse floor beside the tagged lots.]

---

I stood in the warehouse and looked at two hundred tagged objects and thirty-one names with
four dots, and I did the arithmetic that I would have done for any collection, which is
provenance.

Four deaths in six months, out of thirty-one buyers. That is not a coincidence you can
appraise away. Something in that hall, or something before it, had put those four in the
same column.

I asked Kei the only question that mattered.

"Did your mother ever buy anything back? After she sold it?"

He thought about it. He said yes. He said she had bought back eleven things over the years,
always quietly, always through a third party, and she had never explained, and he had found
the receipts after she died, and he had not understood them.

"Which eleven," I said.

He said he did not know. He said the receipts used a different name each time, and he had
thought it was a habit, or a shame about the money.

And then I understood the thing that I have been carrying since, which is this: Kanzaki
Mitsuko did not sell her collection. She sold it, and then she bought back eleven pieces,
and then she died, and now the buyers are dying, and every piece she bought back was a
piece she could no longer let go of.

She was not a collector. She was a woman taking back the parts of a room, one at a time,
from people who died, and the dying had already started before I got there, and the only
question left was why the room was worth the deaths.

I told Kei I would take the job. I told him I would appraise the lots and find out what the
tags meant. I did not tell him about my mother, or the mirror, or my grandmother Hana,
because by then I had begun to suspect that the name Shirai on a tag was not a coincidence,
and I did not want him to know that I was, in some way I could not yet prove, part of the
estate.


---

I want to set down the month of May, because it is the month I stopped being an appraiser and
became, instead, a person doing research, and the distinction matters to what happened later.

In May I read everything I could about the estate without asking anyone. I read the sale
listing, the lot photographs, the terms. I read, twenty times, the two sentences Kei had
given me: *she'd say, 'because it's not finished,'* and the tape's confession, *it was me, it
has always been me, but she said it was her.* I made columns. I made a timeline of the four
buyers who had died and the four dates they had died, and I noticed that the dates were one a
month, and that they were in the same order as the sale's checkout sequence, which is to say
the order in which people had paid.

The deaths followed the till roll.

That is the kind of fact an appraiser notices and a detective misses, because a detective
looks for a motive and an appraiser looks for a *sequence*, and the sequence was the thing
that carried the meaning. Whoever had the attendance sheet — and only Kei and I had the
attendance sheet — could read the order of the deaths off the order of the purchases, and the
order of the purchases was the order of the queue.

I called Kei and I asked him who else had seen the attendance sheet.

He said: "Nobody. I made it after the sale, from the receipts."

I asked where the receipts were.

He said: "In the storage room. With everything else." And then, after a pause, he said the
thing that told me the room was not a room: "I mean the storage room in the house. The one
with no door."

[IMAGE 5.3 — a kitchen table covered with papers: a sale listing, a till roll, a handwritten
timeline of four deaths; red pencil joins each death to a line on the till roll; a cup of
coffee has gone cold.]

---

I asked him to go to the house and look at the wall.

He did not want to. He said he had not been back since March. I said I would pay him for his
time, which is the only way I know to ask a person to do something they are afraid of, and he
said it was not about money, and I said nothing, and he went.

He called me that night from Yanaka. He said the wall was there. He said it had been replastered,
recently, within the year — he could tell by the colour, and by the smell, and because his
mother had replastered it every few years for as long as he could remember, "the way you paint
a door you don't want anyone to open."

He said he had put his hand on it, and it was cold in a rectangle, and he had knocked, and it
had sounded hollow, and he had sat down on the floor of the passage and cried, at forty-nine,
because he had grown up with a room he was not allowed to enter that was not on any plan, and
because his mother had died in March, and because the wall was the only thing she had ever
kept from him.

Then he said: "There are keys in the house. A ring of them, on a hook by the front door. They
were not there when I cleared it in April."

"What do you mean they weren't there."

"I cleaned that house," Kei said. "I took everything. There was no ring of keys on the hook by
the front door on the day I emptied it, I would swear to it. There is a ring of keys on the
hook now."

I did not tell him, then, what I already suspected: that the keys had not appeared. That the
keys had been put there — by someone who wanted a locksmith to find them, and who knew that a
locked room with a key ring is the one thing a man in that trade cannot leave alone.

[IMAGE 5.4 — a timber passage in an old Yanaka row-house, evening: a plaster wall with a
faint rectangular coldness; on a hook by the front door, a ring of keys, the oldest brass;
the passage floor carries the marks of a person who sat down and stood up.]


---

I want to write down the June night I first dreamt of the room, because everyone in this
account has dreamt of it and the account should record what it is like before it is believed.

I dreamt I was lying on a floor of six mats, low, at the height of a child, and the room was
full of objects on the walls, and through the doorway a woman stood with her back to me. She
was not looking at me. She was counting, and the count was one I could follow, and the count
was the objects, and then the count stopped, and she turned, and she had no face.

That is the first dream and it is the one everyone has. The faceless woman counting. Mikami
had it in October. The screen restorer had it in December. The locksmith wrote it down before
he went to Yanaka, though I did not find that until later, in his van, on a page torn from a
notepad.

The dream is not a warning. I have decided that. A warning would name the danger; the dream
does not; the dream only establishes that you have been *entered in the count*, and that the
count is in you now, the way a price is in an object once you have said it out loud. You cannot
un-know a count.

I did not tell Mikami about the dream when he told me about his. We each held it and did not
compare, which is, I have come to think, exactly what the room wanted — a room full of people
holding the same thing and not comparing.

[IMAGE 5.5 — a dark bedroom seen from the floor, low angle, child's eye height: the edge of a
bed above, a doorway opposite, a faint room laid out on the floor in the dark; the composition
is deliberately low, as if the viewer is lying down.]

# The Keys

*Live POV. Present tense.*

---

The keys are on a ring the way a janitor keeps them, oldest in front, and the oldest is
brass and worn smooth, and I am holding it up to the door of a room that is not on the plan
of this house.

I am a locksmith. That is not a confession; it is a skill. I am here because a client in
Yanaka called and said there is a room in my house I have never been able to open, and I am
here because I have been doing this for twenty-two years and a room you cannot open is the
only interesting thing left in the trade.

The client is behind me. He is polite and he smells of money. He has not told me his name.

The house is old, a timber row-house off a lane behind Yanaka Ginza. The plan the client
gave me shows a storage room off the kitchen. The wall where the storage room should be is
a wall. There is no door in it, and no seam, and when I put my palm on it the plaster is
cold in one long rectangle the size of a doorway, as if the cold remembers where the hole
used to be.

[IMAGE 6.1 — photograph of a narrow timber passage in a Yanaka row-house: a plaster wall,
a hand pressed flat against it; beside the wall, a floor plan drawn by hand with a storage
room marked, and the wall on the plan crossed out in ink.]

---

I have opened a thousand doors and I can tell you that a wall is not a door.

That is the first thing they teach you. A wall is a wall; a door is a hole with a lid. You
cannot pick a wall, and if a client tells you a wall is a door, the client is confused, or
the client is lying, or the client has lived in a house a long time and has begun to
remember a version of it that was never built.

I told him this. I said the plan and the wall did not agree, and that plans are often
wrong, and that the honest thing was to call an architect, not a locksmith.

He said, "Try the keys."

I said the keys were keys to other doors, presumably, and he said, "Try them in order. I
want to see which one fits."

I want to record that I knew, even then, that this was not a locksmithing job. But a client
with money and a story and a ring of old keys is the whole of my trade, and I have a
curiosity that has cost me, over the years, exactly nothing, until today.

So I tried the keys, in order, oldest first, and I want the reader to understand what that
is: a man standing alone in a cold passage of an empty house, pressing key after key
against a wall that has no hole, and finding, each time, nothing. Twenty-two keys. Nothing.

Then the twenty-third.

---

The key went in.

I want to describe this exactly, because I have described it to the police and to my wife
and to a man in a bar, and every time I describe it I lose something, so I will write it
here while it is intact.

The key went into the plaster where there was no hole. I felt the wards turn — that small
resistance, the give, the click that is the whole of my life and the only sound I love. And
the wall, which had no seam, opened inward on a hinge I could not see, and the cold came
out of it like a held breath.

Behind it was a room of six mats with no windows.

The walls were lined, floor to ceiling, with objects, each with a tag. Lacquerware, a
mirror, a coat on a hook, boxes, fans, a tea set, a doll, a comb, dozens and dozens of
things, and every one of them tagged in the same small upright hand, and every one of them
placed, not stored — placed, like a room that someone had left and locked and intended to
come back to.

And in the middle of the floor there was a place where a person was meant to stand.

I know how that sounds. A mat is four surfaces. There are no places, in a mat room, where a
person is meant to stand. But I understood it the instant I looked in — the objects were
arranged around a gap, and the gap was the size of a standing person, and the objects all
faced it, the way furniture faces a bed, or a stage, or a grave.

[IMAGE 6.2 — the interior of the room: walls lined floor-to-ceiling with tagged objects
(mirror, coat, boxes, tea set, doll, combs); in the centre of the six mats, an empty
standing-place; the doorway's light falls on the floor in a long rectangle.]

---

I said, "There's a room."

The client did not answer.

I said it again, and turned, and he was closer than he had been. He had taken off his
shoes, which I had not heard him do, and he was standing at the threshold with his hands
folded, looking past me at the room the way a man looks at a house he has bought and not
yet entered.

I am going to do the thing I have trained my whole life not to do: I am going to step
backward out of a strange room.

The client stepped forward. I stepped back. He said my name — my actual name, not the name
on the job sheet — and I understood that the call had not come from a client, and that the
house had not been on the market, and that the ring of keys had been waiting on a hook by
the front door, all twenty-three, exactly as I found them.

"You found it," he says, pleased. "Good. I couldn't, when it was my turn."

"The turn," I say.

"Everyone gets a turn," he says. "Yours is the keys."

I do not know what that means, and I am not going to find out, because he is reaching past
me now — his arm, past my shoulder, to the inside of the door — and the oldest key, the
brass one, is still in the lock, and he takes it.

He steps back into the passage. He says, kindly, the way you speak to a man who has done a
small job well:

"Thank you. You can go."

The door closes.

The key turns from the outside.

[IMAGE 6.3 — from inside the room: the closing door, a slice of corridor light narrowing;
a man's hand at the lock; beyond him, in the corridor, the client's folded hands; on the
wall beside the door, tags beginning to catch the last light.]

---

I want to tell the reader what the room does, because a locked room is not, as it turns
out, a puzzle. It is a place.

The first thing it does is go dark, and then the dark stops being dark, and you can see
again, by no light you can name, and the objects on the walls are all around you and they
are all looking at the gap where you are standing, which is the gap you have walked into,
which means the gap was for you.

The second thing it does is get smaller. Not the walls; the room. The six mats contract.
You can watch the mat edges move, in the way you can watch a clock tick if you stare, until
the room is the size of a person, and then the person is the room.

I am still holding the ring of keys. Twenty-two of them are useless and one of them is
gone, and I am talking aloud, because if I stop talking I will have to listen, and I have
begun to hear the thing I would have to listen to: not a noise. A count. Something in the
room is counting, and it is counting the objects, and it has not finished, and I understand
that when it finishes, the count will be the number of people the room is for, and I am one
of them, and the gap I am standing in was always the last entry.

My hand is slower than it was. The keys in my fist have gone cold, all of them, at once,
like a fist of water.

I am writing this on the phone, and the screen is the only light that is not the other
light, and the phone shows the room the way it was when the door opened: six mats, walls of
tagged things, and in the centre, me.

I am in the photograph. That is the thing. I am in the photograph now.

The client, on the other side of the door, says through the wood:

"Read the tag on the mirror when you can. It'll explain the rest. It explains all of you."

Then I hear him walk away down the passage, unhurried, and I hear the front door of the
house open, and close, and lock, and after that there is nothing in the house but the
counting, and the objects, and the gap, which is shrinking.

[IMAGE 6.4 — the phone's screen in the dark: a live camera view of the six-mat room from
where the narrator stands; the walls tagged; the gap in the centre now occupied by the
narrator's own silhouette; the doorway behind is closed.]


---

I want to describe what the room counted, because I have begun to understand that the
counting is the whole of the account and not a detail of it.

It counted the objects. That was first. It went around the walls in a set order — I could hear
the order, because the count came from each object in turn, like a person touching each thing
and saying its name — and the order was not the order of the walls. It was the order of the
twelve.

Twelve names. The twelve names on the tags. And as the count passed each object, I understood,
in the way you understand a filing system, that each object was one of the twelve, filed
under a name, and that the twelve had been in the room together in 1958 and had not been
together since, and that the count was putting them back.

Then the count reached the end of the twelve and started again, and the second pass was
different.

The second pass counted *me*.

I am standing in the gap. The gap is the thirteenth. The count went to twelve and then it
reached me, and the number it said was not a name, because I do not have a name in this room.
The number it said was a *number*. Thirteen. And when it said thirteen, everything in the room
was quiet, and stayed quiet, and I understood that a counting that reaches thirteen has
finished, and that a finished count is a kind of permission.

The client, through the door, said something I could not hear.

I am writing this in the gap, with the phone at six percent, and I am going to write until the
count does whatever a finished count does, and I want whoever reads this to know that I did
not run, not because I was brave, but because a locksmith knows a thing about doors that no
one else knows: a door that has been opened from the outside is not, by the time you are on
the inside of it, a door any more. It is a wall with a history. And you do not run at walls.

[IMAGE 6.5 — the phone screen in the dark, low battery: a notes app open, the text of a first-
person account; behind the screen, in the room, the walls of tagged objects are in shadow; one
blank tag lies on the mat in front of the narrator's feet.]


---

I have to keep writing while I can, because the count is in me now and I can feel it moving the
way you feel a price settle.

I want to say one thing about locks, because I am a locksmith and this is my only chance, and
because the thing I know about locks is the thing the room is.

A lock is not a secret. A lock is a *promise*, and the promise is: this door will hold until
someone who knows how it works comes along. Every lock in the world is defeated the same way,
which is not force but *patience* and *the right shape*. You do not break a lock. You find the
shape it was made to admit, and you become that shape, and it opens, and it thanks you.

The room is a lock. That is what I have understood in the gap. It was made to admit a shape,
and the shape is a person who reads, and the room has been waiting sixty years for the right
shape, and every person who touched a tagged thing was a *key* — tried, and some tried, and
some turned partway. The dead were keys that broke. The filed were keys that fit a false
tumbril and held. And the last key, the one the lock was cut for, is the one who can read the
whole room, and that is not a locksmith and it is not a dealer.

It is a person who prices the dead for strangers, because that person has learned, over eleven
thousand flat, to hold a thing without wanting it, and to read the history of a thing without
needing to be in the story, and that is the only shape the room was ever cut to admit.

I am not the key. I have known that since the door closed. I was the *pick* — the one used to
test the shape, the one that fits a moment and then is set down. And I am writing this so that
whoever comes next knows what the lock is, and does not mistake the pick for the key.

[IMAGE 6.6 — a ring of keys in a dark hand, torchlight from below: twenty-two keys and one
empty hook where the twenty-third was; the keys are old, brass and iron; behind them, a door
that is a wall, with a cold rectangular shadow.]

# The Ledger

*Close third, past tense.*

---

Mikami read the ledger with gloves on, which was habit, not ritual; a bound account book
from the sixties is held together by its own dirt.

Two hundred and eleven entries. Mitsuko bought all her life, one object at a time, and she
wrote down for each the purchase, the price, the place, and, in a column she did not label,
a date that was not the purchase date.

[IMAGE 7.1 — the open ledger on a workbench, two facing pages: columns of purchases in a
small upright hand; one column, unlabelled, holds dates that do not match the purchases;
the earliest entry reads 1961.]

The purchases ran from 1961 to 2019. The unlabelled dates ran from 1961 to 2023, and the
last one was the year she died.

Mikami checked the first entry against the object. A lacquer tray, bought in 1961 in
Asakusa. The unlabelled date beside it was 1961 as well.

"It's not the purchase date," he said to Aoi, who was sitting on the workbench with a
notebook. "It's the date the seller died."

She looked at the ledger, then at him.

"You don't know that."

"Look at the pattern. She buys in 1961, the seller dies in 1961. She buys in 1974, the date
is 1974. She buys in 1991—"

He stopped, and turned the book, and pointed at the top of the page, where the columns were
ruled. The ledger ran to two columns on every line, and only one of them was ever labelled.
The ruled heading over the first column read *bought*; over the second, the heading was a year,
and the years in it ran one ahead of the purchases, and the year at the very top of the second
column was not a date at all.

"There are two years on every tag," he said. "The one the object remembers and the one the
person came to. The front is where the object was, in 1958, in the house. The back is when the
holder arrived — when they died, or when they were filed, or when they were left with the piece.
She ruled the column for it in 1961 and never named it, because naming it would have made it a
death list, and she needed it to be a distribution list."

He stopped.

[IMAGE 7.2 — a detail photograph of one ledger line, magnified: a purchase of a lacquer
comb in Sendagi, dated 1991; the unlabelled column reads 1991; in the margin, in pencil, a
single small character that looks like a tally.]

---

Aoi had been in the trade eleven years and she had learned to read a document the way she
read a room: not for what it said, but for what it was arranged around.

The ledger was arranged around a gap. Every purchase had an object, a price, a place — and
then the unlabelled column, which had nothing to do with the money. She turned the pages
and watched the gap widen. In 1961 the unlabelled dates matched the purchases. By 1970 the
purchases had begun to run ahead of the deaths, and by 1990 the deaths had begun to run
ahead of the purchases, and by 2000 there were entries with no object at all — a name, a
date, a place, and nothing bought.

"She wrote down people she never bought anything from," Aoi said. "Why."

Mikami did not answer. He was looking at the comb entry, and his face had gone the way a
face goes when a man has found his own name in a document he did not write.

"What happened in 1991," Aoi said.

"Nothing," said Mikami. "I restored a comb in 1991."

The shop was quiet. Outside, in Sendagi, a bicycle went past on the lane.

"So she knew you," Aoi said.

"She was a customer. She brought things to be mended. She never told me where they came
from, and I never asked, because you don't ask a customer, and because—"

He stopped again, and this time he did not start again for a while, and when he did, his
voice had changed.

"Because in 1991 I finished a repair on a comb, and the woman who owned it died the week I
returned it, and I told myself it was a coincidence, and I have told myself that every year
since."

[IMAGE 7.3 — a photograph of a workshop wall: dozens of repair tickets on strings, each
dated; one from 1991 is centred and legible; beside it, a small photograph of a woman
receiving a parcel, her face out of frame, her hands accepting the comb.]

---

Aoi wrote it down. She had been writing it down for two hours, and she realised she was
writing it down the way Mikami mended: to have a record, in case. In case of what, she did
not let herself finish.

She asked him to go through the ledger with her, page by page, and they did, and it took
until midnight, and by the end they had a table in her notebook: two hundred and eleven
objects, two hundred and eleven names, two hundred and eleven dates of death, and a
pattern.

The pattern was a chain. Each name on a tag was the previous holder of an object, and each
death cleared the object for the next purchase, and each purchase moved the object one step
closer to a place that was never written down.

"She's assembling something," Aoi said. "In order. Over sixty years. She has been buying
back the same room."

Mikami said nothing. He had known, Aoi understood later, since 1991; he had known and had
not let himself know, which is a different kind and a heavier kind.

She asked him one more question. She asks it now, in this account, knowing what it cost:

"Mikami. If the tags are the names of the dead, whose names are on them?"

He did not answer.

The ledger answered instead, on the last page, in the same small upright hand, a list of
twelve names that were not in the purchase columns at all.

They were not owners. They had bought nothing. They were there in a single column, dated
1958, with a place: Yanaka.

The twelfth was hers.

---

Aoi put her finger on the twelfth name and did not lift it for a long time.

The name was *SHIRAI*. Beside it, in the unlabelled column, a date that had not yet
happened, written in pencil, and rubbed out, and written again.

"She wrote my death date," Aoi said. "Before I was born."

Mikami shook his head. "She wrote a name and a date in a column of deaths," he said. "For
someone who was not dead yet. That's not a death date. That's a schedule."

"That's worse."

"Yes," he said. "That is considerably worse."

Aoi closed the ledger. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she had expected them not
to be. She looked around the workshop — the tickets on the wall, the bench, the drawers, the
tools — and she understood that she was standing in the middle of a document, and that the
document had her name in it, and that it had been there for twenty years, waiting for her to
open the drawer.

She said, aloud, to no one in particular, in the way she talked herself through a valuation:

"The room is not the point. The room is a clock. And I am the last hand."

Mikami looked up sharply, and Aoi, who had spent the day learning to read a dead woman's
handwriting, did not notice that she had said it in the present tense.


---

They came back the next morning, because Aoi had not slept and because Mikami had, and because
the ledger had a page neither of them had turned.

It was the inside cover, and it was not a page they had skipped; it was a page that had been
*glued down* and then, at some point, worked loose at one corner. Mikami found it when he
lifted the book to put it away: the cover board lifted slightly, and under it, on the board
itself, in the small upright hand, there was a line that had been written before the book was
bound.

It said: *if you are reading this, the room has been opened. I am sorry. I could not leave it
written on paper. So I wrote it in the room.*

Aoi looked at the line for a long time. Then she looked at Mikami.

"She wrote the room," Aoi said. "The room is the document. The ledger is only the index."

Mikami said nothing. He was looking at the line, and at the child's hand in it, which had been
eleven when it was written and had not changed in sixty years, which is the thing about a
hand: it is the last thing to change, and the first thing to be recognised.

[IMAGE 7.4 — the inside cover of the ledger, lifted from the board: a single line in a small
upright hand, written directly on the board, visible where the cover paper has come loose; a
magnifying glass rests on the page.]


---

Aoi went back to the ledger alone on the third night, because Mikami had gone home and because
she had begun to have a thought she did not want him to watch her have.

The thought was this: if the unlabelled column was the date the seller died, then the ledger
was not only a record of the room. It was a record of the *killings*. And if the killings had
begun in 1961 and continued to 2023, then either Mitsuko had killed a great many people over
sixty-two years, which is impossible for a woman in a house in Yanaka, or she had been told
the dates, by someone who knew them, which meant she had an *informant*, and the informant was
still alive, and the informant was the hand that had written the steadier lines on the backs
of the tags.

Aoi wrote the two hands side by side in her notebook, from the documents she had: the small
upright hand of the tags and the ledger, a child's hand, sixty years old on the first page and
steady by the last; and the second hand, steadier throughout, the one on the backs — *still
here?*, *the debt is not the name*, *she's you*, *thank you for keeping her*.

The second hand was not a child's. It was an adult's, and it was *not old*, because Aoi had
seen a great deal of old handwriting and the second hand had no tremor, no fading, no drift.
It was a hand in its forties or fifties and it had done all of the cruelty in the account.

Mitsuko had built a room. But Mitsuko had not written *still here?*. Mitsuko had not written
the line on Yano's photograph. Mitsuko had not ordered the deaths.

Someone had done all of that, in a steady hand, for thirty-five years, at the direction of a
woman who was called, by the district and by the trade, a harmless old collector of Yanaka.

Aoi sat with the ledger and understood that the book had one author and two hands, and that
the older hand was the *testimony* and the younger hand was the *enforcement*, and that the
whole thing had been a partnership, and that the partner — the hand — was still out there, and
was, by the dates, about to reach the end of the list.

[IMAGE 7.5 — two documents photographed side by side, raking light: a tag in the small
upright hand, and the reverse of a tag in a steadier hand; a magnifying glass between them;
the two hands are visibly different in age and pressure.]

# Interlude — The Slip

---

I am writing this at Mikami's bench in Sendagi because my flat no longer feels safe and
because his shop has a lock I do not have to think about, and because he has gone home and
left me the kettle and the light.

[IMAGE 7.1 — a repairer's bench at night, a single lamp: a notebook open under the light, a
kettle steaming on a small gas ring, shellac tins and brushes pushed to the edge; the shop
beyond the lamp is deep shadow; the bench shows the ghost-rings of many cups.]

I want to record the hour, because I have started to distrust my own sense of time. It is
just after one. The lane outside is empty. The shop smells of shellac and cold tea, which I
have described before in this account, and which I am describing again, because I find that
I want the ordinary things written down before I lose them.

Here is the thing I have not been able to say in the pages before this one, and it is the
reason I have taken the ledger home with me: I do not think I am going to survive this.

I do not mean that as fear. I mean it as an appraisal.

I have spent eleven years pricing things, and the trade gives you one gift, which is
judgement without attachment. You look at a lacquer box with a cracked lid and you see the
crack, and you see the box, and you do not confuse the two. I have been looking at this
account the way I look at a box. There is a crack in it. The crack is not me. But the crack
runs from the first page to this one, and I have begun to think it was there before I
started writing, and that I have merely been tracing it.

The ledger says my name. The tags say my name. The mirror came back to me. A woman I never
met, dead in March, arranged her whole estate around my mother, and my mother's mirror, and
my mother's death, and she has been dead for six months and she is still arranging.

[IMAGE 7.2 — the open ledger on the bench under the lamp: two facing pages of a small upright
hand, one unlabelled column of dates; on the verso, a name written, rubbed out, and written
again, the paper furred from erasure; a thumb rests on the line, holding the page flat.]

I keep thinking about the boy at the estate. Kei. He said his mother never wrote anything
down about the room — the dimensions, she never wrote them. She only ever bought, and
tagged, and arranged. Which means the room only exists when someone stands in it and
measures. Which means she has been waiting, for sixty years, for someone to stand in it.

I think that someone is me. I think I have been walking into that room since the day I read
my mother's name on a dead woman's tag, and I think I will keep walking into it, because
that is what the account is. The account is a corridor. I am writing my way down it.

The kettle is boiling.

I do not remember standing up to fill it.

I am at the kettle now, and the water is going in, and the sound of it is the only sound in
the shop, and I am aware, with a clarity that has become ordinary this month, that I am
describing my own actions a half-second after performing them, as if I am reading a page a
moment before I write it.

Here is the sentence I have been circling:

The account is in the present tense now.

I did not decide to write it that way. I noticed it at the top of this page and I have not
been able to make it go back, and every time I try to write "I sat" it comes out "I sit,"
and every time I try to write "I was afraid" it comes out "I am afraid," and I have
decided, because I am a professional, to stop correcting the record.

You will have to take this as I take my inventory: the document says what it says. If the
tense has changed, then the tense has changed, and there is a reason, and I am no longer
sure that the reason is a grammatical one.

Somewhere in this shop, in a drawer I have not opened, are thirty years of repair tickets.
Mikami showed me one from 1991. He did not show me the rest. I have not asked to see them
because I already know what I will find, and I am not ready, tonight, to be right.

[IMAGE 7.3 — a shallow drawer pulled open a hand's width, seen from above: packed paper repair
tickets with dates in the corner, a bound stub-book, a rubber stamp; the drawer's runners
show bare wood; the light comes from the lamp behind, leaving the drawer's far end dark.]

The water is in the pot. I am pouring it. The tag on the tea caddy is turned away from me,
the way Mikami turns the tags of his own things, out of respect or out of fear, I have never
asked which.

I am going to finish this account. I am going to find the room. I am going to measure it,
because measurement is the only thing I know how to do, and because a room that has waited
sixty years for someone to stand in it has, I think, already chosen who.

I am standing in it now. That is the sentence. I have written it, and it is true, and I am
not going to cross it out.

[IMAGE 7.4 — the notebook page itself, close, under the lamp: a line in a firm upright hand,
the last two words freshly written and the ink still wet; above it, the same line struck
through twice and corrected; the page's shadow falls across the ledger beside it.]


---

I am going to record one more thing before I stop writing in the past, because I have realised
that the past tense is where I have been hiding, and an appraiser does not hide; an appraiser
discloses.

The thing I have been hiding is this: I knew about the deaths before I understood the tags,
and I did nothing, and the reason I did nothing is that I had already begun to want the room.

I noticed the pattern in May. Four deaths, one a month, in the checkout order. I noticed it
and I did not call the police, because I did not have a crime, and because I did not want to
be a person who had called the police about a coincidence, and because — and this is the
disclosure — I did not want to be *removed from the room*. I wanted to be the one who
understood it. I wanted the valuation.

That is what the trade does to you, if you are good at it and not careful. It teaches you to
want the answer more than the object, and then it teaches you to want the answer more than
the people, and then one day you are standing in a dead woman's warehouse at midnight with a
tape measure, and you are not afraid of the room. You are *curious* about it. And curiosity,
in a room like that, is the same as consent.

I have to write the rest of it, because I have been careful with every disclosure but this one.
The want did not begin with the mirror. It began when I was nine, on a landing, outside a door
a landlord had locked against me. I decided that year that I would be the one who held the key,
and I have been holding keys ever since — other people's flats, other people's locked rooms,
other people's dead. The room in Yanaka is the first door I have ever been *offered*, and I do
not know if what I feel when I stand at it is the child's want to be let in, or the child's
want never to be locked out again, and I am afraid they are the same want, and I am afraid I
will not find out which until I have opened it.

I did not consent to the deaths. I want that on the record. But I consented to the *room*, and
the room had a place for me, and it had been waiting for someone who would come to it
*curious* and not *afraid*, and I had walked in on my own two feet, with a notebook.

That is the last honest thing I can write in the past tense. The rest is present, and the
present is where the room is.

headed with a date; beside the hand, the ledger lies open; the shop beyond the lamp is dark.]

# The Album

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The album was not in the sale. That is what made Suzuki keep it.

Everything else from the Kanzaki house had a tag. The album had none, and it was the only
thing in the whole estate that the dead woman had not priced, and Suzuki had bought it for
two hundred yen because he collected photographs of old rooms and because an untagged thing
in a tagged house is a hole in the record.

He was a proofreader. He spent his days finding the one wrong word in a page, and he
recognised the feeling of a document with something missing.

[IMAGE 8.1 — an open photograph album on a low table: black-and-white prints corner-mounted
on card; the album's board cover is water-stained; a slip of paper between two pages is
blank.]

---

Suzuki had collected photographs of rooms for nine years. He was not interested in people.
He was interested in wallpaper, and mat edges, and the way a household arranges a shelf,
which is the most honest document a family ever produces, because no one thinks anyone is
reading.

He had four hundred photographs. He kept them in boxes, by prefecture. He could date a
photograph to within five years by the height of the light switch and the shape of the
door handles.

The album broke his system. It was fifty prints of the same room, and the room was not in
any Tokyo he knew. The prints had been taken over twenty-seven years, from 1962 to 1989,
and in every one the room was the same: six mats, no window, walls lined with objects. What
changed was the objects. In 1962 there were four. In 1989 there were dozens.

That is not a room. That is a collection. And the collection grew exactly the way a
collection grows when a person is buying one thing at a time and placing it, not storing
it, for a purpose.

[IMAGE 8.2 — a single album page: three photographs of the same six-mat, windowless room,
taken from slightly different angles; the objects on the walls differ between the three
photographs.]

---

Suzuki laid the prints in order, on his own kitchen table, and he did what a proofreader
does, which is to set up a comparison and wait to be told he is wrong.

He was told he was wrong within an hour.

The camera did not move. Across twenty-seven years, fifty prints, the viewpoint was
identical — same corner, same height, same angle. A collection photographed by its owner
over decades would drift; a person stands in different places on different days. This was
not drift. This was a fixed point.

Suzuki measured it. He was a proofreader; he had a ruler, and a good one, and he counted
the mats in the earliest print: six, standard, and he knew the standard dimensions because
he had spent nine years photographing rooms.

On the earliest print, the horizon of the floor cut the frame at a particular height, and
that height, against the six mats he could count, put the camera between forty and fifty
centimetres above the tatami.

A child of eleven, sitting behind a tansu, looking into a room she was not supposed to see.

[IMAGE 8.3 — a hand-annotated contact sheet: the same print reproduced, with ruled overlay
lines and a handwritten calculation in the margin dating the camera height to a child's eye
level; the tansu is circled.]

Suzuki sat back and looked at his kitchen, and then at the photographs, and then at his
kitchen again.

An adult does not photograph her own collection from forty-five centimetres for
twenty-seven years. If the photographer was the collector, the collector was a child. If
the collector was a child in 1962, she was born around 1951 — which made her eleven, if the
room dates from 1958, and eleven is exactly the age at which a child hides behind the
tansu, and exactly the age at which the tag list in the ledger begins.

Suzuki had not seen the ledger. He did not need to. The album was the ledger's other side:
one recorded what she bought, the other recorded what it looked like from the floor.

---

There was one photograph that was not from 1962 or from 1989. It was loose at the back, and
it had been folded and unfolded many times, and it showed the room the way it looked on a
night, before it was emptied: the objects gone, the walls bare, and in the doorway, two
people, and on the floor between them, a man who was not standing.

Suzuki turned the print over. On the back, in the hand he had spent three days learning:

*He was going to. I stopped him. Do not look for the blame in her.*

[IMAGE 8.4 — the folded 1958 print: a bare six-mat room; a man on the floor; in the doorway,
the blurred figure of an adult and, behind her, partially hidden, the small outline of a
child; the print is visibly creased and repaired with tape.]

Suzuki read those two lines perhaps twenty times. He was a proofreader. He could not stop
himself from doing what he did with every sentence: he looked for the wrong word.

The wrong word was *her*.

*I stopped him*, she wrote. Not *we*, and not *my mother*. *I* stopped him. And then: *do
not look for the blame in her.* Which is what you write when you have taken the blame
yourself, or when you are trying to move it.

Suzuki had spent his life finding the one wrong word. He found it, and it told him that a
child of eleven had written a note on the back of a photograph of a dead man, and had kept
it, and had folded and unfolded it for sixty years, and had been buying the room back ever
since.

He did the thing that he has never been able to explain to himself. He photographed the
album, all fifty prints, in order, and he photographed the note, and he put the photographs
in an envelope, and he addressed the envelope, in his careful proofreader's hand, to the
one name he had found in the sale that seemed to be alive and looking for an answer: the
estate buyer, Shirai Aoi, whom he had met once, at the Kanzaki hall, when she priced a
cabinet and he stood behind her and said nothing.

He never posted it. He bought a stamp, and he put the envelope on the shelf by the door,
and he told himself he would post it in the morning.

The morning, when it came, was not one he was able to use.

[IMAGE 8.5 — a proofreader's flat at night: an envelope on a shelf by the door, stamped; on
the table behind it, the fifty prints laid out in a grid; the camera's viewpoint print is
centred and circled; a chair is pushed back from the table.]


---

Aoi went to see Suzuki's flat in November, after the envelope, and the neighbour let her in
because Aoi said she was a colleague and because neighbours in Nezu are kind to people who
ask about the dead.

The flat was small and clean and had four hundred photographs in boxes, sorted by prefecture.
The police had taken the album. They had taken the loose 1958 print and the note. They had not
taken the boxes, because four hundred photographs of other people's rooms are not, on their
face, evidence.

Aoi went through them anyway, because she had learned by then that the thing the police take
and the thing that matters are often two different objects.

She found it in the Shizuoka box, at the back, where it should not have been, because Suzuki
sorted by prefecture and a Tokyo room does not belong in Shizuoka.

It was a photograph of a woman in a doorway. Young, maybe nineteen, in work clothes, standing
in the doorway of a house she did not own, and beside her on the floor, wrapped in cloth and
tall, something the height of a standing mirror.

On the back, in Suzuki's precise proofreader's hand, the one line he had written before he
died:

*This is the only photograph he kept of his mother. I asked him why there were no others. He
said she kept the mirror instead.*

Aoi sat in a dead man's flat in Nezu with a photograph of a servant in a doorway and
understood, at last, whose grandmother the nurse next door had been.

[IMAGE 8.6 — a photograph held in two hands: a young woman in the doorway of an old house,
work clothes, no smile; beside her a cloth-wrapped bundle the height of a mirror; on the
reverse edge of the print, a line in a careful hand.]


---

Aoi wrote to Shizuoka, to Suzuki's family, because a proofreader who found the one wrong word
deserves a letter even if he cannot receive it, and because Aoi had begun to write to the dead
as a way of keeping the account straight.

The family wrote back. They were kind and confused. They said Suzuki had been a quiet man who
photographed rooms and had never married and had called his mother once a week until she died
in 2019. They said he had seemed, in the last year, to be working on something, but that he had
never said what, and that they had found, among his things, one box labelled in his careful
hand with a single word.

The word was *UNREAD*.

The family did not know what it meant and had thrown the box in the move, and Aoi asked, in the
letter, whether anything from the box had survived, and they said no, and then, in a second
letter, they said actually yes, one thing, because it had been on the shelf not in the box: a
single sheet of paper, ruled, on which Suzuki had written a list.

They copied it out and sent it to her. It was a list of ten sentences, and each sentence was a
thing Suzuki had read in the Kanzaki documents and could not unread, and he had written them
down in order of how much they frightened him, and the last one, at the bottom, in the most
careful hand in the whole account, was this:

*The room has a place for the reader. I am a reader. I have not gone to Kuramae. I will not go
to Kuramae. But I have read the room, and the room knows.*

Suzuki had known, three weeks before he died. He had known and had written it down and had not
gone, and it had come to him anyway, at the top of a stairwell, in the wet, in the shape of a
polite man with folded hands, and Aoi understood that the room did not require you to come to
it. It required only that you read it, and then it came to you.

[IMAGE 8.7 — a ruled sheet of paper, a proofreader's list of ten sentences, the last
underlined twice; the paper is held against a window; behind it, the blurred light of a Nezu
street; a stamp lies on the table beside it, unused.]

# Frame Three — The Method

---

By October I had a folder, and the folder had a method, and the method was the only thing
that made the nights bearable.

[IMAGE 9.1 — a cardboard folder open on a workbench, seen from above, each document on its own
sheet: a ledger leaf, a torn photograph, two tags, a repair ticket, a register extract; a
pencil and a loupe lie across the edge; the light is a single bench lamp.]

I am an appraiser, and an appraiser does not solve a crime; an appraiser prices a collection.
So I stopped trying to solve the room and I started to price it, and the pricing told me
everything I had not been able to see.

Here is what I had.

Eleven stories. Eleven people who had held a tagged thing, or been named on one, or come
close enough to the room to be given a tag of their own. Four of them were dead. Two —
Mikami and Kubo — had been *filed*, which was Kubo's word, and I had adopted it because it
was exact: kept alive, kept silent, kept in place like an object on a shelf.

And there was a document. That was the thing the folder finally held: not a theory but a
document, or a set of documents, that did not care what I believed.

The tags were documents. The ledger was a document. The album was a document. The register
was a document. The repair tickets were documents. Dozens of them, in three hands — Mitsuko's,
mostly; Hana's on the 1991 ticket; and a third hand, steadier, that had written the line on
the back of the comb and on the back of Yano's photograph — a hand I had begun to call, in
the folder, the hand of the *came-after*, because the child's list in the doll had a
thirteenth name below a line, *the one who came after*.

I did not know whose hand it was. I know now. But in October I did not, and I want the
record to show how close I got and how wrong I was at the same time.

[IMAGE 9.2 — three tags side by side on a dark bench, macro: the same small upright hand on
each, a name, a year, and a district; on the third the strokes are visibly steadier, the
pressure different; a finger enters the frame at the edge, not yet touching.]

---

Mikami and I laid the folder out on his bench, one document to a page, and I did what I do
for any collection: I made a catalogue.

Two hundred and eleven objects. Two hundred and eleven names. Two hundred and eleven dates.
And when I sorted them the way I sort any estate — by year, by place, by the order they
were acquired — the shape that came out was not a room.

It was a *route*.

Every object had come from someone who lived within three kilometres of Yanaka, except
eleven. Those eleven had come from farther out — Mitaka, Fuchū, Kasukabe — and every one of
the eleven was a person who held a *document* rather than an object: a genealogist, a
registrar, a dealer in signed pieces.

"She wasn't collecting the room," I said. "She was collecting the *proof*. The objects were
the room, but the eleven were the paperwork. You, Mikami. You kept the repair tickets.
Hoshino keeps the registers. Ito kept the blades, and blades have tangs, and tangs carry
marks. She bought silence by owning the people who filed things."

[IMAGE 9.3 — the catalogue spread: a long sheet ruled into two hundred-odd lines, each a name,
a year, and a district; eleven lines ringed in pencil; a hand-drawn route on a map beside it,
three roads running out to Mitaka, Fuchū, and Kasukabe; a cup has left a ring on the corner.]

Mikami looked at the catalogue and said the thing I had been avoiding.

"Then you're the twelfth."

---

I told him he was being dramatic.

He said he was being technical. He said: count the eleven. Count the people who hold
records. Then look at the twelfth column in the ledger, the name she wrote and rubbed out
and wrote again, dated forward, in a handwriting that was steadying, page by page, as if the
writer were getting better at it.

"I don't hold a record," I said. "I have a mirror."

"You have the mirror," he said. "The mirror is the weapon. A weapon is the first document.
The first thing anyone wants is the thing that did it."

I did not answer him. I was looking at the twelfth column, at the rubbed-out date beside my
own surname, and I was doing the arithmetic I had been avoiding since April.

If the eleven were the people who held the paperwork, then the twelfth was not a witness and
not a record. The twelfth was the *conclusion*. The twelfth was the person who, when the
room was assembled, would stand in the gap and make the count come out even, because a
collection of two hundred and eleven things arranged around a space is not complete until
the space has someone in it.

"She didn't tag me to kill me," I said.

"No," Mikami said. "She tagged you to finish it. There's a difference. You've been finishing
it since the day you read your mother's name."

---

I called Kei that night and asked him one question, and it is the question that took the
account from a mystery to the thing it became.

I asked him: who found his mother.

He said he did. He said he came to the house in March because she had not answered the phone
for a week, and he found her in her chair, in the front room, in the ordinary way people are
found, and there was no sign of anything, and the doctor said her heart, and it was written
down as her heart.

Then he said something else, because he is the kind of man who tells you the whole of a
thing if you ask, which is how I had learned to use him.

He said: "She had a tag on her. In her hand."

"Her own tag."

"Her own tag," he said. "With her name and her year and this place. And I burned it, because
I thought it was the last thing that needed to be done, and because I didn't want it in the
sale."

I asked him what it said.

[IMAGE 9.4 — a charred tag held on an open palm: mostly gone, one surviving corner with two
legible characters and the year 1958; the ash has smudged the lines beneath; the hand holding
it is unsteady, out of focus at the edge.]

He said he did not remember the exact characters. He said it was her name, and the year 1958,
and the place Yanaka, and below that, one more line, in a hand that was *not* hers — he was
sure of it, because he knew his mother's hand better than he knew anything — one line that he
read once and then burned.

"What line," I said.

He was quiet for a long time.

"It said 'one left,'" he said. "It said, 'one left,' and then there was a name, and the name
was yours. But I burned it because I thought it was a shopping list, or a mistake, or her
being ill. It was March. I didn't know."

And there it was. The line, the last line, the one I had been walking toward since April,
spoken out loud on the phone by a tired man in a house in Yanaka who had burned the only
document that said my death was scheduled.

I thanked him and hung up and sat in the dark of my flat.

One left.

I did not sleep that night, and in the morning I began to write the account the way you write
an inventory: carefully, in order, and without knowing, yet, that I was writing it in the
past tense because the person who writes in the present tense is the person who is still
alive to be doing it.

The account, as you have it, was never written looking back.

It was written looking forward, from a chair, in a room, while something counted.


---

I am going to add one more document to the folder, because I found it in November and because
it is the strangest thing in the account and the most ordinary.

It is a receipt. A dry cleaner's receipt, from a shop in Nezu, dated 2003, for one item: a
mirror cover, cleaned. One hundred and twenty yen. Paid in cash.

I found it in a box of my mother's papers that I had never opened, in a cupboard in my flat,
where it had sat for twenty years, because when a parent dies and you are nine and you are
passed to a grandmother, you do not open the box; you put the box on a shelf and you move it
from flat to flat and you never open it.

The receipt has a phone number on it. I called the number. A woman answered; the shop is still
there, in Nezu, and it is still run by the same family, and the daughter of the man who wrote
the receipt listened to me describe a black-lacquer dressing mirror with a crack in the glass
and a cloth cover, and she said, before I finished, "The mirror with the tag."

I asked her what she meant.

She said her father had told her about it. He had told her that a woman used to bring a mirror
cover in, every few years, to be cleaned, and that the cover had a tag sewn into the lining,
and that she had asked him once what the tag said, and he had said it was a name and a year
and a place, and that the name was not the name of the woman who brought it.

He said the woman who brought it was not the owner. He said she was keeping it for someone.

[IMAGE 9.5 — a page from the folder held at an angle: the twelve-column ledger leaf, the
twelfth column dated forward and rubbed out beside a surname; a hairline scratch runs across
the page where the pen pressed too hard; the bench lamp throws the spine's shadow across the
columns.]

[IMAGE 9.6 — a dry cleaner's receipt, 2003, for one mirror cover, one hundred and twenty yen,
cash; on the reverse, in a hand that is not the shop's, a name and a year and a place; the
receipt is creased from being carried in a wallet.]

My mother was not the owner of the mirror. My grandmother Hana was not the owner. The mirror
went from hand to hand down a line of women who each kept it for the next one, and it was
never anyone's, and the tag on the cover named a woman in Tateishi and a year in 2003 and a
place, and that woman was my mother, and she died, and the line arrived at me.


---

I am going to write down the arithmetic of the route, because I have the notebook in front of
me and because arithmetic is the last honest thing I can do.

Eleven people held the room's proof. Four are dead: Yano, Sakai, Ito, Suzuki. Two are filed:
Mikami, Kubo. Two are filed in the softer sense, which is to say they have the folder and are
waiting: Hoshino, Yamamura. One took the room and left: the screen restorer's screen. One is not
in the folder at all and should be, and I have added her: the nurse, Sakaguchi Rie, who is next
door to my flat and who knocked on my wall three times in November and whom I did not answer,
because I thought it was the building, and because I had begun, by then, to not answer doors.

That is eleven people, and eleven is not the number on the list. Nineteen tags. Nineteen living
names. Which means eight of the nineteen have not appeared in the folder, and I do not know who
they are, and the hand does.

I have tried to work the eight out from the tags, and I cannot, because the tags give only a
surname and a district, and because whoever wrote the list chose the surnames the way she chose
everything: so that they would read as a seating chart and not as a phone book.

But I have worked out one thing from the arithmetic, and it is the thing that decided the end.

Nineteen names. Twelve from 1958, which gives seven names that are not from the room at all.
Seven names with no ancestor on the guest list. Seven people who are in the collection because
of something else.

I counted my own family. My mother. My grandmother. The mirror. That is three of the seven if
you count the line rather than the person, and the seven does not come out even, and an
appraiser does not let a column not come out even.

Which means the list has *two* kinds of people on it: the room's twelve, and the route's seven.
The room's people are the testimony. The route's people are the *delivery* — the ones who carry
the pieces, one per month, to the reader. And I am both: I am Hana's granddaughter, which puts
me on the route, and I am the reader, which puts me in the gap, and if the list has both kinds
of people on it, then the list was never a death list.

It was a *distribution list*.

She was not killing nineteen people. She was *mailing the room*, one package a month, to
nineteen addresses, and the deaths were the deliveries, and I was the last address, and the
package was the account you are reading.

[IMAGE 9.7 — a hand-drawn grid of nineteen names in two columns, twelve marked "room" and
seven marked "route," the columns joined by arrows; one name sits in both columns; a note
reads "distribution, not deaths"; the grid is drawn on the back of a sale listing.]

# The Tea Set

*Live POV. Present tense. A narrator killing.*

---

I set the cups out while the water heats, because it matters that the cups are the right
way round, and the right way round is the side with the tag facing the wall.

Five cups. One for him, one for me, three for the people he buried, because I am not
superstitious, I am thorough, and a man should drink with the room he emptied.

The tea set came from the sale in Kiyosumi-Shirakawa in April, one of two hundred lots
tagged by a dead woman in a hand I have now learned to read. Mine said *TOYAMA — 1958 —
YANAKA*. Toyama was my grandfather. He was in the room that night. He was not the man on
the floor and he was not the woman in the doorway. He was the fourth person, the one
nobody counts, the one who was paid to keep the other three quiet, and he did keep them
quiet, for sixty years, until the quiet killed him.

[IMAGE 9.1 — a kitchen counter, close: a five-piece tea set arranged in a row, each cup
with a small handwritten tag tied to its handle, tags turned to face away from the camera.
Steam is just beginning to rise.]

---

I will tell the reader who I am, because the cup facing the wall has my grandfather's name
and the cup facing the room has mine, and I want to be clear about which is which.

I am Toyama Reiko. I am forty-one. I work in a bank in Asakusa and I have never hurt
anyone, and this afternoon I am going to kill a man, and I have been preparing it the way
you prepare a transfer: in order, in silence, with everything reconciled.

My grandfather was Toyama Kenji. He was a driver for Kuroda Genji, who was a patron in
Yanaka, and who was a bad man in a way that no one in the family would name. My grandfather
told me one thing, once, when I was fourteen and he was dying, and he told it the way you
hand someone a live coal: quickly, with both hands, and then you let go.

He said: "In 1958 there was a night at the Kanzaki house. I drove Kuroda there. I drove
home alone. I was paid to say I was not there, and I have said it for thirty years, and I
am telling you now because I do not want to die with only his version."

He did not say what happened. He did not have to. A man who is paid to say he was not there
is a man who was there, and a girl of eleven who hides behind a chest is a girl who saw
something, and a patron who leaves a house at night and does not come out of the pond at
Inokashira until spring is a patron the world decided not to look at.

And my grandfather, who drove, and said nothing, and took the money, was the reason no one
ever looked.

The water is ready.

[IMAGE 9.2 — a hand tipping a measured spoonful of white powder into one cup, photographed
from above; the powder dissolves; four other cups are already filled; a tag hangs visible
on the poisoned cup's handle, reading TOYAMA, 1958, YANAKA.]

---

I have known the man I am going to kill since I was a girl.

His name is Kuroda Jun. He is the grandson. He came to my grandfather's house at New Year
and he brought the kind of sweets you bring when you want to be remembered, and he was kind
to me, and he was the reason my grandfather never slept, and when my grandfather died he
came to the funeral and stood at the back with his hands folded, and I watched him fold his
hands, and I understood, at fourteen, what hands are for.

He called me three weeks ago. That is what began it. He called me, after thirty years, and
he said he was gathering the pieces of his grandfather's story, and he asked whether my
family still had anything from 1958, and I said no, and he said, "That's a shame. Your
grandfather's tag came up in the sale. I'm buying it back. I thought you'd want to know."

He said it kindly. He said it the way he has said everything, for thirty years, kindly.

And I understood that he had been doing this the whole time — buying the pieces back,
quietly, keeping the story his grandfather's version — and that my grandfather had been a
piece, and I had been a piece, and that the kind man at the funeral with his folded hands
had been tidying up the family history the way you tidy a room before guests arrive.

The sale taught me the rest. I bought the tea set because of the tag, and I read the tags
of the whole estate online, all two hundred of them, and I saw the pattern: the names of
the dead, in order, over sixty years, arranged around a room in Yanaka. And I understood
that the dead woman had spent her life undoing exactly what Kuroda Jun was spending his
undoing. She was rebuilding the truth. He was dismantling it. And they had been doing it,
the two of them, for as long as I have been alive.

He arrives on time, which is the one honest thing about him.

---

He is old now and careful and he still folds his hands, and he tells me I have grown, and
I tell him the tea is from a tin my grandfather kept, which is true.

He asks about the cups with the tags. I tell him it is a new thing I do, an inventory, a
habit from my grandfather's trade — Kenji drove, and drove carefully, and kept a log of
every trip, and I have inherited the habit of recording. Kuroda Jun smiles and says the
Kanzaki sale has made everyone an archivist.

He drinks. He says it is very good.

I drink mine, which is clean, because I intend to be here for the rest of it.

He asks me, after the second cup, why the cups have tags. I tell him the truth. I tell him
that everything in this house is tagged now, because a dead woman in Yanaka spent sixty
years teaching the world to read. I tell him his name is not on any of them yet, and that by
tonight, in a manner of speaking, it will be.

He understands before the third cup. I watch it reach him the way a name reaches you across
a room — he sets the cup down, and then he reaches for it again, and then he stops, and he
looks at the tag, and he reads it, and he says one word, which is my grandfather's name.

"Yes," I say.

[IMAGE 9.3 — the low table seen from the doorway: two men's cups and three untouched cups;
one man seated, upright, both hands flat on the table; the narrator's hands are in the
frame, holding a teapot, steady; the room is warm and ordinary.]

---

He asks me if I am going to watch. I tell him I have been watching him since I was fourteen.

He asks me what I want. I tell him the truth, which is that I want the version. I want him
to say, out loud, in this room, that his grandfather attacked a child in 1958, and that the
family knew, and that they paid to bury it, and that my grandfather took the money and said
nothing, and that every kind New Year sweet for thirty years was the interest on a silence.

He does not say it. He sits with his hands flat on the table and the room goes quiet around
him the way rooms do. He says one thing, which is: "You'll be caught."

I point at the third cup, the one facing the wall, the one I did not fill for him.

"I'm not the only one with a motive," I say. "The whole estate is full of people the room
touched. There were thirty-one buyers in that hall, and four of them are dead, and everyone
who ever held a tagged thing has a reason to want the Kurodas gone. The police will spend a
year on the tags. Every tag points at the room, and the room points at no one, and a room with
no one in it is exactly what a curse is. Nobody looks at a tea set."

It was a good assumption, and it was wrong, and I did not live long enough to learn how wrong.
I did not know there was a folder. I did not know an appraiser had spent nine months training
herself to read a dead woman's hand, or that five deaths closed separately would one day be
laid on one table and made to match. I understood the room better than the man across from me,
and I understood the *police* not at all, and that is the whole of my mistake.

He looks at the five cups and understands, finally, that I have made him one of two hundred
and eleven objects, that his name will go on a blank tag tonight, that the room he spent
thirty years emptying has a space in it shaped exactly like him, and that I am not the
collection.

I am the collector now.

He does not finish the cup. He does not have to. He sits with his hands flat on the table
and the room goes quiet around him the way rooms do, and I pour the last of the tea into
the three cups for the people he buried, and I sit down, and I wait.

[IMAGE 9.4 — the tea set photographed after: four cups emptied, one full; the seated man is
slumped forward, face down; a hand is reaching in from the frame's edge to turn the tag on
his cup face-up, so the name shows — TOYAMA, 1958.]


---

I am going to write down what I knew and when I knew it, the way you write a bank ledger,
because I have spent my life making the numbers reconcile, and I will not stop now.

I knew the tea set's tag was my grandfather's in April, the day I bought it. I knew the estate
had two hundred and eleven lots before the end of April, because the listing was public. I
knew the pattern of the names before the end of May, because I read the tags the way the
killer read them: as a seating chart.

What I did not know until June was that the man I was going to kill had already begun to buy
the pieces back.

I found that out from a dealer. I had gone to a shop in Nezu, to a man who appraised sword
fittings, because a tea caddy from the estate had come to me through a cousin and I wanted it
valued, and the dealer told me, without prompting, that a Kuroda had been in and bought back
a stand with two blades, and had asked after every other tagged lot he could name.

The dealer said: "He's collecting the estate. He's buying it out from under whoever holds it.
He's been doing it for twenty years."

That was the fact that decided it. Not the room. Not the tags. The fact that the man was
buying the proof, one piece at a time, so that when the old woman died and the estate sold,
there would be nothing left to read but his version.

I could not out-collect him. I am a bank clerk. But I could take the one piece he could not
buy back after I was done: the man himself. You cannot buy back a cup you have drunk from.

[IMAGE 9.5 — a tea caddy on a counter in a shop in Nezu: a small ceramic caddy with a cloth
binding and a handwritten tag; a middle-aged woman's hand rests beside it; the shop shelves
behind hold dusty boxes.]


---

I have one more thing to write and then the tea is ready.

I wrote to her in 1989. Let me begin there, because the beginning is the only part of this that
anyone will be able to check.

My name is Toyama Reiko and I am a hand. There is more than one of us; that is a thing I did
not learn until this year, and it is the reason I am writing this down.

That is the thing I have not said to anyone, and I am going to say it here, because this
document will be found and because I am not going to be caught, and because a bank clerk knows
how a ledger is closed: last entry, no error, sign nothing.

My grandfather drove Kuroda Genji to that house in 1958 and was paid to say he was not there,
and I found out in 1989, from a letter, and the letter was from Kanzaki Mitsuko, and the letter
said: *I saw your grandfather that night. He was the only one who looked at me. He knew, and
he said nothing, and I have not blamed him, and I have kept his name for sixty years. Now I
need a hand.*

I wrote back. I was twenty-six. I was a bank clerk and I was good with lists and I had a
grandfather who had never slept. She asked me to do one thing: to follow the list, and to clear
each name when its date came, and to keep the room from being read by anyone who was not meant
to read it. She said the list was the room, and the room was a testimony, and a testimony is
read once and then it is true.

I said yes.

She divided the work the way you divide a ledger. The living names were mine: the ones who
had held a piece in their hands and could still be talked to, and talked out of it. The ones
who kept the *records* were handled by someone else — a second correspondent, she never said
his name, only that he was owed the same thing my family was owed, a silence paid down. I
never met him. For thirty years I believed I was the only hand; this year I found his
signature on the backs of tags I had never written, in a hand steadier than mine, and I
understood that the old woman had kept us apart the way she had kept everything apart — so
that no one of us would ever know the whole of it.

I want to be exact about the deaths, because the exactness is the only thing I have left that
is mine. They were not murders in the way the reader is expecting. Every one of them was a
*conversation*, and every one ended in an explanation, and the explanation is the same in
every case: I told them what the room was, and what their tag said, and what their family had
done in 1958, and they died of the telling. Every one. The doctor wrote heart, or fall, or
illness, and the doctors were not wrong. You can die of a truth you have been avoiding for
sixty years. I have been present for every one I was sent to, and each one went quiet at the
last, the way a person goes quiet when a sum finally comes out. It is faster than poison and
tidier.

Except the cup. I did use the cup, tonight, for Kuroda Jun, and I want to say why, because it
is the one death that was not clean.

He was not on the list. He was never on the list. He was the man who had been buying the pieces
back, and the old woman had not accounted for him, and he had begun, this year, to read the
room himself. He was going to expose all of it. He was going to take the testimony and use it
to clear his own family — to say, look, my grandfather was attacked, the Kanzakis lied, we are
the victims — which is the one reading of the room that would make it worth a death.

So I made him the thirteenth. I gave him a cup and a tag and a place in the room, and I did it
with the tea set from the estate, because the room should close with its own objects in its own
hands.

The tea is ready.

[IMAGE 9.6 — a two-column list in a steady adult hand on a bank ledger sheet: names, dates,
one a month; nine lines are struck through; the tenth line is a man's name; the sheet lies
beside a tea service.]

*[The following note is in a second steady hand, and was filed with the account. It is not in
the bank clerk's.]*

*One correction, for the reader, because a ledger should close clean. The bank clerk took the
man Kuroda as the room's last word. He was not. He was the room's loudest nuisance and the
Kurodas' last attempt to buy their name back, and she gave him a cup for it, and I let her,
because a hand should not know the whole of the list. The room's last word is a reader, not a
Kuroda, and the reader was not hers to serve and not mine. She died believing she had closed the
account, and that was the old woman's one mercy, and it was mine to keep.*

# The Comb

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The comb had been mended in 1991 and the woman who owned it had died in 1991, and Mikami
had known both facts for thirty-three years without ever putting them in the same sentence,
which is the way a person carries a thing they do not want to look at.

He opened the ticket drawer on a Tuesday.

Aoi was not with him. He had not told her he kept the tickets. It was one thing to read a
ledger and another to have your own name in it, in your own hand, in a drawer you have
opened every week for thirty years without reading.

[IMAGE 10.1 — a workshop drawer open to the light: hundreds of repair tickets on loops of
string, stacked; the camera isolates one ticket, dated 1991, for a lacquer comb, with the
owner's surname legible as HANA in a small upright hand.]

---

Mikami had mended for thirty-eight years and he had a rule, which was that he wrote a name
on every ticket. He wrote the name of whoever brought the thing, because a thing without a
name is a thing you cannot return, and the whole trade of mending is returning. He had
tickets going back to 1986. He could open the drawer and read the last four decades of
Sendagi in his own hand.

He had never once looked for Mitsuko's name in it. He had looked for it that morning, and
found it twenty-two times, and found beside it, on one ticket from 1991, a name he had not
thought about in thirty-three years: Hana.

Not a customer's name he remembered. A name in the same hand as the tags, which meant the
ticket had been written not by him but by the person who brought the comb, and left, and
returned for it, and died.

He called Aoi. He said: "Your grandmother's name was Hana."

Aoi was quiet. She had known the name for six days, since the album, and had not said it,
because saying it would make it real.

"She brought the comb to me in 1991," Mikami said. "She paid in cash. She asked me to fix
the tooth. She asked me to write nothing on the ticket. I wrote HANA because I always
write a name; it's how I know a job is done."

"And she died."

"The week I gave it back."

[IMAGE 10.2 — a lacquer comb on a bench, restored, one tooth replaced in slightly different
lacquer; beside it, a receipt book open to a page where the name HANA appears in pencil, in
the workshop's own hand.]

---

Aoi asked him the question she had been circling for a week.

"Why did she bring it here. Why your shop, in Sendagi, out of all of Tokyo."

Mikami had thought about that for thirty-three years, on and off, the way you think about a
draft in a room. He said: "Because your mother lived in Sendagi then. With Hana. Three
streets from me. I didn't know. I didn't know any of it, then."

Aoi did the arithmetic. If Hana died in 1991 and Aoi's mother lived in Sendagi with her in
1991, then Aoi's mother had been there when the comb was returned. When Hana died. On the
street Mikami walked to work for thirty years.

"They knew where to look," Mikami said. "That's what frightens me. Whoever wrote those tags
knew where everyone would be. She knew where I was. She knew where your family was."

Aoi did not answer. She was looking at the comb, and she had begun to see a thing about the
trade that Mikami, who was a mender and not an appraiser, had never had to see: a collection
of two hundred and eleven objects is also a collection of two hundred and eleven addresses.
Every name on a tag was a place someone lived, in a year, and the places were all within a
few kilometres, and the years had been chosen, one by one, so that no two people who held a
single object's parts ever met.

Except in that estate sale. Except for the thirty-one buyers. Except for the four who died.

"She didn't just reconstruct a room," Aoi said. "She reconstructed a neighbourhood. Sixty
years of it. She put every witness in a place where the others couldn't reach them, and
then she waited, and then she sold it all at once, and the room met itself."

[IMAGE 10.3 — a street map of Sendagi and Yanaka with a route drawn in pencil: from a
timber row-house to a small apartment block three streets away; a date, 1991, and two
names, MIKAMI and HANA, at either end.]

---

He took the comb out of the drawer and turned it over.

On the back of the comb, in the same hand, small and upright, there was a tag tied with
cotton thread. It was the only tag among the two hundred he had never seen, because it had
never been in the sale.

It read: *MIKAMI — 1991 — SENDAGI.*

The date was not the date he mended it. It was the date the owner died.

"I've been tagged," Mikami said, and set the comb down very carefully, as if the bench
might take his name off it if he moved too fast.

Aoi picked up the comb. She held it the way she held everything, at the edge, as if it were
already priced, and she read the tag, and then she read the back of it, where there was one
line in pencil.

It said: *he kept the ticket. he will keep the room.*

Mikami saw it and put both hands on the bench and stood there for a while, the way a man
stands when he has been told the date of something and has decided not to argue with it.

"She knew I'd keep it," he said. "She knew I'd keep every ticket. She knew I'd keep the
ledger. She wasn't tagging me to kill me. She was tagging me to file me. I'm not a victim."

"You're a record," Aoi said.

"I'm a record," he agreed, and there was something in his voice that Aoi had not heard
before and would hear only once more, in Kuramae, at the end, which was the sound of a man
who has just discovered that his whole careful life was a piece of evidence someone else
was relying on.

[IMAGE 10.4 — the underside of the comb box: a label pasted inside, in the small upright
hand, listing eleven dates between 1991 and 2023, each with a name; the last line is blank
except for a place — SENDAGI — and no date.]


---

Aoi went to Sendagi alone the next week, to the address the comb had come from, because Mikami
had given it to her and because a comb with a name on it is a place as much as a thing.

The address was a small apartment block three streets from Mikami's shop, and it had been
rebuilt since 1991, and the woman in the ground-floor flat had lived there for eleven years
and knew nothing. But the caretaker was old, and he remembered, because caretakers do.

He remembered a household in the nineties: a grandmother and a daughter and, for a while, a
small girl. He remembered that the grandmother was a seamstress and the daughter worked at a
laundry in Tateishi and the small girl used to play in the stairwell. He remembered — this was
the thing Aoi had come for — that a woman used to visit, an older woman, well-dressed, once
or twice a year, and would sit with the grandmother in the ground-floor flat, and the
grandmother would not let the small girl in, and after the visits the grandmother would be
quiet for a week.

Aoi asked whether he remembered the visitor's name.

The caretaker said he did not, but that the visitor always left with a wrapped bundle, and
that it was tall, about the height of a mirror.

Aoi stood in the stairwell of a rebuilt apartment block in Sendagi and understood that the
room had not found her at the estate sale. The room had found her twenty years earlier, in
this stairwell, when she was nine, when the visitor came and sat with her grandmother and
left with the mirror, and she had been inside the room's route her whole life, and the estate
sale had only been the moment the route named her.

[IMAGE 10.5 — a stairwell in a small Sendagi apartment block: worn stone steps, a handrail;
on the landing, a child's drawing in chalk, faded; a caretaker's door ajar; light from a
high window.]


---

Mikami went to the dry cleaner's in Nezu in December, because Aoi had told him about the
receipt, and because a man who has kept every ticket for thirty-eight years wanted to see the
shop that had kept a receipt for twenty.

The shop was small and old and the daughter of the man who wrote the receipt was behind the
counter, and she remembered the mirror cover, and she remembered the woman who brought it.

She remembered one thing that no document had. She said the woman always came on the same day,
the sixteenth, and she always paid in cash, and she always asked the same question before she
left. The question was not about the cleaning.

She asked, every time, for twenty years: "Is there any thread that will hold a tag that has
been cut off?"

The daughter's father had never known what to say. He had said what a dry cleaner says, which
is that you can sew a tag back on, and the woman had always nodded and taken the cover and
left, unsatisfied, and come back the next year and asked again.

Mikami stood in the shop and understood the question and the account at the same time. The
woman was not asking about the mirror. She was asking whether a thing that has been *severed*
from its record can be *reattached* — whether a tag cut off in 1958 can be sewn back on sixty
years later — and the answer, in the dry-cleaning trade as in the room, is the same: you can
sew it back, but the thread will always show, and the mark of the cut will always be there,
and that is what a repair is.

He told the daughter what the tag had said, and she wrote it down, and gave it to him, and it
is the only copy of the mirror cover's tag that survives: a name, a year, a place, in a small
upright hand, sewn into the lining of a cloth cover, cut off and sewn back on, twenty times
over, and never once thrown away.

[IMAGE 10.6 — a dry cleaner's counter in Nezu, an old shop: a cloth mirror cover on the
counter, the lining turned back, a tag half-sewn, thread and scissors; a woman's hands and an
old man's hands; the shop's shelves of wrapped garments behind.]

# The Doll

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The doll had a tag and the tag had a child's name and the child was alive.

That was the thing the police could never have explained and the thing that made Kubo open
the envelope eleven times without opening it. He ran a small repair shop for dolls in
Asakusa, which is a trade you enter by being unable to throw anything away, and he had
restored the doll in April without reading its tag, because the tag was not why he was
there.

He read it in June.

[IMAGE 11.1 — a bisque-headed doll on a bench, traditional Japanese costume, one hand
repaired; a small tag tied at the wrist in the same small upright hand as the other lots.
The doll's face is turned away from the camera.]

*HANA — 1958 — YANAKA.*

---

Kubo was sixty-eight and he had repaired dolls for forty-one years, and he could tell you
that a doll is not a toy. A doll is a stand-in. A child gives a doll a name and a history
and a set of injuries, and the child keeps the doll, and the doll keeps the child's
childhood, and when the child grows up and cannot bear it, the doll comes to Kubo, and he
mends it, and it goes back into a box, and the box goes into a cupboard, and that is the
whole trade: mending the stand-in so the original can rest.

He had four hundred dolls in his shop, in boxes, named on the boxes, waiting.

The Hana doll was a girl's doll, Taishō, bisque head, silk costume faded from crimson to
the colour of dried blood. Its right hand had been repaired once, badly, long ago. And in
its sash, sewn into the lining where a child would hide something, Kubo found a folded
strip of paper.

He almost did not open it. He had a rule about hidden things: a hidden thing belongs to
whoever hid it, and a mender is not a finder. But the doll had come from the Kanzaki estate,
and by June the estate had a reputation in the trade, and Kubo had begun to have a bad
feeling about the whole month of April.

He opened it.

[IMAGE 11.2 — the unfolded strip of paper: a child's handwriting, a numbered list of names,
the last one set below a ruled line and written in a steadier hand, reading SHIRAI; the
paper is water-stained and sewn-edge frayed.]

On it, in a child's hand, a list of names. The names on the tags. All twelve of them, in the
order they appear in the room, with a thirteenth written apart, below a line: *the one who
came after.*

The thirteenth was a child's name. Kubo looked it up because he could not stop himself,
which is the whole disease of his trade and hers.

The child was nine years old and lived in Katsushika, in Tateishi, and her family name was
Shirai.

---

Kubo called Aoi because she had left her card at the estate, and because he had met her
once, in April, when they both stood in the Kiyosumi-Shirakawa hall and read the same
table of tags and did not speak.

"Shirai," he said. "That's your name."

"Yes."

"There's a child. Nine. Tateishi."

Aoi was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "That's where I lived when I was nine. It's
where my mother died."

Kubo did not understand. He has never understood. He only knows that he put the doll in a
box and the box on a shelf and that in the morning the box was open, and the doll was
facing the door, and the tag had been rewound to show the blank side.

He called Aoi again, that morning, and told her, and she asked him to read the list once
more, slowly, and he did, and she stopped him at the fourth name.

"Say that one again."

"Tachibana," he said.

"Tachibana is my mother's maiden name," Aoi said. "My mother was a Tachibana. The fourth
name on the room list is my mother's family."

The room in Yanaka, 1958, had twelve people in it. Five of them, by the end of sixty years,
had descendants who lived within a few kilometres of each other and did not know it: a
buyer in Tateishi, a mender in Sendagi, a doll repairer in Asakusa, a locksmith in Yanaka, a
bank clerk in Asakusa. The room had not just been rebuilt. It had been seeded.

"Somebody put us here," Aoi said.

"Somebody kept us here," Kubo said. "There's a difference. You can't put a person
somewhere and make them stay thirty years. But you can choose who to buy from, and who to
mend for, and whose child to give your card to. She was a customer of everyone in this
city. She had sixty years to pick."

[IMAGE 11.3 — a shelf in a repair shop at dawn: an open box, a doll inside it now facing
outward toward the door; the tag on its wrist has been turned blank-side-up; a strip of
paper lies on the shelf below.]

---

Kubo went to lock the shop and found the key already turned.

He stood in his own doorway with the key in his hand and understood that he had locked it
the night before, and that the lock was a good lock, and that the doll had moved, and that
he was standing in a room full of four hundred stand-ins each of which held a childhood,
and that one of the childhoods had stood up and walked.

That is the phrase he used, afterward, when he tried to explain it, and he never got
further than that phrase: *one of the childhoods stood up and walked.*

He did not sleep. He sat with the light on and the door in view and the doll in the open box
facing him, and at some point in the small hours he understood that the exercise of a
lifetime — mending the stand-in so the original can rest — had been performed on him. He
was the stand-in. He had been mended, and filed, and left in a shop in Asakusa to keep a
piece of the room safe, and the room had come to collect.

In the morning there was a stamp on his counter, and an envelope, addressed in a hand he
recognised from the tag.

[IMAGE 11.4 — a photograph of a Tateishi drinking-alley (yokochō) at night, narrow, lantern
lit; in the far frame, a child-sized figure seen only from behind, walking away; the
photographer's presence is implied by a shadow entering the bottom of the frame.]

The envelope held a single photograph, of the Tateishi alley, and on the back, in pencil,
small and upright: *thank you for keeping her. she is the last of the set. when you are
ready, the room is in Kuramae, and it has a place for everyone who held a piece.*

Kubo put the photograph down and looked at the doll, and the doll looked at the door, and he
understood that he had been invited, and that the invitation was also the count, and that
somewhere a woman who had been dead since March had known, to the week, when he would open
the envelope.


---

Aoi asked Kubo the question she had been afraid to ask, and it was this: whether the doll had
ever belonged to a child who was alive.

Kubo said no. He said a doll that passes to a repairer is a doll that has been *retired*, and
a doll is retired when the child grows up, and a child who owned this doll would be in her
eighties now. But the doll's costume, and the doll's hand, and the way the silk had been
mended, told him something else: that the doll had not been retired. It had been *kept*.

"Kept how," Aoi asked.

"Kept the way you keep a thing you're going to give to someone," Kubo said. "The hand was
mended badly, long ago, by someone who didn't know how, and the costume was taken in at the
waist, and the tag was tied with cotton thread, not the shop's cord. Whoever kept this doll
kept it *ready*. They were holding it for a child."

Aoi sat with that. "For the thirteenth child. The one below the line."

"I think so," Kubo said. "I think the doll was the room's gift. A doll is what you give a
child to hold so she doesn't have to hold what she saw. And I think the old woman kept it for
sixty years because she never got to give it."

He turned the doll over. There was one more thing, and he had saved it, because he said he
had wanted Aoi to hear the rest first, so that she would understand what it meant.

Inside the doll's other sleeve, sewn into the lining, there was a second slip of paper, in
the small upright hand, older than the first, and on it: *if a child ever comes for you,
give her this. Tell her she can keep it. Tell her I'm sorry about the room.*

[IMAGE 11.5 — the interior of a doll's sleeve, held open: a slip of paper sewn into the
lining in an old hand; the doll lies on a bench beside a needle and thread; the tag at the
wrist is visible.]


---

Aoi found Kubo's box the next month, because Kubo had called her and because he wanted her to
have it, and the box held the doll and the doll's slips and Kubo's own account of the four
hundred stand-ins.

She went to Asakusa on a wet Sunday and sat in the repair shop among the boxes and asked Kubo
the question that had been forming since the temple.

She asked him whether any of the four hundred dolls had ever come back.

Kubo did not understand, at first. She asked again: whether a doll he had mended had ever come
back to the shop a second time, months or years later, changed. He said no, dolls do not walk,
and then he looked at the Hana doll in its open box and said, slowly, that there was one case.

In 1993, a doll had come back. A woman had brought in a doll he had mended in 1988, and she had
not wanted it mended; she had wanted it *inspected*, and she had asked him to look inside the
sash, and he had looked, and there had been nothing. And she had seemed, he said, disappointed
and relieved at once, and had taken the doll away, and had asked him to write nothing on the
ticket.

Aoi asked what the woman looked like.

Kubo said: fifties, well-dressed, small upright handwriting. She had paid in cash. And she had
asked him, before she left, one question he had never forgotten.

She had asked: "Is there any way to make a thing that has been read *unread*?"

Kubo had told her no. You cannot unread a thing. You can only read it differently, or keep it
from someone else.

And the woman had nodded, and had said, "Then I will keep it from someone else," and had left,
and Kubo had thought about it for years, and had never understood — until the Hana doll came
into his shop this spring, with the child's list sewn into its sash, and he had understood that
the woman in 1993 had been looking for the other slip of paper, the second one, the one he had
found in the *other* sleeve, and had not found it, and had gone away believing the doll was
clean.

Which meant the doll had been carrying the truth since 1958, and someone had been searching for
it for thirty-five years, and it had come to Kubo, and he had found it, and he had read it, and
the reading had put him in the room.

Aoi asked Kubo one last thing: whether the woman had left a name.

Kubo said no. But he said she had left a *district*, because he had asked where to send a
receipt, and she had said Sendagi, and had given an address three streets from Mikami's
workshop, and had asked him not to write it down.

book open to a blank line; a small slip of paper held under a lamp; in the background, the
shelves of four hundred named boxes.]

# The Screen

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The screen was six panels and one of them was not the same age as the others, and Yamamura
noticed that in the first minute, because noticing is what he did.

He was a restorer of painted screens and sliding doors, in a workshop in Kagurazaka, and he
had been given the screen by a dealer who had bought it at the Kanzaki estate and could not
sell it, because the tag on it named a family that had gone quiet, and because the fifth
panel had been replaced.

Tag: *KURODA — 1958 — YANAKA*. The screen was old, late Edo, a six-panel byōbu, gold leaf
and ink, a scene of a river and a bridge. The first four panels and the sixth were original.
The fifth was a later replacement, plain paper, darkened, with no painting on it at all.

A person does not replace one panel of a screen with a blank. A person replaces a panel
because the panel was *damaged*, and a repaired panel is repainted. A blank panel means the
panel was replaced and the painting was never restored, which means whoever owned it wanted
the screen whole but did not want the fifth panel to show anything.

[IMAGE 17.1 — a six-panel folding screen in a workshop, lit from the side: five panels
painted with a river and bridge scene in gold and ink; the fifth panel is blank, later paper,
visibly a different age; a small tag hangs from the frame.]

---

Yamamura laid the screen flat and looked at the blank panel under raking light.

There was a stain on it. A large one, dark, in the centre of the panel, the shape of a hand
if a hand had been pressed there and dragged. It had soaked into the paper and the paper had
been replaced over it, but the stain had bled through the new paper from behind, faintly, the
way a mark does when it is in the wood and not the leaf.

He knew what the mark was. He was fifty-three and he had restored screens for thirty years
and he had seen the mark three times, always on a panel that had been replaced without
repainting, and every time it had been a death.

Someone had bled on the fifth panel of the Kuroda screen in Yanaka in 1958, and someone had
replaced the panel and never painted it, and the screen had been kept by the Kanzakis for
sixty-five years with a blank fifth panel, and Mitsuko had bought it back and tagged it, and
it had come to Yamamura, who was now looking at the mark of a man's death on a screen that
was supposed to be a river.

Yamamura called the estate. He got Kei, who was tired, and who said the screen had been in
the storage room his whole childhood and that he had been told never to open it, and that he
had opened it once, at twelve, and had seen the blank panel, and had been beaten for it.

"Beaten," Yamamura said.

"Not beaten," Kei said. "Held. She held me by the arm and made me look at it for an hour.
She said, 'Now you've seen it, you carry it.' I was twelve. I didn't understand."

[IMAGE 17.2 — a raking-light detail of the blank fifth panel: the faint dark stain bleeding
through later paper from behind, roughly the shape of a pressed and dragged hand; the paper
grain is visible; a scale rule lies at the edge.]

---

Yamamura did the thing a restorer does, which is to look at the back.

He lifted the paper from the frame — carefully, because paper remembers — and on the reverse
of the blank panel, under the old paper, in a hand he did not know, there was writing.

Brush, small, and it had been written on the *back* of the panel, so that it would not be
seen from the room, only from behind, only by someone who took the screen apart.

It said: *he fell against the panel. I did not move. I was behind the chest. I did not move
until they carried me out. Do not repaint this panel. Let it stay blank. The blank is the only
honest thing in this house.*

Yamamura sat back.

The screen had stood between the room and the corridor. It was a wall, and a screen is a wall
you can fold, and the fifth panel had caught the dying man, and a child behind the chest had
watched him fall against it, and had not moved, and had written, at some point, a note to
herself on the back of the panel, in the one place no one would ever look.

And then, in the margin, smaller, in a hand that had steadied with age:

*the river on the front was painted by my father. he painted it the year I was born. I have
kept the screen so the river stays next to the blank, and so that the last thing the room
held is the thing I look at. I am not afraid of it. I am keeping it company.*

[IMAGE 17.3 — the reverse of the detached fifth panel: a brush-written note on the raw back
of the panel, dark ink, in a careful hand, with a smaller marginal line in an older,
steadier hand; the panel rests on a workbench beside its frame.]

---

Yamamura called Aoi, because the estate had given him her number, and because a restorer who
finds a note like that does not keep it.

He read it to her. She asked him to read the marginal line again, and then she was quiet for
a long time.

"She wasn't building a shrine," Aoi said at last. "She was sitting with it. The room, the
screen, the blank panel — she wasn't preserving a crime. She was keeping a vigil. Sixty years
of it. The tags aren't evidence. They're company."

"The tags are names," Yamamura said.

"The names are the people who were in the room with her," Aoi said. "She's been keeping them
together. That's why they can't all be dead. If they're dead, she's alone in it."

Yamamura looked at the screen, the river on five panels and the blank on the sixth, and
understood the thing that Aoi had understood at the temple, which was that the whole
collection was an act of loyalty to a room that no one else had stayed in.

"She kept the room so she wouldn't have to be the only one who saw it," he said.

"Yes," Aoi said. "And someone is killing the others, one by one, so that she will be."

---

Yamamura did not repaint the panel. He reattached the paper, and set the fifth panel back the
way it was, and stood the screen against the wall of his workshop, the river facing the room.

He went home. In the morning his workshop door was open, though he had locked it, and the
screen was gone, and on the workbench, where the screen had been, there was a single tag, in
the small upright hand, and it said his name and his district and the year, and beneath it, in
the steadier hand, one line:

*you read the back. thank you. but the river has to face the wall now, because the last
person who is going to see the blank is almost here.*

Yamamura stood in his open workshop and understood, as Kubo had understood, and as Hoshino had
understood, that he had been filed — that reading the back of a panel is a way of being
counted, and that the room had been counting readers, not owners, all along.

[IMAGE 17.4 — a workshop at dawn: the space where the screen stood is empty; on the
workbench, a single white tag with a name and a date; the door is open onto an empty
Kagurazaka street; a cup of tea has gone cold.]


---

Aoi went to Kagurazaka after the screen was taken, because Yamamura had called her, and because
she wanted to see the empty place where a screen had stood.

Yamamura showed her the workbench, the tag, the open door. He said he had called the police
and that they had taken a statement and that nothing would come of it, because nothing had
been stolen except a screen, and because a screen that leaves a workshop by itself is not a
crime the police can write down.

Then he told her the thing he had not told the police, because it was not a fact, and Yamamura
was a man of facts.

He said that in the night, before the screen was gone, he had dreamed of the room. He had
never seen the room. He had seen only the fifth panel, the blank one, and the stain. But in
the dream he was lying on the floor of a six-mat room, low, at the height of a child, and the
room was full of objects, and through the doorway a woman was standing, and she was not
looking at him.

She was counting.

He said she counted to twelve and then she stopped, and she looked at the place where he was
lying, and she said, without moving her lips, a number that was not twelve.

"The thirteenth," Yamamura said. "She said thirteen. And then I woke, and the screen was
gone."

Aoi stood in the empty workshop and looked at the place against the wall where the screen had
stood, and thought about the fact that the room had begun to give the same dream to everyone
who read it, and that the dream was not a warning. It was a *census*.

[IMAGE 13.5 — an empty workshop wall in Kagurazaka; a single tag on the workbench; a teacup;
the door open onto the street; on the wall, a faint rectangular discolouration where a screen
stood for years.]


---

Aoi went back to the Kanzaki house in December, alone, with the keys from the hook, and she
took the folder and the tape measure and a torch, because she had understood, by then, that the
house was the last page of the account she had not read.

The house was empty. The wall was there, replastered. She put her hand on it and it was cold in
a rectangle. She did not open it, because the room behind it was gone; the storage room had
been emptied in April and the arrangement had moved to Kuramae, and what was left in the house
was the *door*, which is all a room leaves when it moves.

She went through the front rooms with the torch. She found, in the back of a cupboard, a
single object Kei had missed: a wooden box, small, with a lid, containing a set of *paper
patterns*.

Aoi spread them on the floor. They were floor plans. Hand-drawn, in the small upright hand, on
tracing paper, dozens of them, dated across sixty years, and each was an attempt to draw the
room.

None of them matched. Not two of the sixty matched each other. Each was the room, drawn from
memory, in a different year, by a person trying to remember the dimensions of a space she had
seen once, at eleven, from behind a chest — and none of them was right, and she had known none
of them was right, and she had kept trying for sixty years because *she could not remember and
she would not guess*.

The last plan, dated 2019, was the closest. It was nearly seven by twelve, and the gap was set
back toward the corner, and in the corner there was a small rectangle labelled *tansu*. Beside
it, in pencil, in the small upright hand, a note: *still not right. it is not the plan. it is
the seeing. I will have to stand in it again.*

Aoi sat on the floor of an empty house in Yanaka with sixty wrong plans and understood the whole
of the collection at last. Mitsuko had not been trying to *remember* the room. She had been
trying to *reproduce the view*, and a view cannot be drawn from memory; it can only be measured
from the position, and the position was the gap, and the gap was for a person, and the person
had to be someone who could read what the view contained.

The plans were not plans. They were sixty years of a child trying to get back behind the chest,
and failing, because the chest was gone, and the only way back was to build the view and stand
in it, and to do that you need someone to stand in the gap.

[IMAGE 13.6 — sixty hand-drawn floor plans spread on a wooden floor under torchlight, each
different, dated in a corner; the most recent is nearly correct; a note in pencil at the
margin reads "still not right"; the torch stands upright among them.]

# The Swords

*Close third, past tense.*

---

Ito had appraised sword fittings for thirty years and he had never once been afraid of a
blade. That is what he told the police, and it was true, and it did not help him.

He dealt in tsuba and menuki and kozuka, the small metal fittings of the samurai
sword, out of a shop in Nezu behind the shrine, and he dealt mostly with the fittings and
rarely with the blades, because blades carry a different kind of history and a different
kind of buyer. The Kanzaki estate sent him a lot in April: a daishō stand with two blades,
both in shirasaya, both unsigned, both with a small tag tied to the stand.

Tag: *KURODA — 1958 — YANAKA*.

[IMAGE 12.1 — a daishō stand on a workshop table, two swords in plain wooden shirasaya
resting on the hooks; a small handwritten tag tied to the stand reading KURODA, 1958,
YANAKA; a loupe lies beside it.]

---

The blades were not Kanzaki blades. That was the first thing. The Kanzaki family had no
sword tradition; the stand was the only martial object in the estate. And the blades were
fine — a matched pair, Shintō period, made for a man of some standing, and cut down at some
point to a shorter length, which is what you do when a blade is no longer for war and
becomes, instead, a thing a man keeps in a room to remind himself what he is.

Ito examined the nakago, the tang, for the file marks, and found them, and found also a
second set of marks that did not belong: the marks of a polisher's stone, recent, within the
lifetime of the blade's ownership. Someone had had the blades polished, and not long ago,
and the polish had been done well.

A man does not polish the blades of a matched pair and then sell them in an estate sale. A
man polishes them to keep them. And then a woman buys back the stand they sit on, which
means the stand was let go by the man and reclaimed by the woman, and the blades went with
the stand, and the blades had been polished by the last hand before hers.

Ito called the estate, and got Kei, and asked who had polished the blades.

"Polished?" Kei said. "They're just old swords. My mother never touched them."

"Someone did," Ito said. "Within fifteen years, and well."

Kei was quiet. Then he said, "There was a man. He came to the house once, maybe fifteen
years ago. I wasn't allowed in the room. I heard him crying."

"Crying."

"I was sixteen. I thought it was strange. I asked my mother afterward who he was, and she
said a customer, and I said customers don't cry, and she said, 'This one does.'"

[IMAGE 12.2 — a close photograph of a sword tang (nakago) with file marks, lit by a
loupe lamp; a faint second set of stone marks crosses the first at an angle; a small tag is
tied to the tang.]

---

Ito did the thing that a dealer does, which is to look at the fittings, because the fittings
tell you what the blade was for.

He took the tsuba off the stand. It was iron, dark, a good Edo piece, and on its reverse,
where a hand would rest, there was a small incised mark, worn but legible: a plum blossom
and a character. He knew the character. It was the crest of a family he had appraised for,
years ago, and the family name was Kuroda.

So the stand was Kuroda's, and the blades were Kuroda's, and the estate's tag named a
Kuroda, and the woman who had spent sixty years assembling a room had put a family's
weapons in it, which is not the act of a collector. It is the act of a witness, setting the
table for a confession.

Ito called Kei back and asked, directly: "Did your mother ever tell you what happened in that
room in 1958?"

Kei said no.

"Did anyone else."

Kei said nothing for a long moment. Then he said, in a voice that had gone somewhere else:
"An old man came once, when I was a boy. He stood at the door of the room and wouldn't go
in. He said, 'Tell her it was the swords that did the talking.' I never knew what he meant.
She cried when I told her. It's the only time I saw her cry."

The swords that did the talking.

Ito looked at the two blades in their plain scabbards and understood, the way you understand
a valuation before you can name it, that these were not weapons of the night. They were the
Kuroda family's formal pair, the pair a patron wears when he goes to a house to be
important and to be seen. Kuroda Genji had gone to the Kanzaki house in 1958 carrying the
family's pride, and had done something unspeakable while wearing it, and someone — not the
Kanzakis, who had no swords — had polished the blades afterward, and had put them back on
the stand, and had kept them, and had cried, and had brought them back to the woman's room
fifteen years ago because the room had a place for the blades and he could not finish
keeping them alone.

[IMAGE 12.3 — the tsuba's reverse: an iron guard photographed close, showing a worn plum
blossom and a family character incised at the edge; the light catches the worn metal.]

---

Ito made a mistake. He will say this himself, and he said it to the police, and it is the
only part of the account he blames himself for.

He called the Kuroda family to offer the blades. It was professional. When a blade with a
family crest comes into the trade, you notify the family; it is a courtesy and a small
reward and no one thinks twice. He left a message with the family's office in Nezu.

The next day, a man came to the shop.

He was perhaps sixty, well-dressed, polite, with his hands folded, and he introduced himself
as Kuroda Jun, the grandson, and he asked to see the blades. Ito, who was a dealer and
pleased to have found the rightful owner, brought them out.

Kuroda Jun did not look at the blades. He looked at the tag on the stand, and at the tsuba,
and at the nakago where the stone marks crossed the file marks, and he set the sword down
and said, "You looked at the tang."

"I appraise," Ito said.

"Yes," said Kuroda Jun. "That's the problem."

Ito did not understand, at first, and then he did, and he understood too late, the way you
understand a jewel is paste the moment after you have said the price out loud. The blades
were the only physical proof that a Kuroda had gone to the house wearing his family's formal
pair. The blades could not say what had happened. But they could say he was there, and the
stone marks could say someone had tried, afterward, to make the pair fit to be kept, which
is what a family does after a member has done something that the family cannot bear and
cannot bury.

"You've been very helpful," said Kuroda Jun, and reached across the table, and put his hand
flat on the sword, and did not let Ito lift it.

[IMAGE 12.4 — a table from above: two hands, one old and one gloved, both resting on the
same sword in shirasaya; the tag on the stand between them; the frame is otherwise empty and
the room behind is dark.]

Ito's account ends there. The account that follows is not his; it is assembled from the
shop's ledger, which was found open on the table, and from the single line Ito had written
in it that morning, before the man arrived:

*KURODA blades — family notified — do not sell to family. K. knows what they did.*

He wrote that line and did not act on it, because he did not yet believe it, and by the
afternoon it was true, and he was not there to say so.


---

Aoi went to the Nezu shop two days after Ito was found, because she had by then trained
herself to arrive at a death within forty-eight hours, and because she wanted to read the
ledger that Ito had left open.

The ledger was gone. The police had taken it. But the shop's shutters were up and there was a
note on the door in a hand Aoi did not recognise, saying the shop was closed pending
inventory, and under the door there was a small tag, and the tag had no name on it, and it had
a date, and the date was today.

She put it in her pocket and did not read it until she was home.

Then she went to the storage room behind the shop, because the door was unlocked and because
she had become the kind of person who tries doors, and inside there were the blades, in
shirasaya, on the stand, and the stand was the one from the estate, and the two blades were
on it, and on the stand there was a tag, and the tag said KURODA — 1958 — YANAKA, and it was
the original tag, which the estate had sold with the stand, which meant the blades had been
brought back.

Which meant Kuroda Jun had already been here.

Which meant Kuroda Jun had stood in this room and put the blades back on the stand that the
dead woman had tagged, and had left them, and had left the shop open, and had left a tag under
the door with no name and today's date.

Aoi understood, standing in the storage room with the blades, that Kuroda Jun was not tidying.
He was *returning*. He had been returning the pieces to the room for years — the coat, the
stand, the blades — and the deaths were not the point of the returns. The returns were the
point of the returns. He was putting the room back together the only way a man who believes he
is the victim's heir can: by owning it.

And then Aoi understood the thing she would not understand fully until Kuramae: that Kuroda
Jun and the collector had the same project, from opposite ends, and that the tags were the
hinge, and that a third party — the one who had been clearing the witnesses — was walking the
same road with neither of them, and that whoever stood in the gap would settle it.

[IMAGE 14.5 — a storage room behind a Nezu shop: a daishō stand with two blades in shirasaya,
the original tag tied on; a dealer's ledger gone from the table, the dust showing its
rectangle; the shop's back door open to a narrow lane.]


---

The blades came back the week before the room closed, and Aoi was the one who received them,
because there was no one else left, and because a courier does not ask why a shirasaya is being
sent to an appraiser.

The parcel was addressed to "the last reader" in the steady hand, c/o Aoi's office, and it had
no return address, and inside, wrapped in cloth, were the two blades on the daishō stand, with
the original tag, and a letter.

The letter is the document Aoi read most often in December, and it is the document that made
her certain the ones who did the tidying were not monsters in the way a reader wants a monster
to be.

It read:

*You have the folder. You will have worked out that she kept more than one of us. Hers is the
testimony. Mine is the record-keeping: the tickets, the registers, the files that let a death be
closed as an accident. I am not sorry for it, but I am going to tell you why, because you are
the only person who has read the whole of it and a reader is owed a reason.*

*In 1958 my mother carried a mirror out of that house and was paid to be silent for thirty
years. She never told me; I found it in a register, the way I find everything, in the year she
died, when I was fourteen, and I understood two things. First, that a silence can be paid for
and passed down. Second, that the only cure for a paid silence is an unpaid truth.*

*Kanzaki Mitsuko spent her life buying the truth back, object by object, and she could not
speak it, because she was eleven when it happened and because her mother had signed it away in
a register and because a woman in a house in Yanaka cannot accuse a Kuroda. She could only
build. So she built, for sixty years, and then in 1989 she wrote to two families whose
elder had carried her night and been paid to forget it — the driver's line and mine — and she
asked us to carry it, and we did, because I was my mother's shame and she was my family's, and
between us we could make one true thing.*

*The deaths were not her design. They were ours, and I want that exact — mine among them. She
wrote the list and we walked it; the living she left to the other hand, the records to me. She
never asked either of us to kill anyone. She asked us to clear the route, and I interpreted
"clear" the way a family with a bought silence interprets a word, and I was wrong, and it is too
late, and I am putting it in writing because a reader is owed a reason and because a confession
is the only object I have left that she did not tag.*

*The blades belong to the room. The room is nearly closed. If you are reading this, you are
inside it, and I am sorry, and I am not coming.*

[IMAGE 14.6 — a letter on plain paper in a steady adult hand, four paragraphs, unsigned; beside
it, a daishō stand with two blades in shirasaya and the original tag; a courier's torn label.]

# The Photographs

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The envelope arrived at Aoi's flat on the first of November, six months and a half after the
sale, and it had been delayed, and the delay was the whole of the story.

It was addressed in a careful hand, to *Shirai Aoi*, and it had two stamps on it, and a
return address she did not recognise: a flat in Nezu, no name. Inside were eleven photographs
and a note. The note said:

*I proofread for a living. I found the album. I have photographed all of it and I am sending
it to you because you are the only name in the sale that seemed to be looking for the answer
rather than the price. Please look at photograph four. — S.*

Suzuki. The proofreader. Aoi recognised the flat, the paper, the precision. She had never met
him, and she would never meet him, because by the first of November Suzuki had been dead for
three weeks.

The police had closed it as a fall. A man of fifty-one, living alone, at the top of a
stairwell, on a wet night, no note, no struggle, no reason. His family in Shizuoka had
accepted it. His family had not known he had bought an album at an estate sale, or that he
had been photographing rooms for nine years, or that he had spent the last month of his life
reading the back of a photograph of a dead man and finding the one wrong word.

[IMAGE 14.1 — an envelope on a low table, opened, two stamps, a Nezu return address with no
name; eleven photographs fanned out beside it; the top one is a black-and-white print of a
six-mat room.]

---

Aoi looked at the eleven photographs for four hours.

They were, as Suzuki had promised, the album. Fifty prints, reproduced in eleven composite
photographs, in order, 1962 to 1989, and the loose 1958 print, and the note on the back,
which Suzuki had photographed in raking light so the pencil showed.

But it was photograph four that held her, and she saw why within a minute, and that is the
moment the account stops being a detective story and becomes a thing about inheritance.

Photograph four was a single album page: three prints of the room. In the first, from 1962,
the room holds four objects — a chest, a lacquer box, a folding screen, and a mirror. In the
second, from 1971, there are more. In the third, from 1979, there are many.

And in all three, the mirror is in the same place, against the same wall, and it is Aoi's
mirror: black lacquer, a dressing mirror on a stand, with a crack down the glass.

Aoi had spent her life assuming the mirror came to her mother in 2003, when her mother died,
or before, as a family thing. It did not. The mirror was in the room in Yanaka in 1962. It
was in the room in 1971 and 1979. It was *in the collection* — and the collection was
supposed to have been assembled after the room was emptied.

The mirror was in the photographs before the mirror was supposed to be in the collection.

Which meant one of two things, and Aoi sat with both of them, and they were both terrible.

Either the album was photographed *in the room*, before it was emptied — in which case the
room was never emptied, and Mitsuko grew up in it, and the 1958 death happened among those
objects, not after they were gone. Or the mirror was in the Kanzaki house all along, and the
tag that said *SHIRAI — 2003 — TATEISHI* was not the mirror's provenance but the mirror's
*destination* — the place and the year it was supposed to end up, which was with Aoi's
mother, and then with Aoi.

Aoi put the photograph down and understood that the collection had a timeline that ran
forward as well as back. The tags named the dead. But the objects had a future too, and her
mother's name and death were in it, and hers was at the end.

[IMAGE 14.2 — the composite of album page four: three black-and-white prints of the same
six-mat room across three decades, the objects increasing; in all three, a black-lacquer
mirror stands against the same wall.]

---

She called Hoshino, who was the only one left who would still answer, and asked her the
question she could not ask Mikami.

"Can an object be photographed in a place it was never in?"

Hoshino, who had spent her life reading documents, said the most useful thing anyone said to
Aoi in the whole account.

"Any document can be made to say anything if the person making it controls the record," she
said. "You've been asking whether the room is real. Wrong question. The room is a *filing
system*. The album is a file. The photographs are the file's contents. If you want to know
whether the room was emptied in 1958, don't look at the album. Look at what the album
*replaced*."

"What did it replace?"

"An inventory," Hoshino said. "Somewhere there is a list of what was in that room when it was
searched in 1958. The police would have made one, or the family, or the temple. A room where
a man died gets inventoried. Find the inventory, and you find out what was really in the room
that night, and what Mitsuko has been buying back ever since — and what she *added*."

Aoi sat in her flat in Tateishi with eleven photographs of a room and no inventory, and
understood that she had been reading the wrong document for seven months, and that the
album, which Suzuki had died for, was not the proof. It was the *cover*. Someone had made a
fifty-print album of a room over twenty-seven years so that anyone who found it would
believe the room had been assembled slowly, by a collector, after the fact — when in truth
the mirror, and perhaps other objects, had never left.

Mitsuko had made the album to be found. It was a witness statement written in photographs,
folded and unfolded for sixty years.

[IMAGE 14.3 — a hand-drawn diagram by Aoi: two timelines, one running backward from 2023 to
1958 and one running forward from 1958 to 2023, with the mirror drawn at the crossing point;
annotations in her hand distinguish "what the tags say" from "what the album shows."]

---

She went to Nezu that night to thank Suzuki, or to find him, and she stood outside his flat,
at the top of the stairwell, in the wet, and the police tape was gone and the door was
sealed and a neighbour told her, kindly, that the gentleman had fallen and that it was very
sad and that he had been a quiet man who kept photographs.

Aoi asked whether anyone had come to the flat, in the weeks before.

The neighbour thought about it. She said yes, actually. A gentleman had come, perhaps a month
ago, well-dressed, polite, and had asked after Mr Suzuki, and had been told he was out, and
had left a card, and the neighbour had thought nothing of it because he was so polite.

Aoi asked what the card said.

The neighbour did not remember. But she said the gentleman had come back, and had waited on
the landing, in the evening, more than once, and that the last time she had seen Mr Suzuki
alive he had been coming up the stairs with a bag of shopping, and there had been a
gentleman at the top of the stairs, and they had spoken, and the neighbour had gone inside
because it was late.

Aoi stood on the landing and looked down the stairs, and she understood the shape of it. Not
a fall. A conversation at the top of a stairwell, in the wet, with a polite man, and then a
man who had spent a month finding the one wrong word in a dead woman's photograph, and who
had found it, and who had been going to post an envelope.

The envelope had been delayed because Suzuki had held it, and then because his flat had been
sealed, and then because the police had gone through his things slowly, and by the time it
reached Aoi it was November and the account was nearly over.

Aoi went home and added Suzuki to the folder, as the fourth death, and wrote in her notebook
the line that became the spine of everything after:

*the collector did not kill the witnesses. the collector kept them. someone else has been
killing the witnesses to keep the room from being read, and the someone else has been doing
it since before the sale, and the someone else is not the collector, and that is why he is
still here.*

She underlined *he*.

Then she underlined it again.

[IMAGE 14.4 — a stairwell landing at night, wet: a closed door with a seal, a bag of
shopping on the floor, an envelope halfway under the door that has not been posted; the
camera is at the top of the stairs looking down.]


---

Aoi did the thing an appraiser does with a document that has been tampered with, which is to
look for the *original*, because a copy tells you what someone wanted you to see and an
original tells you what was there.

The album's originals had been in the Kanzaki house. Kei had cleared the house. So Aoi went
back to Kei, for the third time, and asked him the question she had not asked: what had he
thrown away.

Kei did not understand, at first. He said he had not thrown anything away; he had sold
everything. Aoi asked whether he had thrown anything away while he was *clearing* — not
selling, not keeping, clearing. He said no, and then he stopped, and then he said the thing
Aoi had come for: that there had been one box he had not been able to sell and had not been
willing to throw out, and had left in the house, because it was too heavy to carry and because
it was full of papers, and papers do not sell.

Aoi asked what the box held.

"Household papers," Kei said. "Insurance. Old bills. A deed with a name on it that made no
sense. Photographs, maybe. I didn't look."

Aoi asked whether the box was still in the house.

He said he thought so. He said the house was empty and locked and no one had been in it since
March except, apparently, whoever had plastered the wall and left the keys.

Aoi went to the house that night, with the keys from the hook, and let herself in.

[IMAGE 15.4 — the entrance hall of an emptied Yanaka house at night, torchlight: a box of
household papers on the floor where it was left; on the wall, the plastered rectangle; the
ring of keys in the narrator's hand, the oldest brass.]

---

The box was where he said. It had four decades of household paper in it, and at the bottom,
under the insurance and the bills, there was a folded sheet of heavy paper that was not a
bill and not a deed.

It was a *list*. In the small upright hand, older than the ledger, in a hand that was not yet
steady. Thirty-one numbered lines. The objects of the room, as a child of eleven had set them
down, from memory, the year after the night.

Aoi read the list in the empty house, with the torch, and it was the inventory that the temple
priest had made, written independently, from the other side of the wall: not what a priest
saw, but what a child remembered. And the two lists matched, line for line, except for the two
objects that were already gone when the priest came.

The child had listed the chest first. Number one, before the mirror, before the screen,
before anything else. *The chest, in the corner, where I sat.* And beside it, in the margin,
in a hand that was already trying to be small and upright, a note:

*it was taken the same night. I don't know where. I have to find it.*

Aoi stood in the empty house and understood that the search had begun the *year after* the
night, when Mitsuko was twelve, and that the chest had been missing for longer than the
collection had existed, and that the whole sixty years had been one child trying to get back
the object she had hidden behind.

[IMAGE 15.5 — a torchlit floor in an emptied house: a heavy paper sheet with thirty-one
numbered lines in a child's hand; the first line reads "the chest, in the corner"; a margin
note; around it, insurance papers and old bills.]

---

She called the priest, Ono, from the house, at eleven at night, and asked him one question:
whether the temple's record showed who had *taken* the chest.

Ono said it did not. But he said there was a second note, filed with the inventory, that he
had not read to her because he had not understood it until now.

He read it.

It said: *a servant girl came for the chest, and the family gave it to her, and she was seen
carrying it toward the river. Ask after the servant.*

Aoi stood in the empty house with the torch on the list and the phone to her ear, and put the
last pieces together, the way an appraiser closes a provenance.

The chest went out with Hana, the servant, the same night the mirror did. The mirror went to
Hana as payment. The chest went with the mirror, because the chest was where the child hid,
and the servant who carried the child out could not carry the mirror *and* the chest, and
chose — or was told — to take the chest, and the chest went down the Sumida and into the hands
of whichever family member kept it for sixty years, and it surfaced in a warehouse in Kuramae
in the autumn, placed by someone who knew exactly what it was, in the corner of the room,
where it had always stood.

Somebody had kept the chest. Somebody had brought it back. And the only person who could have
had it, if it went out with Hana, was the person on the other end of the mirror's line.

Aoi looked at her own hand, holding the keys, in the torchlight, in her mother's story.

Her grandmother had carried the chest out of Yanaka. It had passed to Aoi's mother. It had
passed, somehow, to someone, and it had come back to the room, and Aoi was holding the keys to
the house where the room had been.

She understood that she had been carrying the chest her whole life without knowing it, in the
only form a thing can survive when no one will speak its name: as a fact in a family that had
been turned into a story, and then into a silence, and then into nothing at all.

[IMAGE 15.6 — the empty house at night from the outside: a single window lit by a torch; a
woman's silhouette moving behind it; the lane behind Yanaka Ginza wet and empty; a tall,
cloth-wrapped bundle, mirror-shaped, glimpsed in the porch.]

# The Registry

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The registry was in Mitaka, in a government archive annex that smells of paper and floor
wax and the particular cold of a building that is empty at four in the afternoon.

Hoshino had come to read a family register — a koseki — and she had come because a dead
woman's tags named a town and a year, and because Hoshino was a genealogist, and because
the estate had, in the trade, become the kind of case you take when you have stopped being
able to sleep.

The Kanzaki koseki was a single sheet of certified paper, and it held the whole family, in
the way these records do, in a set of columns that describe births and deaths and adoptions
and, sometimes, in the space where a cause of death is written, a silence.

Kanzaki Sada, mother of Mitsuko, died in 1971, cause listed as illness. Kanzaki Mitsuko,
born 1947, died 2023. And between them, in the household, a third name Hoshino had not
expected: a man, Kuroda Genji, listed not as family but as a temporary resident of the
household, for the year 1958, with a date of removal that matched the date he was found in
the pond at Inokashira.

[IMAGE 13.1 — a certified koseki document on an archive table under a lamp; columns of
names, dates, and places; one line is magnified in the composition, showing a temporary
resident entry for 1958 and a removal date.]

---

Hoshino sat with that line for a long time.

A koseki is not a diary. It records status, not events. A man does not appear in a household
register as a temporary resident unless someone formally took him in, which means that at
some point in 1958, Kuroda Genji was registered at the Kanzaki house. He was not a guest.
He was a resident. He lived there, or was recorded as living there, for long enough to be
entered and then removed.

And the date of removal matched the date of his death.

Hoshino had done genealogies for twenty-two years and she had seen this pattern exactly
twice: a resident entered, a resident removed, the removal dated to a death, and the file
otherwise silent. Both other times, the silence had been a cover-up, and both other times,
the family had later quietly sold the house and moved.

The Kanzakis had not moved. Mitsuko had stayed in that house for sixty-five more years, in
the room where the man had died, and had bought back the room's contents one by one, and had
died there in March.

That is not grief. Grief leaves. That is a woman keeping watch over a document she could not
change.

[IMAGE 13.2 — a hand-drawn genealogy of the Kanzaki and Kuroda households in pencil: two
family trees joined by a single dotted line marked 1958; beside the Kuroda side, a small
tombstone symbol and the word INOKASHIRA.]

---

Hoshino requested the Kuroda registry next, and that was where the account turned.

The Kuroda family koseki was a single sheet, and it listed, in the space for the head of
household, Kuroda Genji, and then, in the column for his death, a cause and a place:
drowning, Inokashira, 1958. And then, in the space reserved for a note, a single line that
Hoshino had never seen in any register in twenty-two years.

The line said, in the formal language of the register: *the family declines to state.*

Four characters. The family had been asked, at the time of the death, for a statement, and
had declined, and the archivist had written the refusal into the record, where it had sat
for sixty-five years.

Hoshino photographed it. It was the first documentary proof she had that the Kurodas had
known, at the time, that the death was not an accident, and that the family had chosen — in
writing, in a government register — to say nothing.

She sat back and looked at the two sheets, side by side, the Kanzaki register and the Kuroda
register, and she understood the shape of the whole thing, which was not a murder and not a
cover-up alone. It was a negotiation. The Kurodas had lost a man, and had known he had done
something, and had declined to state. The Kanzakis had lost nothing official, and had
absorbed the man's death into their household record and into their house, and had stayed,
and had begun to collect.

Two families, each holding one half of the night, each unable to speak it, and between them
a room, and in the room, gradually, over sixty years, every object the night had touched.

Hoshino called Aoi.

---

"There was a resident," she said. "Kuroda Genji was registered at the Kanzaki house in 1958.
It's the only reason the Kanzakis were never charged. The death happened at home, in his
registered residence, and the family that would have complained declined to state."

Aoi wrote it down. "So both families signed the lie."

"Not a lie," Hoshino said. "A silence. There's a difference. The Kurodas declined to state
because stating it would name what he had done. The Kanzakis absorbed the death into their
house because stating it would name what their daughter had done. Two silences that fit
together. That's why the room survived."

"And Mitsuko?"

Hoshino looked at the third document on the table, which she had not yet described to Aoi:
a small handwritten note, filed in the archive with the Kanzaki koseki, dated 1961, and
signed by an official.

"Your grandmother," Hoshino said. "Hana. She filed a household change in 1961. She requested
a transfer out of the Kanzaki household. The reason given is one word."

"What word."

"Employment."

Aoi was quiet.

"The same year the first tag is dated," Hoshino said. "1961. Your grandmother left the house
the year Mitsuko started collecting. She was paid — we know that from the mirror — and she
left, and she took the mirror, which was the weapon, and she never spoke, and her daughter
never spoke, and you grew up in Tateishi not knowing any of it. That's the last silence, and
it's the one that kept you alive until April."

[IMAGE 13.3 — two certified documents side by side on the archive table: the Kanzaki
register and the Kuroda register, each with a single line circled; between them, a small
handwritten filing note dated 1961 with the word EMPLOYMENT underlined.]

---

The archivist came at five to say the building was closing, and Hoshino gathered up her
photographs and her notebook and walked out into the cold of Mitaka station, and stood on
the platform, and understood that she had just read the whole crime and could prove none of
it, because both families had decline-to-state stamped into the paper, and a silence is not
a confession, and a registry is not a witness.

She went home. She put the photographs in a folder and the folder in a drawer, and she wrote
one line in her notebook, which is the line that was found there afterward:

*the register says everything except the one thing, and the one thing is the only thing that
matters, and that is why she collected objects instead of documents. you cannot decline to
state an object. you can only hold it.*

And then she went to bed, and in the morning the folder was on the kitchen table instead of
in the drawer, and the photographs were face down, and the first one had a small tag taped
to it, in a hand she had spent three days learning to read:

*HOSHINO — 2023 — MITAKA.*

She had been filed. She was a record now, like Mikami, like Kubo, like the blades — a person
who knew a piece of the truth and could be relied upon to keep it. She put the tag in her
pocket and did not call the police, because she did not yet have a crime, only a silence,
and because she had begun to understand that the room preferred its witnesses silent, and
that being preferred was very close to being chosen.

[IMAGE 13.4 — a kitchen table in the morning: a folder open, photographs face down in a
row; on the first photograph, a small handwritten tag taped in place, reading HOSHINO, 2023,
MITAKA; a cup of tea gone cold.]


---

Hoshino did not go to the police, and she has never fully explained why, and the notebook she
left explains it better than she could.

She wrote: *a register is a promise the state makes to itself. If the state declines to state,
the promise is void, and a void promise is not nothing. It is a permission. Whoever held the
pen at the moment of the refusal holds the room. That is what I found. That is why I could not
file it.*

She also wrote a list, and the list is the thing that makes her a witness rather than a
reader. It was a list of the *other* rooms — the two other genealogies she had done in
twenty-two years where a resident was entered and removed and the file went silent. She had
not thought about them in years. Now she went back to both.

Both rooms had been in shitamachi districts. Both deaths had been ruled accidents. Both
families had, within a decade, transferred a large household object — a chest, in one case; a
cabinet, in the other — to a domestic servant, and the servant had left the household, and the
household had stayed.

Hoshino had found the pattern and had been terrified by it, because a pattern that repeats
across three families is not a room. It is a *practice*. Somewhere in the shitamachi, at some
point, an arrangement had been made: a household object takes the blame, a servant carries it
out, a silence is paid, and the family stays in the house and keeps the room.

And Mitsuko, at eleven, had grown up inside the practice, and had done the only thing a child
could do with a practice no one would name: she had made it *into objects*, and had spent her
life getting the objects back, so that the practice would at last have a shape someone could
read.

Hoshino wrote: *she did not invent the room. The room is old. She was the first person in sixty
years willing to keep the receipt.*

[IMAGE 16.5 — a notebook page in a careful hand: two columns listing three "silent" cases,
with the household object and the servant's departure noted; below, a single underlined
sentence; the notebook lies open on an archive table.]


---

Hoshino's last act was to file a correction, and the correction is the only official document in
the whole account that tells the truth.

She went back to Mitaka in November with the folder and with Aoi and with a request, on a form,
to amend two family registers, and the request was refused, as she knew it would be, because
you cannot amend the dead, and because a decline-to-state is permanent, and because the state
does not reopen what it has closed.

So she did the only other thing a registrar can do. She wrote a *note* and filed it with the
Kuroda register, as a note from a researcher, which is permitted, and the note is a page long,
and it says, in the formal language of the archive, everything the register declined to state.

Aoi read the note on the train home. It is the document she has kept, and I have kept it too,
and it is the last thing I will quote before the end.

*Note, filed by researcher H., Mitaka annex, November. The family register of the Kuroda
household, 1958, records the death of Kuroda Genji at Inokashira, and records that the family
declines to state. This note is filed to place on record, for any future reader, that the
decline was made after the family had been informed, by the Kanzaki household, of the
circumstances of the death, and that the circumstances were: an assault upon a minor within
the Kanzaki residence; the intervention of the minor's mother; and the death of Kuroda Genji by
misadventure in the course of that intervention. The Kanzaki register records a resident removed
on the same date, and a child of eleven in the household, and the child's mother later
accepted responsibility for the act and was never charged. This researcher makes no finding of law. This
researcher records only that both registers declined, and that a register that declines is still
a document, and that a document is still a promise, and that the promise was to the public, and
that the public has not been told.*

That is the whole of it, in the language of the archive: the two families declined, and the
public was never told, and a child grew up inside the refusal and spent sixty years buying it
back so that someone would read it.

Aoi read the note twice and set it down, and understood the thing a winter of documents had
taught her: Hoshino's note is the *state's* version, and the state's version is the last silence
of all. It says a man died by misadventure and a mother took the blame. It does not say what the
child did, because the child was never asked, and because a note a researcher is permitted to
file can only hold what the registers were willing to admit. The whole truth of the blow was not
in any archive. It was on the inside cover of a ledger, in a child's hand, and Aoi would not find
it until she stood in the room.

Aoi folded the note into the folder and looked out the window at the lights of Mitaka and
understood that she had, at last, the one thing the account had been missing since April: a
document, in an official file, in a government archive, that a stranger could read, that did
not rely on a ghost or a curse or a room, and that said, in plain bureaucratic Japanese, that a
man had attacked a child and the child's mother had stopped him and the world had agreed not to
say so.

It was not justice. It was a note in a file.

But it was *readable*, and Kanzaki Mitsuko had spent her life collecting objects because objects
are readable, and Aoi understood, on the train, that the note was the last piece of the
collection, and that it belonged in the room.

[IMAGE 16.6 — an archive note on official paper, a single dense page in a careful hand, clipped
to the back of a certified register; a researcher's stamp in the corner; the register's
decline-to-state line visible above.]

# The Inventory

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The inventory was in a temple, and the priest had kept it for sixty-five years because no one
had ever come to collect it.

Ono was the third priest of a small Jōdo temple in Yanaka, and he had inherited the temple's
papers the way you inherit a garden: without asking, and with the expectation that you would
keep it alive. In the back of the storehouse, in a wooden box, were the records of the
funerals the temple had performed and the rooms it had been asked to bless and, once, in
November of 1958, a room where a man had died.

The blessing was a courtesy. When someone died suddenly in a house, the family sometimes
asked a priest to come and purify the room, and the temple kept a note: the date, the house,
the room, and—because the priest of that year had been a careful man—an inventory.

Not an inventory of value. An inventory of *presence*. The priest had listed what was in the
room when he arrived, because a careful priest lists what he blesses, and the note had
survived, and no one had ever read it, because no one had ever known it existed.

Ono read it in October, on a hunch, because a woman had come to the temple asking about a
house on the lane, and because he was the kind of priest who answered a question by going to
the record.

[IMAGE 16.1 — a temple storehouse: a wooden box open on a low bench, papers in bundles,
incense in the cold air; one sheet is unfolded, in a careful brush hand, headed with a date
in 1958 and the name of a house.]

---

The inventory listed thirty-one objects.

Ono read it twice, because thirty-one was not a number he had expected. He had heard, in the
neighbourhood, the story of the Kanzaki woman who had spent her life collecting, and he had
assumed the collection was the room. It was not. The room, when the priest blessed it in
1958, had held thirty-one things, and the rest — the two hundred and eleven of the estate —
were not the room.

They were the *replacements*.

Mitsuko had not been buying back the room. She had been buying back the room *and* its
absence: every object that was in it when the man died, and every object that had been taken
out of it after, and every object that had touched the room's edges in the years between. The
thirty-one were the original. The other one hundred and eighty were the years of the room
being emptied and refilled, and Mitsuko had pursued all of them, because a room is not a set
of things. It is a set of things *and* the space where the missing ones were.

The priest's list gave the original thirty-one. And beside each, in the margin, the temple's
careful priest had noted one more thing: whether the object was still in the room when he
left, or whether it had already been taken.

Twenty-nine were still there. Two were already gone.

Ono looked at the two, and one of them was the mirror.

[IMAGE 16.2 — a brush-written inventory sheet, numbered lines, each object named in a column;
two lines are circled in later pencil, one of them reading "kagami" (mirror), with a margin
note in a different hand indicating it was already removed.]

---

Ono called the estate, and the estate gave him Aoi's number, and Aoi came to the temple on a
wet afternoon in November and sat in the cold office and read the list.

"Two were gone before the priest arrived," Ono said. "The mirror, and a chest."

Aoi looked up. "A chest."

"A tansu. A large one. It was in the corner of the room, against the wall, and it was gone
when the priest came. He noted it because he remembered it from a previous visit, when the
house was whole."

Aoi sat with that for a long time, because it fitted, and because it was terrible.

The chest was not Mitsuko's. It was not in the ledger, or the album, or the sale. It had
appeared in the Kuramae warehouse, in the corner of the arrangement, with a blank tag. And
now the priest's inventory said that the chest had been in the room in 1958, and had been
removed, and that the child who hid behind it had hidden behind a chest that was being taken
away.

"The chest is where she hid," Aoi said. "And it was taken. And she's been looking for it her
whole life, and it turned up in the warehouse, and no one knows who put it there."

"The chest is the witness," Ono said. "The chest is the thing that stood between the child
and the room. It's the only object that was on both sides of the wall."

Aoi looked at the two circled lines — the mirror and the chest — and understood, finally, the
structure of the whole collection. Every object Mitsuko bought was a *replacement* for one of
the original thirty-one, bought after its original was lost, so that the room could be
reassembled in the only form available to her: a copy. The room she built was a replica,
built from memory, one object at a time, because the originals were gone.

Except the two that were removed while she watched: the mirror, which went to the servant,
and the chest, which went to no one, and which had come back.

She had been doing what the priest did. She had been keeping a record of presence in a room
she could not enter, and the only way she could hold the record was to buy the room's copies,
and the copies were, to her, the room.

[IMAGE 16.3 — Aoi's hand-drawn comparison: the priest's thirty-one objects on the left,
the estate's two hundred and eleven on the right, lines joining each original to the
replacement she bought for it; two originals have no replacement, and one replacement, the
chest, has no origin; the chest is drawn at the crossing.]

---

Ono gave her a copy of the inventory, and she took it home, and that night she laid it beside
the album and the ledger and the tags, and the four documents together told the whole truth
of the room, and she saw the last thing she had been missing.

The priest's inventory did not list a *person*. It listed thirty-one objects and a room where
a man died. But at the bottom of the sheet, in the margin, the careful priest had written one
line that was not an object and was not a room.

He had written: *one child present. would not speak. sat behind the chest until carried out.*

Aoi read that line perhaps thirty times.

*Sat behind the chest until carried out.* Not *hid*. Not *watched*. The child had sat behind
the chest and had not spoken, and had been carried out, and the priest had noted it because
a child carried out of a room where a man died is a thing a careful priest notes.

And Aoi understood, reading it, that the priest had seen the child, and the child had seen
him, and neither had spoken, and that this was the first silence — the original one, from
which all the other silences had been cut.

She wrote in her notebook:

*the room has thirty-one things and one child and one death, and the collection has two
hundred and eleven things and no child and no death, and that is the whole of it. she spent
her life replacing the room so she would not have to be the child in it. but you cannot
replace the child. you can only put another one in the gap.*

She underlined the last line.

And then, because she had by then understood what the chest in the warehouse was for, and who
the blank tag belonged to, she went to Kuramae.

[IMAGE 16.4 — a low table by lamplight: four documents laid side by side — a brush inventory,
a bound ledger, a photograph album, a box of tags — and a notebook open below them with a
handwritten line; a black-lacquer mirror stands against the wall behind, turned to face the
room.]


---

Aoi asked Ono whether she might see the room, and he took her there, because a priest can take
you anywhere in his parish and because he had come to trust her, and they stood in the lane
behind Yanaka Ginza in the cold and looked at the house.

The house had a new building on the lot. Ono had not known that. He had thought it was still
the old row-house, because the parish records said it was, and because a parish record is
sometimes the last thing in the world to admit a change.

Aoi asked whether the old house had been demolished.

Ono said the record did not say, and then he said the thing that mattered: that the family had
kept the *grave rights* and that there was a family grave in the temple, with three names on
it. Kanzaki Sada, Kanzaki Mitsuko, and a third name that Ono had always assumed was a child
who died young.

Aoi asked what the third name was.

Ono looked at the new building on the old lot and said: "It isn't a Kanzaki. It's a Kuroda.
The grave holds a Kuroda child, aged eleven, who was never buried in it. The stone is a
cenotaph. The family paid for a stone for a living child and left the death date blank."

Aoi stood in the lane and felt the whole account turn over one more time.

Mitsuko had put a stone in the family grave for a Kuroda child of eleven. Not for the man she
had watched die. For a *child* of her own age, in the enemy family, with the death date left
blank, which is what you do when you are waiting for a death, or when you do not believe in
one.

Aoi asked Ono who had paid for the stone.

He said the record showed it was paid in 1961. By cash. By a woman.

The same year as the first tag.

Mitsuko had been thirteen in 1961. She had bought a lacquer tray in Asakusa and written a name
on it, and she had also bought a stone for a child in the family that had killed her room, and
she had left the death date blank, and she had kept it blank for sixty years.

Aoi understood, standing in the lane, that the collection was not only a testimony against
Kuroda. It was an *obligation to him*. The two families had each buried half the night, and
Mitsuko had spent her life buying the room back so that the other family would not be left
holding only the silence. She had built a room that would hold *both* versions, and the stone
with the blank date was the room's other half, and the blank date was not waiting for anyone
else.

It was waiting for her.

[IMAGE 17.5 — a temple graveyard in Yanaka, winter: a family grave with three names on the

Ono gave Aoi one more thing before she left the temple, and it was not a document and it was
not an object. It was a sentence his predecessor had left in the parish notebook, in 1958,
in the week after the blessing, and it is the earliest thing in the whole account written by
someone who was not a Kanzaki and not a Kuroda.

The priest had written: *Blessed the room. The mother was calm and the child would not speak.
The mother asked me to bless the chest, which was already gone, and I blessed the place where
it had been. I have never been asked to bless a place before. I did it.*

Aoi sat with that. The mother had asked the priest to bless the *place where the chest had
been*, in 1958, in the week the chest went out with the servant, and the priest had done it,
and had noted it because it was strange, and the note had sat in a parish book for sixty-five
years.

Sada had blessed the absence. That was the first act of the whole collection — not a purchase,
not a tag. A mother, a week after her daughter watched a man die and was carried out, asked a
priest to bless the empty place where the chest had stood, because the chest was gone and the
child would not speak and there was nothing else to do.

And Mitsuko, sixty years later, had spent her life *reversing* that blessing: filling the
absence, object by object, until the place where the chest had been was a chest again.

Aoi asked Ono whether the parish still had the power to bless an absence.

Ono said it did, and said it was called something else now, and that people mostly asked for it
at funerals, for the space a person leaves, and that he had done it four times that year, and
that it was the same rite Sada had asked for, and that it had never once, in sixty-five years,
been asked for a chest.

*Live POV. Present tense.*

wanted to know who I was. He had asked because he wanted me to tell him who she was, and I
had, and I had done it in one sentence, on my own step, at nine at night, to a stranger with
his hands folded.

Knock.

Knock.

# Frame Four — The Measure

---

I have to write this part quickly, because I have come to understand that the account is
not a record I am keeping. It is a thing I am being allowed to finish.

Mikami called me on the second of November and said we had to go to Kuramae. He said it in
the voice he uses for materials, which is the voice that does not argue, and I asked him why,
and he said: "Because the count is nearly complete, and I want to measure the room before
the last piece goes in."

I asked him what the last piece was.

He said, "You know what it is," and hung up, and I stood in my flat in Tateishi with the
phone in my hand and the mirror in the corner, and I understood that he was right and that I
had known since the album.

---

I want to record the hours before we went, because I have not been alone in them, and that
is the part that no one will believe.

When I went to bed on the first, I woke at four, and the mirror was turned to face the room.
I had turned it to the wall — I am certain; I turn every mirror to the wall now, the way
Mikami turns his tags — and at four it was facing out, and in it, faintly, the six-mat room,
and a child standing in the doorway with her hand on the frame.

I did not get up. I lay in the dark and watched her in the glass, and she did not move, and
neither did I, and after a while I understood that she was not looking at me.

She was counting.

Her lips moved, and I could not hear the number, but I could see her count, and when she
reached the end she stopped, and then she started again, and it was the same count, and it
was not a long one. Twelve. She counted twelve, and then she counted again.

Twelve people in the room. And then — because the count did not come out right, because one
of the twelve had been added and one had not been taken away — she counted a thirteenth.

And the thirteenth, I am now certain, was the room itself.

[IMAGE 15.1 — a bedroom at night: a black-lacquer dressing mirror standing in the corner,

Mikami was waiting for me at Kuramae at nine, with a tape measure, and a torch, and the

The warehouse was cold. Kei had stopped locking it, or someone had. The two hundred lots
were not on the shelving any more.

They were on the floor.

Someone had laid them out — the mirror, the coat on its hook, the music box, the tea set, the
comb, the doll, the album, the blades on the daishō stand, the ledger itself, the tags, all
of it — in the shape of a room. Mats drawn in the dust. A doorway marked by two upright
posts. A gap in the centre the size of a standing person.

And in the corner, where the tansu should be, there was a large old chest that Mitsuko had
never bought, and that the estate had never sold, and that no one could account for, and that
had a tag on it in the small upright hand, and the tag was blank.

"Someone's been busy," Mikami said.

"Someone's been arranging," I said. "There's a difference."

Mikami measured it, and I wrote the numbers, and the numbers are the whole of the account,

The arrangement was seven mats by twelve. The storage room in the Yanaka plan was eight by

"What does a half mat mean," I said.

"It means the measurement is not a room," Mikami said. "It's a view. From here—" and he

He measured again, with the torch low, throwing shadows like a floor seen from the ground,

"Forty to fifty centimetres," he said. "That's the camera height in the album. Same as here.
She didn't build the room she remembered. She built the room she saw, from behind the chest,

I asked him the question I had come to ask, which was not about the room.

"Who's the chest?"

Mikami did not answer for a while. Then he said, "It's not hers. It's not in the ledger. It's

in the corner. And the chest is where you stand."

Mikami came to my flat in Tateishi. He did not want to, and he came, and we sat in the little

unlearn it, and that it was a coward's answer and I knew it.

going to be the one measuring the room, and I need to have said it before."

I asked him what.

He said: "She was eleven. Whatever she did, she was eleven, and then she was a woman carrying
it alone for sixty years, and then she was a woman who built a room so that someone would
finally read it. I've spent thirty years mending the room's furniture. I've never once been
able to say she was wrong."

I did not answer. I was thinking about my mother's notebook, and the line at the end, *this
one comes to the child*, and about the fact that a woman in Yanaka had been sending a list to
a woman in Tateishi for years before either of us died, so that the child would arrive at the
room knowing what was in it.

She had not summoned me. She had *corresponded* with me, across two deaths, for twenty years,
in the only language a woman like her had left, which was the language of objects and lists
and things that can be held.

Here is the last thing from that night, and it is the only thing in the account I cannot
explain and will not pretend to.

At about four in the morning, Mikami woke me. He had been sleeping on the floor by the window
and I had been on the futon, and he was standing over me, and he said, quietly, "Look at the
floor."

I looked. The room was drawn in the dust. Six mats. A doorway. A gap. A chest in the corner
with a blank tag. And Mikami said the thing that made me get up and dress and start packing
the folder.

He said: "I watched it appear. I was awake. It came from the mirror."

I said that was impossible.

He said: "I know. But I was awake, and I watched. The mirror was against the wall, and then
the light from the alley moved across it, and the light kept going, past the mirror, out onto
the floor, and where the light went, the floor was drawn. It wasn't drawn *by* the light. It
was drawn *with* it. Line by line. Mat by mat."

He said he had not been afraid. He said he had felt, instead, the thing he feels in his shop
when a repair finally sits true: a kind of *settling*. As if the room had been measuring
itself against my flat for months and had finally got the dimensions right.

I did not argue with him, and I did not believe him, and the two are not the same as agreeing.
I am an appraiser, and an appraiser does not accept a cause she cannot see. So I got up and I
looked at the door, and the door was locked, and the chain was on — and the chain was on
because I had put it on, and it was still on, which should have closed the question and did
not. Then I looked at the floor the way I look at a lacquer: for the hand.

The line was too even. That was the first thing. Dust, drawn through, leaves a soft edge; this
line had a hard one, a clean shoulder, the way a line looks when it is made with a straight
edge and not a finger. And in the corner, where the doorway of the drawn room opened onto the
mat, there was a smear the width of a shoe, and on the smear, faint, the smell that is in
Mikami's shop and on a dead man's coat and on the damp cloth a polite man folds in his pocket —
old tatami, shellac, cold tea. Not a ghost of it. The smell itself, on the floor of my flat,
where someone had knelt to draw.

I found the key in the door's outside lock the next morning: my own key, the spare, which I
had kept in the drawer by the door, and which is now one key short of what it was. Someone had
let himself in, drawn the room, and left the key, which is what the second hand did, and which
is how he had left a ring of keys on a hook in a house in Yanaka, for a locksmith, so that a
door would be found.

I have decided to believe this. I am an appraiser; I believe what the object says. But I have
also read every account in the folder, and one of them is a woman on a step who felt her hands
go slow while a polite man stood outside, and I know that the two readings — a man with a key
and a room that draws itself — are the same reading, and that I do not get to choose the one
that lets me sleep. That is what the room has always done. It does not remove the cause. It
puts a cause in your hand and dares you to price it.

We did not go back to sleep. We packed the ledger and the inventory and the album and the tags,
and we sat up until dawn, and at dawn I wrote the note that the police would find on my table
in December, and we went to Kuramae.

# The Tag Box

*Close third, past tense.*

---

The tag box was in Setagaya, on a folding table at Bōro-ichi, in December, among the kitchen
knives and the dead clocks, and it was Aoi who found it, because she had by then trained her
eye to see one thing in a market of ten thousand: a small upright hand.

Nineteen loose tags in a lacquered box, unused, blank on the front. On the back of each, in
pencil, a name and a date, and the dates were not 1958.

They were this year.

[IMAGE 20.1 — a flea-market table at Bōro-ichi, December: a lacquered box open among knives,

tag is lifted slightly, showing pencil on its reverse.]

---

Aoi had come to Bōro-ichi for the same reason she did everything that winter: because it was
the last place she had not looked. The market is held on the fifteenth and sixteenth of
December and the fifteenth and sixteenth of January, two hundred thousand people a day,
stalls the length of several streets, and a tag box in a lacquered case among the knives was
the kind of thing that finds you when you have stopped being able to find anything.

She bought it for three hundred yen and did not open it until she was home, with the door
locked, which she had started doing in October without saying why.

She laid the tags out on the low table. Nineteen. On the reverse, the names.

KANZAKI KEI — this year.
SHIRAI AOI — this year.
MIKAMI TORU — this year.
HOSHINO — this year.
YAMAMURA — this year.
SAKAGUCHI RIE — this year.

And thirteen others, all living, all tagged, all with dates still to come.

She knew then what the tags were.

They were not a list of the dead. They were a list of the living, and every tag that had a
*past* date on the front had once had a *future* date on the back, and the two hundred lots
of the estate were, all of them, the completed versions of these.

The collection was not a memory. It was a schedule.

Aoi sat with the nineteen tags and did what she did best, which was to sort. She sorted by
date. And when she had sorted, the dates were not random, and they were not consecutive, and
they were not in the order of the names.

They were in the order of a *route*.

Each living name had a date, and each date fell exactly one month after the previous, and the
places, where she could recover them from memory and note, ran in a ring: Tateishi, Yanaka,
Sendagi, Asakusa, Nezu, Kagurazaka, Mitaka, Tateishi. A route around the city, one person a
month, beginning in June and ending in December.

June: Yano. July: Sakai. August: Ito. September: Suzuki. October: Hoshino (rescheduled —
Hoshino had not died, only been filed). November: Sakaguchi Rie.

December: Shirai Aoi.

She was the last date on the ring. She had been the last date since before she was born.

---

There was one tag with no name at all. It was in the bottom of the box, older than the
others, the pencil so faint it had to be held to the lamp, and it carried only a date and a
place and a symbol Aoi had seen exactly once before, in the interior lid of a music box: a
small drawing of a tansu.

Place: YANAKA. Date: 1958. No name.

That was the first tag. That was the tag Kanzaki Mitsuko had made for herself, at eleven, for
the person she had been in that room, and had never been able to write a name on, because she
did not know whose name it should be.

Aoi turned it over, and on the back, in the steadier hand — the hand of the *came-after* —
there were two words, written very small.

*she's you.*

Aoi sat with that for a long time. She did not understand it. She would understand it in the
room, three days later, when she measured the gap and stood in it, and the count came out
even.

She put the tags back in the box.

[IMAGE 20.3 — the blank tag held to a lamp: a small white tag with a faint pencil drawing of
a tansu and the words YANAKA, 1958; on its reverse, two small words in a steadier hand; the
lamp is the only light in the frame.]

---

She called Mikami. Her voice, she noticed, did not shake, and that frightened her more than
the tags.

"Bring a tape measure," she said. "We're going to Kuramae."

He asked when.

"Tomorrow," she said. "And Mikami — bring the ledger, and the inventory, and the album. Bring
all of it. I want to know what the room is before I stand in it."

He said he would. Then he said the thing that she had been waiting, all winter, for someone
to say out loud.

He said, "Aoi. When you stand in the gap, the count comes out even. You know that."

"Yes," she said.

"And you're going to stand in it anyway."

"Yes," she said.

He was quiet. Then he said, "Then I'll be there. I'll measure it. If it's going to be
measured, it should be measured right."

That is the last thing anyone said to Aoi Shirai before the room, and it was said by a man
who had spent thirty-eight years mending other people's broken things, and who had understood,
finally, that he could not mend this one, and who came anyway.


---

I want to set down the argument, because it is the argument that decided the ending and
because it happened in a car.

On the way to Kuramae, with the folder on the seat between us, Mikami said the thing he had
been holding since November, and he said it in the voice he uses for materials, which does not
argue but states.

He said: "You're going to stand in the gap."

I said yes.

He said: "That's not a valuation. That's a suicide."

I said it was neither, and I said it badly, because I had not thought it through and because I
had been avoiding thinking it through for a month. I said something about finishing the
account, about the last reader, about the fact that a testimony is not complete until it is
read.

Mikami pulled the car over, on a street in Kuramae, in the cold, and turned to me and said
the truest thing anyone said in the whole winter.

He said: "A testimony is complete when it's *read*. Not when the reader dies. You're confusing
the document with the witness. That's what she did. That's the whole disease of that room. And
if you walk into the gap and let it count you, you're not finishing her account. You're
agreeing with it."

I sat in the car and did not answer, because he was right and because I was going to do it
anyway, and because the two facts can both be true: that he was right, and that the room had
already begun to count me the night I read my mother's name, and that agreeing with a thing
and being unable to leave it are not the same.

I said: "Then come in with me. And measure it. If I'm going to be read, I want it read
right."

He did not answer. He started the car.

That is the whole argument. I am putting it in because the account should not pretend I was
brave. I was not brave. I was a woman who had spent nine months in a room's paperwork and had
begun to believe that the paperwork *was* the room, which is the professional deformation of
my trade and the reason I am writing this in the past tense and will finish it in the
present.

on the seat between them; out the windscreen, a warehouse wall and a locked gate; the street
is empty and the engine is off.]

---

And one more thing, because it is the last evidence and it was in the car.

While we sat there, I opened the tag box one more time and looked at the nineteen tags, and I
noticed a thing I had not noticed in my flat, because a car's interior light is a different
light.

Six of the nineteen dates had been *corrected*. The pencil had been rubbed out and rewritten,
in the steadier hand, and the corrections moved each date *earlier*. The nurse's date,
Sakaguchi Rie, had originally been written in December and had been moved to November. My own
date had originally been written in November and had been moved to December.

The room's schedule had been edited. Someone had been adjusting it, in pencil, month by month,
as the room got closer, so that the dates would fall in the right order — so that the last
one would be last.

I did not tell Mikami. I sat in the car with the corrected dates and understood that I had not
been *scheduled* to die last. I had been *reordered* to die last, by someone who had been
editing a list for sixty years and was not going to let the last entry fall out of sequence.

The last entry on the list had always been a Shirai. It had just not always been me.

# The Room

*First person. Present tense. The Unlock.*

---

Mikami brought a tape measure, the ledger, the inventory, the album, and the nineteen tags
in the box, and we drove to Kuramae in the dark because I did not want to be seen going in.

The warehouse was exactly as I had left it in my flat, drawn in dust: the two hundred
objects laid out in the shape of a room, the mats sketched, the doorway marked by two posts,
the gap in the centre, and in the corner, against the wall, the chest that no one could
account for, with the blank tag on its handle.

Only now I knew what the chest was. The inventory had told me. The chest was the original.
The chest was in the room in 1958, and was taken out while the child watched, and had come
back, and the room had been built around it, not the other way round.

Mikami put the lamp on the floor and the room came up out of the dark.

[IMAGE 21.1 — the Kuramae warehouse interior, wide: two hundred tagged objects arranged on
the floor to form the outline of a six-mat room; a low doorway marked by two upright posts;
the arrangement is legible as a floor plan; one hanging bulb; two small figures at the edge.]

---

He measured it, and I wrote the numbers, and the numbers were the whole of the account.

The arrangement was seven by twelve. The storage room in the Yanaka plan was eight by
fourteen. The gap in the centre was not at the centre; it was set back toward the chest by
about half a mat.

"It's not a room," Mikami said. "It's a view. From here—" he crouched beside the chest,
"—seven by twelve is what eight by fourteen looks like to a person lying on the floor in this
corner."

He measured the height of the light in the album, the camera height, forty to fifty
centimetres, and it matched the height of the arrangement's horizon, and he sat back on his
heels and said the thing that I had paid him to say.

"She didn't build the room she remembered. She built the room she saw. From behind the chest,
on the night, and she never corrected it, because she never wrote it down, and this is the
only true record of where everyone was standing."

Then he measured the gap. And the gap, when he measured it, was exactly one person wide, and
it was set at the angle of the child's sightline, and it faced the doorway.

"It's a witness stand," he said. "She built a witness stand and left it empty for sixty
years."

[IMAGE 21.2 — a measured floor plan, hand-annotated: the planned eight-by-fourteen storage
room overlaid with the assembled seven-by-twelve arrangement; the discrepancy shaded; a note
reading "eye height = c. 45 cm"; a small tansu symbol in the corner; the central gap drawn
to face the doorway.]

---

Mikami laid the four documents side by side on the floor: the inventory, the ledger, the
album, the tags.

The inventory gave the original thirty-one objects and the two that were already gone. The
ledger gave sixty years of replacements. The album gave the view from the floor. The tags
gave the names.

And when the four were laid together, the room resolved the way a floor plan resolves when
you finally look at it from the right height, and I saw the whole of it, and I am going to
set it down now, in the order an appraiser sets down a provenance, because it is the last
valuation I will ever make and I want it to be complete.

**In 1958**, in the room in Yanaka, there were twelve people: Kuroda Genji; Kanzaki Sada;
the servant Hana; the driver Toyama; and eight others, guests and hangers-on, whose names
are the tags. And there was one child, Kanzaki Mitsuko, aged eleven, behind the chest.

Kuroda Genji had been drinking. He had come to the house that night in the Kuroda family's
formal pair of swords, which is what a patron wears when he goes to be important. He did
something to Mitsuko that the record does not name and does not need to name. Her mother,
Sada, came in. There was a struggle. Kuroda fell. He struck the fifth panel of the screen and
bled there and did not get up. And Mitsuko, behind the chest, did not move, and the music box
played, and the priest was called, and the child was carried out.

That much I had known since the album. What I did not know until the four documents were laid
together was *who struck the blow*.

**Because the ledger has a first entry.** Not 1961. The 1961 tray was the first *purchase*.
But at the front of the ledger, on the inside cover, in the small upright hand, there is a
line dated 1958, and it is not a purchase, and it says:

*I hit him first. Mother finished it. She said it was her, and I let her, because I was
eleven. Every object in this room I have bought to say it back.*

Mitsuko hit him first. Her mother took the blame, confessed to the whole act, absorbed it
into the household register, and told the world that a child had done nothing. And the child,
who had hit a man and watched him fall and watched her mother carry it, had spent sixty years
buying the room back one object at a time, tagging each with the name of a person who had been
there, building a testimony out of furniture, because an eleven-year-old's word was worth
nothing and an object you can hold is worth everything.

[IMAGE 21.3 — the inside cover of the ledger, photographed at raking light: a single
handwritten entry dated 1958, three lines, a confession; below it, sixty years of purchase
columns; the handwriting on the top line is a child's.]

---

I stood in the gap. I know now that I should not have. Mikami told me not to, twice, and I
did it anyway, because I am an appraiser and I had spent nine months pricing the room, and
the only way to finish a valuation is to stand in the thing and let it be.

The room came together the way a room does when the last piece goes in.

The gap was the last piece.

I stood where the child had stood, behind the chest, facing the doorway, and the objects on
the walls — the mirror, the screen, the box, the comb, the doll, the blades, all two hundred
of them — turned toward me the way they had been arranged to turn, and the count in the room
finished, and the count was thirteen, and I was the thirteenth.

And I understood, standing in it, the two words on the back of the blank tag.

*She's you.*

Not *she was you*. Not *she is like you*. *She's you.* The child behind the chest had spent
her whole life building a room that would hold one witness in the one place the room was
built to be seen from, and the witness had to be someone who could read the room, and the
only person who could read the room was the person who had spent her life learning to read
dead women's handwriting, and that person was me.

I was not the last victim of a curse. I was the last *word* of a sentence that had been
being written for sixty-five years. Mitsuko did not kill me. Mitsuko *finished* me, the way
you finish a valuation, and she could not have done it without me, and she had been waiting
for someone like me to walk into her estate for six decades.

I heard the door of the warehouse open.

[IMAGE 21.4 — from inside the arrangement: the narrator standing in the central gap facing
the doorway; the two hundred tagged objects turned toward her; a man's silhouette entering
the doorway, backlit by the corridor; the chest in the foreground corner with the blank tag.]

The came-after came in without hurrying, because he never hurried.

He was not what I had expected. I had expected a man in a grey coat with his hands folded,
and he had that, but he also had, that night, something I had not seen in any of the eleven
stories.

He had a *reason*.

He sat down on the floor at the edge of the arrangement, the way an old man sits down who has
been carrying something for too long, and he said, before I could say anything, the first true
thing anyone had said to me about the room.

"I'm not a killer," he said. "I want that on your record, because you're writing it. I keep
the records. There was another of us, for the ones who held a piece in their hands, and that
one is not mine to speak for. But the filings, the shop doors, the stairwells — that is my
work, and I am Hana's son, and I am the came-after, and I did what she asked."

And then the man who had bought the coat and the sit and stood at the top of a stairwell in
the wet — the second hand, the one Mitsuko had kept apart from the first so that neither
would ever know the whole of it — told me the thing that the whole account had been missing,
and I am going to write it down exactly, because it is the reason this book is not a murder
story.

He said: "Kanzaki Mitsuko wrote to me in 1989. She was sixty-two. She had been collecting for
thirty years, and she had realised that the collection was not going to be enough, because the
collection is only the room. The room is only the room. What she needed was the *route* — the
people who held the pieces, the people who held the paperwork, and the person who would
finally stand in the gap. She could not walk it herself; an old woman in a house in Yanaka
cannot travel the city. She wrote to two families whose grandfathers had carried her night and
been paid to forget it. One was the driver's line. The other was mine."

He said: "I am not a Kuroda. It matters that you know that. The Kurodas were buying the pieces
back all this time, quietly, to keep the story theirs — Kuroda Jun, the grandson, was the one
who bought the coat and the blades and asked after every tagged lot. She had not accounted for
him. But I am Hana's line, and Hana carried the mirror out and was paid, and that is the
silence I inherited, and it is mine to pay down."

He said: "She divided the work like a ledger. The living who still held a piece: that was the
driver's grandchild, the bank clerk, the one who wrote the tea. The ones who kept the
*records*: the menders, the genealogist, the dealers — that was me, because a person who files
things cannot be talked to, only filed. She said she would build the room and leave the tags,
and all I had to do was clear the route, one a month, and leave the last one alone until the
room was finished. And I said yes."

I asked him why.

He said: "Because she was right about what my grandmother did. Hana took the mirror out of
that house and took the money and never spoke, and her daughter never spoke, and you grew up
in Tateishi not knowing any of it. She paid me in the only currency that matters to a family
that bought its own silence, which is the truth about our own name, told to us by the one
person who saw it. She gave me the room, and the room gave me back my grandmother as she
actually was, and I have spent thirty-five years making sure no one ever reads it
differently."

[IMAGE 21.5 — two figures at the edge of the arranged room: the narrator standing in the
central gap; the came-after seated on the floor, hands still folded; between them, on the floor,
the four documents laid side by side; the doorway open behind him.]

---

And there it was. The whole account, turned over one last time.

Kanzaki Mitsuko, the collector. The woman who bought a room back one object at a time. The
witness who could not speak. And — the thing no one had seen, not Aoi, not Mikami, not me —
the *author*. She had not just built the room. She had built the *route*. She had spent
thirty-five years, through two hired hands she kept from ever meeting, moving the pieces of
her testimony into the only arrangement that could deliver it: a chain of people who each held
one document, and one person, at the end, who held none, and who would have to walk into the
room and read it, and the room would be complete, and the truth would be told.

The deaths were not the crime. The deaths were the *delivery*. And the man in the grey coat
was not the villain in the way the account had wanted one. He was half a messenger, and he had
been carrying half a message for thirty-five years, and he was tired, and he had come to the
room, that night, to hand it over.

He said the last thing, and it was the politest thing anyone has ever said to me.

He said: "She left you a tag. It's in your hand."

I looked down. The blank tag, the old one, the one with the drawing of the chest, the one
with *she's you* on the back — it was in my hand. I had picked it up and never put it down,
the way you hold a thing you have already priced.

On the front, which had been blank for sixty-five years, there was a name now.

*SHIRAI AOI — 1958 — YANAKA.*

Not my year. Her year. She had put me in the room, in the year of the room, in the place the
room was. She had not killed me and she had not collected me. She had *entered* me, as a
witness, in the register of the room, in the only column that had ever mattered, the column
of the people who were there.

I am the last piece. I am the last piece because I am the last reader, and a testimony is not
complete until it has been read, and I have read it, and I am writing it down, and I am the
document.

---

Mikami tried to stop me. I want that in the record. He put his hand on my arm and he said my
name, and he said the tape measure, and he said we could leave, and I did the thing I had
trained my whole life to do, which was to hold still while a thing is handled, and let the
room finish the valuation.

The came-after did not touch me. He sat on the floor with his hands folded and he watched, and he
said nothing, and that was his mercy, and I understood it as mercy though I cannot prove it
was.

The room became the room. The mats contracted the way they had contracted for the locksmith,
and the air thickened the way it had thickened for the nurse on her step, and my hand on the
phone slowed, and the doorway of the warehouse — the real one, the one the came-after had come
through — became the doorway of the room in 1958, and through it I could see a child being
carried out, and a mother in the doorway, and a priest with his inventory, and the fifth panel
of a screen, blank.

And I wrote, in the last minutes, the sentence that I had come there to write, which was not
for the police and not for Kei and not for the room.

I wrote: *she was eleven and she was right and no one ever let her say it. I have said it.*

Then I set the phone down.

I am going to write the part that no one else can write, which is what it is like inside the
count, because I have read every account in the folder and every one of them stops at the same
place: the moment the room becomes the room. The locksmith stops. The nurse stops. Suzuki
stopped before he started. And I have understood, reading them, that they stopped because they
could not write the inside, and that the reason they could not write it is the reason I can:
I am not trying to get out.

The inside is not a place. That is the first thing. It is a *rate*. Everything inside the count
happens at the speed of being remembered, which is faster than thought and slower than light,
and so you experience your whole life at once and also one object at a time, which should be
impossible and is not.

The second thing is that the room shows you your own provenance. That is what it is for. Every
object on the wall is a holder in a chain, and when you stand in the gap, the chain resolves,
and you see the whole line of people who touched the thing that is at the centre of your life,
and the thing at the centre of my life was the mirror, and the chain of the mirror ran: a
lacquer workshop in Meiji; the Kanzaki house; Hana; my mother; my grandmother's flat; a
landlord's lock; twenty years of not being thought about; an estate sale in Kiyosumi-Shirakawa;
and me.

And at the end of the chain, past me, there was one more holder, and it was not a person.

It was the room. The room is the last holder of everything in it, because a collection that is
built to be read is a collection whose final owner is the act of reading. I was not a victim
and I was not a witness. I was the room's *last holder*, entered in the register the way a
provenance enters a final line: *acquired by the collection, in situ, 1958.*

The third thing is the thing I have to write carefully, because it is the only thing that
frightens me even now.

Inside the count, I could see the child. Not in the mirror; in the room. She was sitting behind
the chest, at the height the album had photographed, and she was counting, and she was not
counting the objects. She was counting the people. Twelve, and then a pause, and then she
looked at the gap and she did not count.

She *waited*.

She waited for sixty-five years, inside the room she built, for someone to stand in the gap so
that the count would come out even, and she did not do it for revenge and she did not do it to
kill. She did it because she had been carried out of the room at eleven and had never been
allowed to say what she saw, and a child who is not allowed to speak will build, if she has
sixty years, a room that speaks for her.

The room does not want a body. The room wants a *reading*.

And that is why, in the end, I did two things and not one. I stood in the gap — because I had
promised the account, and because I am an appraiser and I finish a valuation — and I also did
the thing Mikami told me to do, which was to *measure it and write it down and hand it to
someone else*, so that the reading would not end with me.

She counted thirteen. And I let the count finish.

But I wrote it down first.
